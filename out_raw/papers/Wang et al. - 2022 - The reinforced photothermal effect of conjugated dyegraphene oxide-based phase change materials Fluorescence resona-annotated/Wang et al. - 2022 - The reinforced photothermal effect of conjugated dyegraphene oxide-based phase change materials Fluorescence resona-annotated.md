#### ABSTRACT

The development of shape-stabilized phase change materials (ss-PCMs) with efficient solar energy conversion performance, large energy storage capacity, and high thermal conductivity is essential for solar energy utilization. Here, novel photo-driven composite ss-PCMs were successfully fabricated by grafting blue anthraquinone dyes (Bdye) on carboxylated graphene oxide (GO) and impregnating poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) into Bdyegrafted GO nanoconjugate system (GO-co-Bdye). The connection between grafting Bdye and GO was close enough for fluorescence resonance energy transfer from Bdye to GO. The GO-co-Bdye with a three-dimensional (3D) interconnected network structure prevented the leakage of PEG above its melting point, improved the crystalline nature, thermal stability, and thermal conductivity of composite PCMs. The composite displayed an excellent photothermal conversion capacity of 86.6% and improved thermal conductivity of 98.0% compared with the pristine PEG. The thermogravimetric analysis and differential scanning calorimetry showed that the composite PCMs exhibite high thermal stability and thermal storage performance.

# **1. Introduction**

Harnessing and utilizing solar energy is one of the most promising ways to reduce the mismatch between energy supply and demand [\[1\]](#page-9-0). Phase change materials (PCMs), as advanced thermal energy storage (TES) materials, are widely applied to storage energy due to their large latent heat and isothermal phase transition temperature during the phase change process. Consequently, solar energy acquisition and storage associated with PCMs have become a research hotspot [\[2](#page-9-0)–5]. However, visible light, which accounts for approximately 44% of solar radiation, cannot be directly or effectively applied in PCMs [\[6](#page-9-0)–9]. Thus, utilizing composite PCMs to harvest and convert visible sunlight into thermal energy has attracted significant attention in modern energy technology [\[1,3,4,10\].](#page-9-0)

Currently, the materials that can absorb and convert light energy into thermal energy include mainly inorganic materials [\[11\]](#page-9-0) and organic pigments and dyes [\[12\].](#page-9-0) Inorganic materials are mostly applied in solar selective absorption coatings, such as carbon black [\[13](#page-9-0)–15], CrxOy [16–[18\]](#page-9-0), and CuS [\[19,20\],](#page-9-0) for their electron-jumping energy levels matching the photon energy in the near-infrared spectral region as well as their surface plasmon resonance effect [\[10\].](#page-9-0) They can also be doped into PCMs as photothermal conversion materials, but they are prone to uneven dispersion and phase separation. For example, incorporating CuS nanoparticles into the paraffin/SiO2 PCM can significantly improve the photothermal conversion performance of the composite PCMs [\[21\]](#page-9-0). On the other hand, organic photothermal conversion materials such as azo dyes, anthraquinone dyes, polyaniline, and indocyanine green (ICG) are easily connected to PCMs or support materials through chemical bonding and promote heat transfer performance, and avoid phase separation [\[22\]](#page-9-0). Besides, organic shape-stabilized PCMs with photothermal conversion and visible light-harvesting abilities can be achieved via condensation polymerization of the corresponding dye and PCM

Herein, an intelligent photo-responsive blue anthraquinone-grafted GO nanoconjugate (GO-Bdye) system was developed, which can improve the photothermal conversion efficiency and reduce the fluorescence effect via exhibiting fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) effect between blue anthraquinone dyes (Bdye) and GO (Fig. 1). Bdye selectively absorbed 510 ~ 770 nm light radiation and converted light energy into heat energy mainly through non-radiative leapfrogging, which was stored by the PCM. Simultaneously, since the fluorescence emission spectrum of Bdye overlapped with the absorption spectrum of GO, the distance between the Bdye groups and GO sheets was extremely close due to the chemical bonding. The small amount of fluorescence generated by Bdye's radiative leap can transfer to GO, which acted as a fluorescent receptor and converted the received fluorescence energy to additional thermal energy, i.e., the FRET effect occurred. On the other hand, GO showed a large surface area, excellent mechanical properties, and regular structures [30–[33\].](#page-9-0) Thereby, it improved the crystalline nature, thermal stability, and thermal conductivity of composite PCMs. The obtained shape-stabilized photothermal PCMs also displayed high PCM loading of 80.4 wt% and photothermal conversion efficiency of 86.6%, signifying that the composite materials are a promising candidate for photothermal management systems like solar water heaters and building insulation materials.

#### **2. Experimental**

# *2.1. Materials*

Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) (molecular weight = 2000) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd and dried at 40 ◦C under high vacuum (0.1 kPa) for 48 h before use. Raw materials for the synthesis of blue anthraquinone dyes (C26H20N4O2, Bdye), Quinizarin (C14H8O4) was produced by Tokyo Chemical Industry CO., Ltd. The catalysts for amide reaction, 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethyl carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and 1-hydroxy pyrrolidine-2,5-dione (NHS) were purchased from Alfa Aesar (China) chemicals Co., Ltd. Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) and N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) were obtained from Beijing Chemical Works. Other reagents were purchased from Guoyao, Inc., China, and used without further purification.

#### *2.2. Synthesis of GO-COOH and GO-co-Bdye*

GO was prepared by Hummer's method following previously reported literature [\[34\].](#page-9-0) The synthesis process of GO-COOH was carried out as follows: 1 g chloroacetic acid and 1.2 g sodium hydroxide were added into 100 ml GO suspension (2 mg⋅ml<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ) with the aid of ultrasonic bath treatment for 1 h, and then followed by magnetic stirring for 24 h to react sufficiently and form carboxylated GO [\[35\].](#page-9-0) Finally, GO-COOH was obtained by filtration, repeated rinsing with deionized water, and drying at 60 ◦C in a vacuum oven for 6 h.

# *2.3. Preparation of composite ss-PCMs*

The composite PCMs, PEG/GO-co-Bdye, were prepared by a blending and impregnating method. GO-co-Bdye powder was dispersed in a flask with absolute ethanol with the aid of ultrasonic bath treatment for about 1 h. Then, the flask was transferred to an oil bath at 80 ◦C and stirred for 30 min. After that, 2 g of dried PEG2000 powder was added into the solution with magnetic stirring for 2 h until the PEG dissolved completely. Finally, the samples were dried at 80 ◦C in a vacuum oven for 8 h. PEG/GO/Bdye composite PCMs were synthesized in the same way.

# **3. Results and discussion**

### *3.1. Morphological and structural analysis of samples*

A schematic for synthesizing the novel PEG/GO-co-Bdye composite PCMs is represented in [Fig. 1](#page-1-0). GO was first treated with chloroacetic acid and sodium hydroxide to increase the amount of carboxyl group. Then the fluorescent dye (Bdye) was grafted onto the carboxylated GO surface via amide reaction. Strong covalent bond and affinity between the negatively charged carboxyl groups of GO sheets and the positively charged amino groups of Bdye molecules anchored Bdye tightly to GO sheets. Simultaneously, Bdye with two *iso*-active amino groups can connect GO nanosheets. As a result, GO-co-Bdye showed a 3D interconnected network structure, which facilitated the loading of the phase change material. GO-co-Bdye served as the photo-driven heating unit that can effectively capture photons, quenching fluorescence, and convert light to heat. Subsequently, the heat was transferred from GOco-Bdye to PEG, which underwent phase transition and achieved thermal energy storage.

As shown in [Fig. 2](#page-2-0)a, GO nanosheets presented a large lamellar structure with folds and ripples, owing to deformation during spalling and re-stacking [\[36\].](#page-9-0) The TEM image of GO nanosheets also confirmed the lamellar structure of GO nanosheets [\(Fig. 2](#page-2-0)b). Whereas the GO-co-Bdye nano-composite showed a 3D "flower-like" interconnected network structure ([Fig. 2](#page-2-0)d-f). Bdye connected with GO nanosheets via amide bonds distorted the two-dimensional (2D) planar structure of GO and further contributed to the formation of 3D structures. Compared with 2D GO, 3D GO-co-Bdye not only facilitated the sequestration of PCMs but also provided a continuous path for heat transfer. In addition, the microstructure of GO-co-Bdye revealed a uniform distribution of some small particles ([Fig. 2c](#page-2-0)), which were attributed to the absorbed or grafted Bdye molecules. The uniform and compact distribution of Bdye molecules on the GO surface created a conducive environment for photo capture. Moreover, the energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping ([Fig. 2](#page-2-0)g) of the N element showed the same morphology as those of the other elements, confirming the uniform dispersion of Bdye in this system. Also, PEG/GO-co-Bdye revealed the homogeneous absorption of PEG in the 3D network ([Fig. 2h](#page-2-0)).

The FT-IR spectra of GO, GO-COOH, Bdye, and GO-co-Bdye demonstrated an amide reaction between the –COOH group of GO and the –NH2 group of Bdye and the formation of amide bonds [\(Fig. 2](#page-2-0)i). The characteristic peak of the ether band showed a redshift from 1050 cm<sup>−</sup> 1 to 1058 cm<sup>−</sup> 1 with weak peak intensity, indicating that chloroacetic acid transformed ether bands into carboxyl groups [\[39,40\].](#page-9-0) Similarly, Bdye showed N–H stretching

# *3.3. Thermal properties*

The thermal stability and thermal degradation behaviors of PEG, GO, PEG/GO, GO/Bdye, PEG/GO/Bdye, GO-co-Bdye, and PEG/GO-co-Bdye were investigated by TGA measurements (Fig. 7). The weight percentage (*ω*) of PEG in the PEG/GO, PEG/GO/Bdye, PEG/GO-co-Bdye calculated from the TGA curves were 68.6 wt%, 69.1 wt%, and 80.4 wt%, respectively, which were shown in Table 1. From the TGA curves in Fig. 7a, the weight decomposition of pure PEG occurred from 330 to 430 ◦C with a total weight loss of 96.0%. The initial decomposition temperature of GO-co-Bdye and PEG/GO-co-Bdye occurred at approximately 170 ◦C due to the evaporation of absorbed water. As to GO-co-Bdye, only a slight weight loss at high temperature occurred, corresponding to the reduction of unstable oxygen functional groups. For the PEG/GO-co-Bdye, the weight loss was almost similar to that of the weight loss of PEG, confirming that approximately 80.4 wt% PEG was loaded in GO-co-Bdye [\[51\]](#page-10-0).

The X-ray diffraction patterns of PEG, GO-co-Bdye, and PEG/ GO-co-Bdye revealed the crystalline morphology of the ss-PCMs (Fig. 8). Sharp and intense diffraction peaks at 18.96◦ and 22.80◦ indicated that PEG and ss-PCMs exhibited similar crystal structures and good crystallization properties. The characteristic peak of the support material in the PEG/ GO-co-Bdye pattern migrated from 8.16◦ to 6.8◦ due to the expanded layer spacing of GO-co-Bdye by PEG, while the expanded space facilitated the mobility of PEG.

As a result, the undercooling degree of pure PEG became decreased. The PEG/GO-co-Bdye exhibited the lowest undercooling degree (ΔT = 21.8 ◦C) due to the 3D interconnected heat transfer path in the GO-co-Bdye. Besides, the theoretical latent heat (Δ*Htheo*) of the ss-PCMs was evaluated using Eq. (2): [\[52\].](#page-10-0)

Δ*Htheo* = Δ*HPEG* × *ω* (2)

where Δ*HPEG* represents the latent heat of pure PEG and *ω* refers to the weight percentage of PEG in the ss-PCMs calculated from the TGA results. The ΔHm of pure PEG was 170.4 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> 1 and the *ω* was 80.4 wt%. Therefore, the Δ*Htheo* of PEG/GO-co-Bdye was reached 139.9 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which was similar to the experimental data recorded, 130.6 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . Compared with pure PEG, the composite PCMs exhibited a partial loss in latent heat because some mass fraction of PEG was replaced by supporting materials. Nevertheless, the loss was reduced with the increasing proportion of dyes grafted on GO probably due to the hydrogen bonding and *π*⋯*π* interaction between the Bdye and PEG. The Tc of the pristine PEG and PEG/GO-co-Bdye composite PCMs were quite close, indicating that the infiltration of the PEG in the 3D network showed little effect on its thermal properties.

### *3.4. Shape-stability and thermal reliability analysis for composite PCMs*

Shape-stability is a crucial characteristic for the prevention of leakage, storage, and transport of ss-PCMs. The shape-stability of pristine PEG and PEG/GO-co-Bdye was characterized through a leakage test by setting samples in an oven at different applied temperatures. As showed in Fig. 10, all the samples were solid with defined shapes below 55 ◦C (the melting point of PEG2000). The PEG/GO-co-Bdye maintained its original shape without significant form-distortion and leakage of the liquid even heated above 80 ◦C, while the pristine PEG pellet quickly melted within 5 min. It revealed that GO-co-Bdye restricted the movement of PEG molecules and prevented leakage during the melting process. This is ascribed to the existence of hydrogen bonding and capillary force between GO-co-Bdye sheets and PEG molecules.

Thermal reliability is one of the critical parameters for the utilization of composite PCMs. Therefore, the thermal reliability of PEG/GO-co-Bdye was conducted after repeated melting-freezing cycles such as 1 cycle, 50 cycles, 100 cycles, and 200 cycles (Fig. 11 and Table 2). After 50, 100, and 200 thermal cycles, no obvious changes in either the exothermal or endothermal curves were observed. Although the crystalline peaks shifted slightly to the low-temperature region after thermal cycling, the crystallization temperature was still around 37 ◦C.

Meanwhile, the latent heat value showed a slight change after 200 thermal cycles. The Δ*Hm* of the PEG/GO-co-Bdye after 50, 100, and 200 thermal cycles decreased by only 2.9%, 3.9%, 4.0%, and the Δ*Hf*  changed by only 2.6%, 3.6%, 3.7%, respectively. #### *3.5. Thermal conductivity*

Thermal conductivity is an important parameter to evaluate the thermal transport from the source to its nearby environment, which has a great impact on the photothermal conversion and thermal storage [\[6\]](#page-9-0). GO exhibited high thermal conductivity due to large grain size and strong carbon–carbon covalent bonding. Although the thermal conductivity of GO-co-Bdye showed a slight decrease due to the surface groups disrupting the structural integrity, it is still much higher than that of PEG. As a result, the thermal conductivity of PEG/GO-co-Bdye composite PCM was 0.499 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> k<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which is 98.0% higher than that of pristine PEG (0.252 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> k<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ) ([Fig. 12\)](#page-7-0). The close contact between the GO-co-Bdye and PEG in the composite enabled the composite to inherit the high heat transportability of the GO-co-Bdye and provided better heat transfer pathways.

The temperature response behaviors of samples were further examined via an infrared camera, which is essential to evaluate the heat response capacities and thermal transfer rates of PEG and ss-PCMs. The temperature distribution images during the heating and cooling process are shown in Fig. 13. During the heating process, the color change of the two samples implied that the temperature response rate of PEG/GO-co-Bdye was much faster than that of pure PEG because of the supporting material that markedly improved the thermal conductivity of the ss-PCMs. As expected, the surface temperature of the ss-PCMs

continuously declined at a higher rate during the cooling procedure. The results also illustrated that the novel PEG/GO-co-Bdye composite contributes a remarkable role in thermal energy management systems.

# **4. Conclusions**

In this work, PEG/GO-co-Bdye capable of photo-driven energy conversion and storage was designed and synthesized via grafting blue anthraquinone dyes (Bdye) on carboxylated GO and impregnating PEG into GO-co-Bdye 3D network supporting materials. The composites effectively stored thermal energy through phase transition triggered by solar-to-thermal energy conversion under solar illumination with a high energy storage efficiency of 88.6%. GO sheets modified with anthraquinone dyes (GO-co-Bdye) exhibited an enhanced photothermal effect compared with pristine Bdye and materials designed with physically mixed ones. Therefore, the high fluorescence resonance energy was transferred from Bdye to GO sheets in the GO-co-Bdye scaffold. Moreover, the PEG supported with the 3D interconnected network structure of GO-co-Bdye showed improved thermal conductivity of 0.499 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> k<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which was 98.0% higher than that of pristine PEG. In addition, the obtained PCMs exhibited high energy storage densities, good durability, and leakage-resistance capability, indicating their profound potential in large-scale energy harvesting, conversion, and storage applications.
