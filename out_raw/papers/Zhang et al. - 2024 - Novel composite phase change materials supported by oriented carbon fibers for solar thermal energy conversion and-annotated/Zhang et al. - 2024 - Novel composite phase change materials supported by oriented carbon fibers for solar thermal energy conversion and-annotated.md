#### ABSTRACT

Phase change materials (PCMs) have aroused significant interest as promising materials for solar thermal energy conversion and storage. However, the long-standing shortcomings of liquid leakage, low thermal conductivity, and weak solar absorptance limit their practical applications. Herein, novel composite phase change materials (CPCMs) with anisotropic heat conduction are manufactured by mixing continuous carbon fibers (CFs) and palmitic acid (PA)/olefin block copolymer (OBC) mixtures using pressure induction and vacuum treatment. Because the oriented CFs in the vertical direction can offer heat transfer channels inside the composites, the vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities of the CPCMs are 5.84 W⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅m<sup>−</sup> 1 and 1.34 W⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively, resulting in a relatively high anisotropic degree of 4.36. Furthermore, to improve the absorption of solar radiation for the composite, carbon black is applied to the upper surface of the CPCMs, achieving a high total solar absorptance of 0.966. Due to the combination of the high solar absorptance of the carbon black and the high vertical thermal conductivity within the composites, the CPCMs exhibit outstanding solar-to-thermal efficiencies of 87.54% ~ 95.08% at 1– 3 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> . In addition, stability testing also confirms that the CPCMs have excellent leakage-proof properties, thermal stability, and cyclic stability for long-term utilization. This work can provide an efficient route to synthesize high-performance CPCMs for solar thermal applications.

# **1. Introduction**

Solar thermal energy conversion and storage technology is essential for the effective utilization of abundant solar energy for industrial heating, hot water supply, and other heating-related applications [\[1](#page-9-0)–3]. However, the intermittent and erratic nature of solar irradiation seriously limits the extensive harnessing of solar energy . Phase change materials (PCMs) have developed into crucial ingredients for solar thermal energy harvesting due to their isothermal phase change properties and high heat storage capacity, thus overcoming the discontinuous properties of solar irradiation [\[4\]](#page-9-0). However, most PCMs are constrained by their poor shape stability, low thermal conductivity, and weak solar absorptance, resulting in slow thermal response and low solar-to-thermal efficiency [[5](#page-9-0)]. Therefore, developing functional composite phase change materials (CPCMs), which are made by chemical modification or the addition of functional fillers, is critical to alleviate these drawbacks and enhance the large-scale applications of PCMs [[6\]](#page-9-0).

The polymer serves as an adsorption and supporting material to enhance the leakage-proof properties of the PCMs. Some polymers, containing olefin block copolymer (OBC) [[8](#page-9-0)], polyethylene [\[9\]](#page-9-0), thermoplastic elastomer [\[10](#page-9-0)], etc., have excellent compatibility with commonly used PCMs. However, because the solar absorptances and thermal conductivities of these polymers are equivalent to those of the PCMs, most CPCMs still suffer from weak solar absorptance and poor thermal conductivity, which can be detrimental to efficient heat absorption and rapid heat spreading inside the composites [\[11](#page-9-0)].

To tackle these issues, extensive efforts have been made to add various highly thermal conductive carbon-based nano-additives, including graphene [[12\]](#page-9-0), carbon nanotubes [[13\]](#page-9-0), graphite [[14\]](#page-9-0), etc., into PCMs, which can achieve considerably increased thermal conductivity and micro-convection at the nanoscale [[15\]](#page-9-0). Mehrali et al. [\[17](#page-9-0)] prepared graphene-based CPCMs, and the results show that the thermal conductivity of the obtained CPCMs is 1.93 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and their total solar absorptance can reach 0.86. Zhang et al. [\[18](#page-9-0)] fabricated CPCMs supported by Ag-graphene nanoparticles, showing that the thermal conductivity and total solar absorptance of the CPCMs are 0.41 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and 0.92, respectively. Qian et al. [\[19](#page-9-0)] proposed CPCMs with single-walled carbon nanotubes, demonstrating that their thermal conductivity can reach 2.7 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and their solar-to-thermal efficiency can reach 78% at 1 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> 2 (i.e., 1 sun). Zhu et al. [\[20](#page-9-0)] fabricated CPCMs by embedding graphene nano-additive into n-octadecane, and the results indicate that their thermal conductivity and solar-to-thermal efficiency under 1 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> 2 can reach 0.80 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and 70.0%, respectively. However, these nanoparticles still show inadequate and unstable thermal conductivity enhancements, which can be attributed to two reasons. One is that nanoparticles are distributed randomly inside the composites, failing to form continuous thermal transport pathways, leading to high interfacial thermal resistances between the PCMs and nanoparticles [\[21\]](#page-9-0). The other is that dispersed nanoparticles can aggregate and settle during the phase change process, which can hinder phonon transport and limit the heat transfer within the composites adversely [[22\]](#page-9-0).

Compared to the nanoparticles added within the composite, threedimensional carbon materials, including graphene foams [\[23](#page-9-0)], carbon foams [\[24](#page-9-0)], carbon nanotube sponges [[25\]](#page-9-0), etc., are selected to construct cross-linked thermal networks, which can provide continuous heat transfer paths to accelerate the thermal response within the composites. For example, Aftab et al. [\[26](#page-9-0)] designed CPCMs by infiltrating polyethylene glycol into carbon nanotube sponges, showing that their thermal conductivity is 2.4 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and their solar-to-thermal efficiency can be up to around 80% under 1 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> . Zhang et al. [\[27](#page-10-0)] prepared CPCMs supported by 3D porous foam, and the results indicate that the thermal conductivity and solar-to-thermal efficiency of the CPCMs can reach 1.04 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and 86.68%, respectively. Yang et al. [[28\]](#page-10-0) designed graphene foam-based CPCMs, demonstrating that their thermal conductivity is 1.82 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which is 574% higher than that of the paraffin wax. Wei et al. [\[29](#page-10-0)] fabricated CPCMs by using carbon aerogels, and the results show that their thermal conductivity is 0.43 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and solar-to-thermal efficiency can be as high as 82% under 3 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> . Chen et al. [\[30](#page-10-0)] proposed CPCMs using the graphitized network, showing that the thermal conductivity of the CPCMs is up to 0.91 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 with increasing filler loadings, which is enhanced by 236% compared with that of the polyethylene glycol. Cui et al. [\[31](#page-10-0)] designed CPCMs with modified melamine foam, and the results show that their thermal conductivity can reach 0.59 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and their solar-to-thermal efficiency is as high as 79.36% under 1 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> . Shen et al. [[32\]](#page-10-0) proposed all lignin-based sponge-encapsulated CPCMs, showing that the thermal conductivity of the composite is 0.36 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and the spectral absorptance can be as high as around 0.9 when the wavelength is in the range of 0.2– 1.0 μm. Lv et al. [\[33](#page-10-0)] fabricated phase change aerogel using graphene and silver nanoparticles, demonstrating that its thermal conductivity is improved to 0.856 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and its total solar absorptance can exceed 0.90 when the wavelength is in the range of 0.25–4 μm. Zhang et al. [\[34](#page-10-0)] designed wood-based CPCMs, showing that the thermal conductivity can reach 0.23 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and the solar-to-thermal efficiency can be boosted to 83% at 2 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> .

Although the thermal conductivities of the CPCMs with threedimensional carbon materials are enhanced, these values still exhibit low and isotropic, which can result in more heat being transferred from the interior of the composite to the environment (i.e., horizontal), thus reducing the solar-to-thermal efficiency [\[35](#page-10-0)]. Therefore, to weaken the heat dissipation to the surroundings and strengthen the heat conduction from the surface to the interior (i.e., vertical), orientation control of the thermally conductive filler is essential to realize anisotropic thermal transport in different directions inside the composite.

Recently, carbon fibers (CFs) have been considered as promising fillers to design anisotropically conductive CPCMs [[36\]](#page-10-0). The directional alignment of the CFs can accelerate the thermal transport inside the composite, thereby increasing the thermal conductivity in a specific direction within the composite [\[11](#page-9-0)]. Meanwhile, the thermal conductivities in the other directions can be weakened, thus reducing heat dissipation to the surroundings. For example, Sheng et al. [\[37](#page-10-0)] designed CPCMs with carbon fiber frameworks, showing that their vertical thermal conductivity can be improved to 0.77 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 at 8.8 wt% CFs, which is 133% higher than their horizontal value. Ma et al. [\[38\]](#page-10-0) proposed polymer composites supported by short CFs, demonstrating that the vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities at 13.0 vol% CFs are 2.84 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and 0.19 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively. Wu et al. [\[39](#page-10-0)] fabricated epoxy composites using CF networks, indicating that their vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities at 17.48 vol% CFs are up to 6.20 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and 1.24 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively. Hou et al. [\[40](#page-10-0)] designed polymer composites by utilizing directional freezing to form oriented and ordered CFs connected by short CFs in PDMS, showing that the vertical thermal conductivity of the composite at 10.8 vol% CFs can be improved to 3.50 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which is 368% higher than that of the composite with random CFs.

Despite oriented short CFs within the composite can lead to anisotropic heat conduction, the vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities reported above are much lower than expected for CFs-based materials. This is ascribed to the fact that larger interfacial thermal resistance appears at the CFs-CFs interface and PCMs-CFs interface, which can diminish the thermal response within the composite, leading to a considerable decrease in thermal conductivity [[41\]](#page-10-0). Hence, low anisotropic thermal conductivity limited by large interfacial thermal resistance within the CPCM remains a critical challenge.

In this paper, we proposed a simple method by mixing long and continuous CFs and the palmitic acid (PA)/olefin block copolymer (OBC) mixtures using pressure induction and vacuum treatment to fabricate anisotropically conductive CPCMs, which can form uninterrupted heat transfer paths and decrease the interfacial thermal resistances at the CFs-CFs interface and PCMs-CFs interface, contributing to boosting the anisotropic thermal conductivity of the composites. Then, the morphology and structures of the obtained CPCM were investigated. Furthermore, the thermal properties of the obtained CPCM were analyzed. Then, the leakage-proof properties of the obtained CPCM were evaluated. Then, the anisotropic thermal conductivity and heat transfer properties of the CPCM were examined. Finally, the solar-tothermal energy conversion and storage performance was measured and assessed within a simulated sunlight testing system.

#### *2.1. Materials*

Carbon fibers (TC-HC-600) were obtained from Shanxi Tiance New Materials Technology Co., Ltd. China, and their axial thermal conductivity is around 600 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . Olefin block copolymer (OBC, INFUSE 9530) was obtained from Dow Chemical Company. The carbon black (99%) was provided by Yueyang Dongrun Chemical Co., Ltd. China. Palmitic acid (PA) was provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical CO., Ltd. China.

#### *2.2. Synthesis of CPCMs with oriented CFs*

The rolled carbon fibers (CFs) were sheared into several dispersed short CFs and then arranged in a certain direction on the metal plate to form oriented continuous CFs. Then, the PA and OBC were mixed in a specific mass fraction and then stirred at 170 ◦C for 1 h to make the molten PA/OBC blend. Subsequently, the molten blend was poured into the CFs in the metal plate and brushed evenly to assure that the CFs and PA/OBC blend could be fully mixed. Then, the obtained PA/OBC/CF mixture was cooled down and divided into many slices. Then, these slices were placed in a mold and compressed at 30 MPa to obtain shaped slices. Finally, the shaped slices with a mold were put in a vacuum oven and then heated at 70 ◦C for 1 h, and compressed again to obtain the CPCMs with oriented CFs (Fig. 1).

In the CPCMs, PA provided high latent heat of fusion and crystallization, OBC served as supporting materials to improve the leakage-proof performance, and CFs served as highly thermally conductive additives to form continuous pathways for boosting the thermal conductivity of the composites. # *3.3. Leakage-proof properties of CPCMs*

The leakage-proof property is a significant factor affecting the thermal properties and morphology of the CPCMs. Therefore, to assess the leakage-proof properties of the CPCMs, the pure PA and different CPCMs were placed on filter paper and exposed to a heating disk at 80 ◦C for 1 h, and their mass was recorded at 10-min intervals. It can be observed in [Fig. 4](#page-5-0)a that the PA melted completely at 30 min and penetrated into the filter paper owing to the absence of leakage protection. In terms of the CPCM3, the leaked PA also leaves a visible sign on the filter paper after 30 min, and its residual weight rate drops to 94% (obtained in **Note S2**) at the end of the experiment, which can demonstrate that the leakage of the composite still exists at OBC loading of 15 wt%. Furthermore, no significant sign is found in the CPCM4 and the residual weight rate can reach 99% after heating for 60 min, indicating that 20 wt% OBC proves to be highly effective in preventing the molten PA from leaking out of the CPCMs. Thus, 20 wt% OBC is chosen as the anti-leakage agent for the composites. For the CPCM5-CPCM8 with 20 wt% OBC, it can be found that melted PA in the composites is hardly leaked during the entire process and their residual weight rates are almost unchanged [\(Fig. 4](#page-5-0)b).

Besides, the mass loss rates of CPCM5-CPCM8 diminish with increasing CF loading ([Fig. 4](#page-5-0)c**, Table S2**), indicating that the addition of the CFs is helpful to weaken the flow characteristics of melted PA and improve the leakage-proof properties of the CPCMs. In general, the above findings confirm that the CPCMs with different CF loadings at 20 wt% OBC can keep stable and avoid leakage.

To accelerate the heat charging/discharging inside the CPCMs, carbon-based materials are incorporated into the PCMs to boost the thermal conductivity of CPCMs. Nevertheless, current typical CPCMs still exist two problems. One is that low vertical thermal conductivity can cause the temperature of the upper surface of CPCMs to be high, resulting in considerable heat dissipation to the environment. The other is that most CPCMs exhibit isotropic thermal conductivity, and their heat transfer capacities are not well matched with the heat transfer direction from top to bottom. Consequently, more heat is laterally transferred to the surroundings, leading to a decrease in solar-to-thermal efficiency. To address these issues, it is necessary to increase the vertical thermal conductivity for efficient directional thermal transport and decrease the horizontal thermal conductivity of the CPCMs to minimize heat dissipation to the outside.

To realize the above purposes, the CFs with anisotropic thermal conductivity are employed within the PCMs to construct anisotropic and continuous thermally conductive pathways. These pathways can strengthen the heat transfer capacity in the vertical direction, thus increasing the vertical thermal conductivity of the CPCMs effectively. Meanwhile, these pathways also extremely weaken the heat conduction in the horizontal direction, decreasing the horizontal thermal conductivity, and therefore leading to low heat loss. To better investigate the anisotropic heat transfer mechanisms of the CPCMs, the typical series and parallel models are used (Fig. 5). In the two models, heat transfer within the composite is similar to an electric circuit, where thermal resistances are linked either in series or parallel. These thermal resistances are influenced by the thermal conductivity and proportion of the CPCMs. As shown in Fig. 5, the CFs perpendicular to the direction of heat conduction can maximize the thermal resistance of the CPCMs in the series model, which can limit most of the heat transfer inside the CPCMs. Then, in the parallel model, the direction of heat conduction is parallel to the orientation of the CFs, which can minimize the thermal resistance within the CPCMs, thus accelerating the heating/cooling process. These models can be applied to estimate the thermal

To sum up, it can be observed that air bubbles have little effect on the vertical thermal conductivity of the CPCM, while the continuity of the CFs influences it significantly.

Then, the vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities of the CPCMs with different mass fractions of CFs were compared to analyze the heat transfer abilities in two orthotropic directions ([Fig. 6b](#page-6-0)). The vertical and horizontal thermal conductivities rise approximately linearly with increasing CF loading. This means the thermal conductivity enhancement can be readily controlled by adjusting the CF loading. Moreover, the incremental range of the vertical thermal conductivity is much higher than that of its horizontal thermal conductivity. For example, the vertical thermal conductivities of the CPCMs can reach 2.32– 5.84 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 at CF loadings of 5– 20 wt%, and their horizontal thermal conductivities are only enhanced from 0.66 to 1.34 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> at the corresponding CF loading. As a result, the anisotropic degree defined as *k*V/*k*H can be increased from 1.00 to 4.36 [\(Fig. 6](#page-6-0)c). Furthermore, it can be observed from [Fig. 6](#page-6-0)c that the anisotropic degree firstly arises rapidly and then rises slightly with increasing CF loading. This is mainly because the excessive CFs can lead to higher interfacial thermal resistance at the CF-CF interface and CF-PCM interface, which can reduce the incremental range of the vertical and horizontal thermal conductivity, limiting the enhancement of the anisotropic degree with increasing CF loading [[41\]](#page-10-0).

Then, to illustrate visually the differential heat conduction properties in both vertical and horizontal directions of the CPCMs, the CPCM4 and the CPCM8 with the same thickness of around 2 cm were put on a heating disk at 80 ◦C to heat their bottoms, and an infrared camera was used to record the surface temperature distributions of the CPCMs ([Fig. 6d](#page-6-0)). For the CPCM4, one side of the composite is chosen to be heated owing to its isotropic thermal conductivity. As shown in [Fig. 6d](#page-6-0), the color of infrared images at the bottom of the side in the CPCM4 becomes brighter gradually with increasing the heating time, indicating that the bottom surface temperature of the composite rises over time. However, the upper surface temperature is still low throughout the whole heating process. This phenomenon is attributed to the low heating rate caused by isotropic and low thermal conductivity of the CPCM4, which prevents heat conduction from its bottom to its deep inside.

Then, the temperature profiles of the CPCM8 in both vertical and horizontal directions were also studied. It can be found that the temperature distributions of the composite exhibit significant variations owing to the high anisotropic thermal conductivity of the composite. When the CFs are parallel to the heating disk, the upper surface temperature of the CPCM8 is only up to around 41 ◦C after heating for 12 min [\(Fig. 6d](#page-6-0)), which is similar to that of the CPCM4. However, when the CFs are vertical to the heating disk, the upper surface temperature of the CPCM can be elevated to 80 ◦C during the whole heating process. The reason for the significant difference in heat transfer abilities in the horizontal and vertical directions is due to the fact that the CFs within the CPCM exhibit excellent anisotropic thermal conductivity, where their vertical thermal conductivities are significantly higher than the values in the horizontal direction. When the alignment of the CFs is identical to the direction of the heat transfer, the thermal conductivity of the composite can be obviously enhanced, thus realizing highly efficient thermal transport and fast thermal response.

Furthermore, the vertical thermal conductivity of the CPCM8 was tested during 500 melting-crystallization cycles at 25– 80 ◦C ([Fig. 6](#page-6-0)e). It can be found that a slight increase in thermal conductivity after many melting-crystallization cycles, with the value increasing from 5.84 to 6.04 W⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which is attributed to increased CF loading within the CPCM due to leakage of partially molten PA after multiple cycles. Meanwhile, the result further demonstrates that the CPCMs can show excellent stability available for long-term efficient thermal energy harvesting.

*<sup>ϕ</sup>* <sup>=</sup> *<sup>λ</sup>*CPCM 100⋅*λ*PCM⋅*φ*filler × 100% (3)

where *λ*CPCM represents the vertical thermal conductivity of the CPCM; *φ*filler represents the proportion of the filler; *λ*PCM represents the vertical thermal conductivity of the PCM; *Φ* represents the improved degree of thermal conductivity per 1 wt% filler loading. For the CPCMs with expanded graphite, graphene, graphite, etc., they present relatively low impact factors (*<*110%). This marked thermal conductivity enhancement of the composites can be ascribed to three reasons. Firstly, the vertical thermal conductivity of the CFs is around 600 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , which can improve the thermal conductivity within the composite effectively. Then, oriental CFs can form efficient heat conduction paths within the CPCMs, resulting in accelerated directional thermal transport and rapid thermal response to temperature. Finally, the CPCMs are obtained by compression at 30 MPa during the fabrication process. Its manufacturing method can increase the contact between the CFs and PCMs, decreasing the interfacial thermal resistance, and thus boosting the heat conduction within the CPCMs.

# *3.5. Solar-to-thermal energy conversion and storage performance*

The solar irradiance absorption performance is a crucial factor for achieving CPCMs with high solar-to-thermal efficiency. To assess the absorption performance of the composite, spectral absorptance testing and analysis were performed on the pure PA, CPCM8, and CPCM8 with carbon black [\(Fig. 7](#page-8-0)a-b). Firstly, the spectral absorptance of the PA varies obviously in the wavelength of 0.3–2.5 μm, and its value can be decreased from 0.920 to 0.44. Meanwhile, the PA also exhibits a low total solar absorptance of 0.558 (obtained in **Note S6**). For the CPCM8, its spectral absorptance can up to 0.919–0.938 due to the addition of the CFs, resulting in a high total solar absorptance of 0.935 ([Fig. 7b](#page-8-0)). In addition, applying carbon black to the upper surface of the CPCM8 can result in high spectral absorptances of 0.944– 0.968 when the wavelength range is from 0.3 μm to 2.5 μm, and the total solar absorptance can be as high as 0.966, which is 0.408 and 0.031 higher than that of the PA and CPCM8, respectively [\(Fig. 7b](#page-8-0)). # **4. Conclusion**

In this work, we proposed a simple method for fabricating anisotropically conductive composite phase change materials (CPCMs) by mixing the palmitic acid (PA)/olefin block copolymer (OBC) mixtures and continuous carbon fibers (CFs) using pressure induction and vacuum treatment. In the obtained CPCMs, CFs contribute to long and continuous heat conduction paths within the composite, leading to high vertical thermal conductivities of 0.47– 5.84 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 and low horizontal thermal conductivities of 0.47– 1.34 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> 1 at CF loadings of 0– 20 wt%. Consequently, the anisotropic degree experiences a substantial increase from 1.00 to 4.36. Furthermore, due to the combination of the high solar absorptance of the carbon black and the high vertical thermal conductivity within the composite, the CPCMs exhibit excellent solar-to-thermal efficiencies of 87.54% ~ 95.08% at 1– 3 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> . In addition, the CPCMs also exhibit outstanding leakage-proof properties, thermal stability, and cyclic stability for long-term utilization. This work presents an easy and scalable approach for synthesizing highperformance CPCMs for solar thermal energy conversion and storage.
