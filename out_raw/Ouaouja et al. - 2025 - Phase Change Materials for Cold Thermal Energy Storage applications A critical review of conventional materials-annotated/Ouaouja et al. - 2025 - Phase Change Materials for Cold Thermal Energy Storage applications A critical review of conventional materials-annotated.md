#### ABSTRACT

The integration of Phase Change Materials (PCMs) as Cold Thermal Energy Storage (CTES) components represents an important advancement in refrigeration system efficiency. These materials have demonstrated significant capabilities in storing and releasing thermal energy, leading to improved system performance and reduced energy consumption. Conventional PCMs such as water/ice, hydrated salts, and paraffin are commonly used in CTES applications due to their favorable thermal properties and/or cost-effectiveness. This review paper explores the benefits and performance of integrating conventional PCMs in various refrigeration systems. It thoroughly discusses the effects of PCM integration on energy consumption, temperature stabilization, storage product quality, and greenhouse gas emissions.

While conventional PCMs are widely used in CTES applications, there is growing interest in exploring sustainable alternatives. Bio-based PCMs, derived from biomass and bio-waste materials, have shown promise as thermal storage candidates. Although these materials have been extensively studied for building applications, their potential in CTES applications remains largely unexplored. This paper also provides a detailed evaluation of bio-based materials based on their phase change temperature and latent heat, assessing their suitability for use in CTES applications.

Through this extensive review, the paper provides insights into the current state and future potential of PCM technology in CTES, highlighting both the proven benefits of conventional PCMs in enhancing refrigeration system efficiency and the potential sustainability of bio-based PCMs.

CTES systems with PCM represent an innovative approach for efficient and sustainable cooling applications. This technology entails a cyclical process involving two sequential stages: charging and discharging. During the charging stage, the refrigeration unit absorbs heat from the PCM, causing it to transition from liquid to solid phase (solidification), and thus stores cold thermal energy as latent heat. Once the PCM is completely solidified, the refrigeration unit is not working, allowing the stored cold energy to be re-used and cool the refrigerated case by absorbing heat. This causes the PCM to reverse the process and transition back to the liquid phase (melting/discharging process). Through continuous repetition of these cycles, a stable temperature can be maintained inside the refrigeration chamber, even when the refrigeration unit is not operating [\[35,36](#page-35-0)]. The cost of final PCM products is influenced by several essential factors. The primary elements affecting the overall expense include the type (organic, inorganic, bio-based, etc.) and processing of raw materials, as well as encapsulation methods (macro, micro, or nanoencapsulation). Additionally, the volume purchased and associated shipping fees play significant roles in the final pricing. Among the organic materials, petroleum-based paraffin is generally considered an economical option [51–[53\]](#page-35-0), while high-purity laboratory-grade paraffin is more expensive [\[54](#page-35-0)]. Salts are recognized as the most cost-effective raw material for PCMs [\[55](#page-35-0)–57]. The encapsulation process significantly influences the final product cost. The price varies based on both the encapsulation material (plastic or metal-based) and the chosen technique: macroencapsulation (*>*1 mm), microencapsulation (0–1000 μm), or nanoencapsulation (0–1000 nm) [\[64,65](#page-35-0)] (Fig. 4). Macroencapsulation, the simplest method, involves containing PCMs in plastic (HDPE) or metal containers (aluminum, copper) and represents approximately 20 % of total costs [[55\]](#page-35-0). Microencapsulation, which involves coating PCM particles with polymer shells at the microscale, is more expensive, accounting for 50 % of the final product cost [[37,55,66\]](#page-35-0). While microencapsulation can increase PCM viscosity, researchers are exploring nanoencapsulation as a potential solution to this limitation [\[67](#page-35-0)].

While the cost and encapsulation methods of PCMs are important considerations for their production and commercialization, these materials also face significant technical challenges. Addressing these challenges is essential to improving their performance and expanding their practical applications. Numerous reviews have extensively covered the practical challenges associated with PCMs and the various strategies employed to mitigate these issues, more details on these matters can be found in the following papers [[17,](#page-34-0)[39,71](#page-35-0)–73]. Key challenges include low thermal conductivity, the supercooling effect, phase segregation, and the corrosiveness to encapsulation materials.

The low thermal conductivity of PCMs presents a significant challenge. Researchers have primarily studied two strategies to address this drawback [\[17](#page-34-0)[,37,42](#page-35-0),[72\]](#page-35-0). The first approach involves introducing high thermal conductivity nanoparticles, which can be either carbon-based or metal-based [[68,](#page-35-0)74–[86\]](#page-35-0). The second strategy focuses on enhancing the heat transfer surface area of the PCM through various techniques. These methods include: implementing structured fins, integrating heat pipes, utilizing porous materials such as metal or carbon foams, and employing advanced encapsulation technologies like micro- and nanoencapsulation [[37,66](#page-35-0)[,87](#page-36-0)–95].

Supercooling and phase segregation further complicates PCM performance by causing delays in latent heat release due to the metastable nature of liquid PCMs that solidify below their freezing temperature [[17](#page-34-0)[,96](#page-36-0)]. #### **2. Application and effect of PCM in refrigeration systems**

Many researches have focused on integrating PCMs in different refrigeration systems such as domestic and commercial refrigerators, cold chains, walk-in freezers, and warehouses. They can be incorporated into different parts of the refrigeration system: in outer walls as thermal insulators, as CTES systems, and as heat exchangers. Unfortunately, biobased PCMs have not yet gained significant traction to be used as CTES. Despite this, other varieties of PCMs have demonstrated their effectiveness.

This section presents an extensive analysis of the efficacy demonstrated by various strategies, highlighting the substantial benefits of incorporating PCMs into refrigeration systems. The integration of PCMs shows promising results in multiple key performance indicators, including the enhancement of coefficient of performance (COP), reduction in energy consumption, and mitigation of temperature fluctuations. [Table](#page-31-0) 3 in [Appendix](#page-31-0) C provides a summary of the findings reported in the various studies examined in this section.

### *2.1. Effect on energy consumption and COP*

# *2.1.1. PCMs as thermal insulation (as thermal shield)*

PCMs have been used as Thermal Energy Storage systems (TES) to enhance the thermal insulation of refrigeration systems [[146,147\]](#page-37-0). In this approach, PCMs act like a thermal shield, absorbing surrounding heat and storing it as latent heat through the process of changing from solid to liquid phase. This transition helps to minimize and shift the peak rate of heat transfer and temperature during the day, thereby reducing the power consumption of refrigeration systems. The PCM's optimal melting temperature for this approach should be close to the surrounding temperature.

Fioretti et al. [\[146\]](#page-37-0) examined the impact of the paraffin layer (Commercial PCM; 35 ◦C) added to the external surfaces of a walk-in freezer as a thermal insulator as illustrated in Fig. 8(a). The objective was to minimize and shift the peak heat transfer. The experiment took place over two summer days under outdoor conditions. The results showed that the PCM acts as an energy storage system that absorbs and shifts the heat flow, effectively, enhancing the thermal insulation of the walk-in freezer. The addition of PCM resulted in a significant difference in peak temperatures between the PCM inner surface (denoted as RN5 in Fig. 8(b)) and the PCM outer surface (designated as RN4 in Fig. 8(b)). Specifically, the peak temperature at RN5 was found to be 12–14 ◦C lower than the peak temperature at RN4. Additionally, this peak was shifted by approximately 4–5 h due to the presence of PCM. They also compared the temperature value at RN5 with that of the external surface of the reference case (represented by R4 in Fig. 8(c)). The temperature in this case was 7–8 ◦C lower than the reference one. On the other hand, there was no significant difference between the internal air temperature of the reference case (at R5) and the PCM case (at RN6), as the refrigeration system effectively regulated and maintained a constant internal temperature. ### *2.1.2. PCMs as cold thermal energy storage system*

Many studies have been conducted using PCMs to store cold energy when it is in excess and used when needed. This strategy can reduce energy usage according to two main operating approaches: *Full storage* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(a)), in which the refrigeration system is completely shut down and the cooling load is provided by the PCM. This shifts the energy consumption from on-peak to off-peak periods. The PCM is then charged during off-peak hours using a refrigeration system cooling load. *Partial storage*: In this case, the PCM provides a part of the cooling loads, and the rest is covered by the refrigeration system. Partial storage is further subdivided into two types: *Load leveling* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(b)) and *Load limiting* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(c)). In load leveling, the refrigeration system is running all day, and the PCM provides a cooling load when the demand is greater than the refrigeration system output. ### *2.3. Effect of PCM's phase change temperature*

The PCT and latent heat capacity are crucial factors in selecting a PCM to ensure the desired food preservation temperature and the effectiveness of the adopted strategy.

If the PCMs are installed inside the refrigeration systems or on the evaporator, it is essential that their PCT align with the operational temperature range of the system, rather than being lower. This ensures that the PCM can effectively charge/discharge at the desired temperatures, thereby maintaining consistent temperature conditions. Alternatively, if the PCM is installed on the condenser, the PCT must correspond to the temperature range of the condenser to absorb heat efficiently and enhance the system's overall performance. Studies have proven that PCT has a positive influence on air temperature fluctuations within various refrigeration systems. They reported that the lower the PCT of the PCM, the lower the temperature fluctuations [\[119,121,122](#page-36-0),[147,151,159,166\]](#page-37-0).

[Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(a) illustrates the effect of PCT on the temperature within a refrigerated truck [[24\]](#page-34-0). The case with a lower PCM melting point continues to cool the cabinet for long periods. However, if the PCT is not selected correctly, it would have a negative effect on the energy consumption and COP of the system. [Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(b) presents the results of a study conducted by Azzouz et al. [\[151,162](#page-37-0)], in which they numerically evaluated the effect of the melting temperature of the PCM on the internal air temperature and the COP of a domestic refrigerator. The PCMs were placed behind the evaporator as shown in [Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(c). The results show that the temperature inside the system and its COP increase as the PCM's PCT increases. The decrease in COP is due to the compressor working harder to drop the internal temperature and achieve the freezing point of the PCM. Therefore, the PCM must be carefully selected to meet the required cooling loads.

## *2.9. Effect on CO2 emissions*

Phase Change Materials have emerged as a promising technology with the potential to indirectly contribute to greenhouse gas reduction. As mentioned in the introduction electricity generation has been identified as the primary sector responsible for generating GHGs. Therefore, by reducing the energy usage of refrigeration systems through installing PCM, we can help mitigate GHG emissions [[30,34,50](#page-35-0),[124](#page-37-0)]. These GHG reductions are often calculated by multiplying the energy reduced by the electricity emission factor, which is defined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change as the amount of GHG emitted per unit of electricity generated. It ranges between 578 and 800 kgCO2⋅MWh<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [[34](#page-35-0)[,124,177](#page-37-0)]. Oro´ et al. [[34\]](#page-35-0) have estimated the CO2 emission reduction achieved through the use of PCMs in the refrigeration sector. The research focused on the European case, considering an emissions factor of 578 kgCO2 for every MWh consumed. The potential CO2 reduction obtained by the assessment is represented in [Fig.](#page-21-0) 24(a). Results show that installing PCMs can lead to a decrease in total annual CO2 emissions ranging from 15.6 ktCO2 (low estimation) to 75.4 ktCO2 (high estimation) corresponding to approximately 5 % to 22 %, respectively.

Chandran et al. [\[50](#page-35-0)] conducted a numerical study to assess the energy efficiency, economic benefits, and environmental impacts of incorporating PCMs in a refrigerated vehicle. The model predicted a significant decrease in GHG emissions. [Fig.](#page-21-0) 24 illustrates the annual CO2 emissions savings for different PCM types and with different masses. It can be seen that the GHG emissions of all PCM types decrease as the PCM mass increases from 45 kg to 105 kg. Among the PCMs evaluated, A28 PCM exhibited the most significant impact, reducing annual emissions from 1818 kg CO2 to 3060 kg CO2/year. While the minimum reduction was observed for PT28. Overall, this work highlights the potential of PCM integration to contribute significantly to GHG mitigation in the cold chain transportation sector.

While the benefits of PCMs in reducing electricity consumption are well recognized, further efforts should now focus on the materials used in these PCMs. It is crucial to move beyond petroleum-based products like paraffin and shift towards more sustainable options by exploring bio-based materials. Transitioning to bio-based PCMs offers additional environmental advantages, such as biodegradability and nonflammability. The following section delves into the various bio-based materials that have been identified as promising alternatives for cold thermal energy storage applications.

Non-paraffin PCMs are mostly manufactured from bio-sources materials and used either directly in their raw form or after processing. Many studies have examined the thermal properties of these materials, focusing primarily on substances from the fatty acids, esters, and sugar alcohols families ([Fig.](#page-21-0) 25). These materials are selected due to their advantageous properties, including biodegradability, less flammability, and non-toxicity [[140](#page-37-0),[141](#page-37-0)]. This section gives an overview of the bio-based materials that could be employed as PCM for CTES. [Table](#page-33-0) 4 in [Appendix](#page-33-0) D presents a summary of the thermophysical properties, including the melting point and latent heat, of the materials discussed in this review.

In this review, the selection and analysis of materials focus on specific criteria to ensure their suitability for CTES applications, as illustrated in [Fig.](#page-22-0) 26. The selection process was guided by several key criteria, with particular emphasis on bio-based materials as the primary consideration. These materials are further assessed for their compatibility with CTES requirements, particularly their phase change temperature (PCT), ensuring they exhibit low or sub-zero PCTs. The DSC analysis revealed that among these esters, ε-caprolactone and γ-valerolactone exhibit appropriate melting temperatures for CTES applications. It has been observed that the phase transition for ε-caprolactone and γ-valerolactone starts around − 6.9 ◦C and − 40.3 ◦C, respectively. Further analysis revealed that the melting peaks of these compounds occur at temperatures of − 1.5 ◦C and − 32.1 ◦C, respectively. It is also important to note that the latent heat of these esters was found to be 121.7 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> and 109.8 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively, which is lower compared to the latent heat of the salt solution PCMs. Triglycerides are identified as a category of esters, where three FAs are bonded glycerol molecules. # *3.2. Edible and non-edible oils*

Vegetable oils are a composition of diverse fatty acids and triglycerides and have been employed as TES in many applications and for different temperature ranges (buildings applications; cold applications) [193–[196\]](#page-38-0). Soybean, canola, and corn oils are characterized by a low melting temperature of − 3.53 ◦C, − 20 ◦C, and − 24.6 ◦C, respectively, making these PCMs appropriate to CTES [\[197](#page-38-0)–199]. However, their latent heat is low, 47.4 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for soy oil, and 57.5 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for canola oil [[199](#page-38-0),[200](#page-38-0)]. Studies demonstrate that this limitation can be overcome by blending these oils with other substances to create a eutectic PCM with promising thermal properties. Studies show that eutectic mixtures based on these oils show promising thermal properties concerning both melting temperature and latent heat.

Rasta et al. [185–[187\]](#page-38-0) found that mixing corn or soy oils with tap water enhances this latter's thermal properties, allowing it to be employed as PCM for cold applications. The experimental results proved that soybean and corn oils act as nucleating agents, promoting quick water crystallization. This leads to a decrease in the supercooling degree by 6 K for 10 % oil (from 7.5 K to 1.1 K). In addition, the DSC analysis – represented in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(b) – shows that the melting temperature of the water-oil mixture decreases with oil concentration. For 10 % oil the melting point reaches − 7.5 ◦C and − 8 ◦C for corn and soy oil, respectively. However, for the same oil mass fraction, the latent has decreased by 42 % and 37 %, corresponding to 171.7 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and 185.9 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for corn, and soy oil, respectively.

In their work, Lorenzo et al. [\[188\]](#page-38-0), explored the development of a bio-PCM with a low melting temperature suitable for CTES applications. The PCMs were synthesized by blending canola oil and beeswax in different mass fractions (4 %, 6 %, 8 %, and 10 %). The DSC technique was used to determine the melting temperature and latent heat of the mixture. Results indicated that PCMs exhibited a high latent heat capacity, which increases as a function of the beeswax mass fraction. Specifically, they reported that the 10 % beeswax PCM was characterized by a melting temperature of − 13.6 ◦C. However, no specific details were provided for other PCMs concerning their melting temperatures. However, by analyzing [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(c) one can see that the melting peak is similar to that of 10 % beeswax.

Coffee ground waste valorization was investigated in a study by Jin Ong et al. [\[189\]](#page-38-0), where they extracted coffee waste oil (CWO) and used it as a sustainable PCM. The CWO was collected through extraction using three different solvents (acetone (A-CWO), ethanol (E-CWO), and hexane (H-CWO)). The chemical composition analysis showed that the oil contains 60–80 % fatty acids, including oleic acid, octadecanoic acid, and n-hexadecanoic acid, among others. The DSC analysis, presented in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(d), revealed that the CWO exhibits melting/freezing at temperatures of 5 ◦C and − 1.5 ◦C respectively, corresponding to a low latent heat value of 50 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> .

Olive mill waste could be valorized by extracting the olive oil, also known as secondary oil. It is estimated that for every ton of oil recovered, approximately 3 tons of waste are generated, including a solid waste known as pomace and a liquid waste called margins. Pomace wastes contain 5–13 % of oil [201–[203\]](#page-38-0), while margins contain 7–11 g⋅L<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [\[204](#page-38-0)–206]. Because of this significant percentage of oil, this waste could be used as a PCM. The process of melting occurs at temperatures ranging from − 4.5 ◦C to 10.4 ◦C, while freezing occurs between − 11 ◦C and − 8 ◦C. The enthalpies involved in these processes are measured at 105 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for melting and 97 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for solidification [\[207\]](#page-38-0). In addition, the secondary oil has a low thermal conductivity ranging from 0.15 to 0.17 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [\[208,209](#page-38-0)].

Another waste that can be recycled is date seeds that contain 4–13 % oil that can be extracted [210–[212\]](#page-38-0). The amount of oil in date seeds is determined by the kind of date and the extraction process. Ourradi et al. [[213](#page-38-0)], investigated the oil content of eight different dates seed types and reported that it ranges from 6 to 10 %. Date seed oil is characterized by a melting temperature of around − 3 ◦C. However, this oil has a low melting enthalpy, between 60 and 70 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [214–[216](#page-38-0)].

# *3.3. Animal wastes*

Fatty acids are compounds that can be derived from sources like chicken, pork, and fish wastes, these materials present promising opportunities as PCMs. While various animal wastes can serve as PCMs, our primary interest lies in materials suitable for low-temperature applications. Additional details on the thermal properties of fish waste oils can be found in [Table](#page-33-0) 4 [\(Appendix](#page-33-0) D).

Other animal wastes, such as pork and chicken waste, have been investigated as potential PCMs. These materials demonstrate promising thermal characteristics that make them attractive for various thermal management applications. Research has highlighted their effectiveness in systems such as building and photovoltaic temperature regulation [[220](#page-38-0),[221](#page-38-0)]. Notably, these fatty acid-based PCMs exhibit a PCT typically ranging between 25 ◦C and 55 ◦C. ### <span id="page-24-0"></span>*3.4. Amino acid*

Glycine, recognized as the smallest and simplest amino acid [[224](#page-38-0),[225](#page-38-0)], has emerged as a widely studied bio-based phase change material (PCM) due to its unique combination of favorable properties. The material's growing popularity in thermal energy storage applications can be attributed to two key factors: its cost-effectiveness and the remarkably high latent heat exhibited by its aqueous solutions [[226](#page-38-0)]. The thermal properties of glycine solutions have attracted considerable research attention, with multiple studies [\[226](#page-38-0)–229] evaluating their characteristics for potential applications in CTES.

Liu et al. Similarly, the PCT showed a decrease from − 5.06 ◦C at 14 % glycine to − 5.12 ◦C at 16 %, reaching − 5.29 ◦C at 18 % concentration.

melting/freezing latent heat of 243.8/198.8 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . While its phase change temperatures were recorded at − 5 ◦C for melting and − 14.6 ◦C for freezing. In addition, the thermal conductivity of the mixture was enhanced by 216 % compared to that of GW-PCM.

Among the tested formulations, the 5 % glycine–NaCl solution exhibited superior thermophysical properties, including a PCT of − 5.94 ◦C, and a latent heat of 291.15 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . To address this challenge, further research is needed to optimize the composition of these bio-eutectic mixtures, aiming to minimize the loss in latent heat capacity while maintaining or improving the desired thermal properties.

## *3.6. Practical implementation of bio-based PCMs for CTES*

Bio-based PCMs have been extensively studied in building applications. However, their use in CTES applications remains underexplored. Therefore, the current literature on this specific topic is quite limited, with only a handful of preliminary studies available [\[227,238](#page-39-0)–240].

**Fig. 30.** Latent heat as a function of melting temperature for various bio-based materials [[37,](#page-35-0)[181](#page-38-0)–192[,196](#page-38-0)–210[,212](#page-38-0)–219[,223](#page-38-0)[,230,232,234](#page-39-0)–237], data converted into graph.

These studies primarily focus on the integration of bio-based PCMs into cooler boxes, with a specific emphasis on evaluating their potential to extend the shelf life of perishable food products.

Liu et al. [[239](#page-39-0)] investigated three distinct storage scenarios for strawberries to evaluate the effect of glycine‑potassium sorbate-water PCM (designated as 6-PCM5) and water-based PCM on the strawberries quality compared to the reference scenario (Without PCM). In each scenario, the strawberries were stored in a cooler box and exposed to the ambient temperature (15 ◦C) for 32 h. The results showed that the first scenario involved the use of 6-PCM5 – which exhibits a PCT of approximately − 5.78 ◦C – was capable of maintaining the temperature within the box between 0.45 ◦C and 3 ◦C for 22.83 h. This scenario resulted in a minimal weight loss rate of 0.74 % and a rotting rate of 7.69 %, indicating an optimal preservation of fruit quality. The second scenario used ice as the cooling medium, yielding a slightly higher average temperature compared to 6-PCM5. Consequently, this scenario exhibited a higher weight loss rate of 0.88 % and a rotting rate of 17.65 %. This highlighted the limitations of ice-based PCM in maintaining ideal storage conditions. The third scenario, exhibits significant deterioration in strawberries quality, with a weight loss rate of 36.8 % and a rotting rate of 58.14 % after the 32-h storage period.

Liu et al. [\[240](#page-39-0)] have also evaluated adding a glycine-expandedgraphite-water mixture (G:EG:W) on the quality of strawberries under room temperature (15 ◦C). The experimental results showed that after 21 h of storage, the weight loss rates of the strawberries were observed to be 5.92 %, 1.03 %, and 0.01 %, for the reference scenario (without PCM), the scenario using ice as PCM, and the scenario using G:EG:W PCM, respectively. The author also reported that the rotting rate when using G:EG:W PCM was 10.11 % and 65.76 % lower than ice and reference scenarios. Measuring 11.63 %, 21.74 %, and 87.5 %, respectively.

Nicolalde et al. [\[238\]](#page-39-0) evaluated the potential application of avocado seed oil as a PCM. The authors conducted numerical analyses to evaluate various PCM distribution configurations within an insulated container, comparing the thermal performance of four 75 mL packages against an alternative arrangement consisting of two 75 mL packages plus one 150 mL package. The results indicated that the configuration using four 75 mL packages exhibited superior performance. In assessing the impact of this bio-based PCM on food temperature regulation, the results revealed enhanced cold temperature preservation compared to the control condition. The data showed that the bio-based PCM maintained lower temperatures for longer durations, reaching 12 ◦C approximately 2 h after the control case. Furthermore, following an 8-storage period, the internal air temperature of the container incorporating the PCM was observed to be 6 ◦C lower than the container without PCM. These results suggest promising applications for bio-based PCMs in temperaturecontrolled storage systems.

The recent research on bio-based PCMs demonstrates promising potential for sustainable food preservation applications, particularly in extending the shelf life of perishable products. Although the number of studies investigating bio-based PCMs for CTES applications remains notably limited, and the implementation of these materials is still in its early stages, the reviewed studies highlight significant improvements in maintaining product quality, with notably reduced weight loss and rotting rates compared to without PCM cases. These findings not only support the feasibility of bio-based PCMs but also indicate their potential role in advancing sustainable cold chain solutions.

### *3.7. Life Cycle Assessment*

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a widely recognized method for evaluating the environmental impact of materials and products across their entire lifecycle, from production to end-of-life, as shown in Fig. 31 [[241](#page-39-0)]. This methodology considers multiple factors such as human health, greenhouse gas emissions, energy consumption, etc. to assess a product's overall sustainability [\[144](#page-37-0)]. Applications of bio-based PCMs remain in the early stages for CTES. As a result, the environmental impacts of bio-based PCMs are challenging to evaluate due to the limited number of LCA studies available [[62\]](#page-35-0). Existing research predominantly focuses on the use of bio-based PCMs in building applications [\[62](#page-35-0),[144](#page-37-0)]. These studies demonstrate that incorporating PCMs in construction can offer more ecological benefits compared to conventional insulation materials [\[144\]](#page-37-0). However, the environmental footprint of PCMs varies significantly depending on their type.

For instance, Fabiani et al. [[242](#page-39-0)] reported that raw palm oil has a higher carbon footprint (2.772 kg CO2-eq) compared to paraffin (0.207 kg CO2-eq). Conversely, palm oil waste is more sustainable, with lower emissions (0.053 kg CO2-eq). Another study [\[243\]](#page-39-0) compared the environmental impact of used cooking oil-based PCMs (UCO-PCM) to commercial paraffin and found that UCO-PCM had a higher environmental impact. This was attributed to its lower latent heat, which requires larger quantities of UCO-PCM to achieve the same heat storage capacity as paraffin, consequently increasing encapsulation material requirements and overall environmental impact.

Noel ¨ et al. [\[244\]](#page-39-0) highlighted potential reductions of GHG emissions of dodecanoic fatty acid derived from palm oil for use in a domestic solar water heater. The study concluded that bio-based PCMs are a viable energy-saving solution, with a payback period of less than two years,

and a potential greenhouse gas emission reduction of approximately 1600 kg CO2 annually for a typical American home. However, environmental considerations must account for associated challenges like potential deforestation in palm oil production.

A comparative study by Menoufi et al. [\[245\]](#page-39-0) evaluated the environmental impact of esters as PCMs compared to paraffin and hydrated salts. The analysis included scenarios using polyurethane foam alone, foam combined with PCM, and a reference case without PCM or foam. The results showed that adding polyurethane foam increased the environmental impact by 20 %. Incorporating PCMs into the foam led to environmental score increases of 6.1 %, 7.7 %, and 18.5 % for esters, hydrated salts, and paraffin, respectively. However, using esters and hydrated salts resulted in 10.5 % and 9.1 % lower impacts, respectively, compared to paraffin.

These studies emphasize the need for more comprehensive LCA studies particularly exploring bio-based PCM applications for CTES applications, to better understand their environmental trade-offs and optimize their sustainability.

### **4. Conclusion**

The integration of conventional PCMs in refrigeration systems has proven to be a promising approach to improving energy efficiency and environmental sustainability. This review has demonstrated that PCMs can be effectively incorporated into various refrigeration applications, ranging from domestic refrigerators to industrial cold storage facilities, through two main strategies: thermal insulation and cold energy storage systems. As a thermal insulation method, PCMs effectively reduce heat transfer through system walls, thereby decreasing the energy consumption required to maintain desired temperatures. The cold energy storage approach uses PCMs to store excess cooling capacity for later use, offering benefits during compressor off-cycles, door openings, defrosting, or power outages. Moreover, this method enables innovative energy management strategies, such as storing cold energy during lowcost electricity periods and utilizing alternative energy sources like photovoltaic power for PCM charging. The effectiveness of PCM integration critically depends on material selection, placement, and orientation. For example, placing PCMs near evaporator fans facilitates natural convection and promotes more uniform temperature distribution. Conversely, improper positioning can negatively impact air circulation and overall system efficiency.

Bio-based PCMs, derived from renewable plant and animal sources, have gained significant attention as eco-friendly and cost-effective alternatives to petroleum-based options. These materials offer a wide range of phase change temperatures, from temperatures suitable for building applications to low/subzero temperatures appropriate for CTES. However, some biomaterials are characterized by low latent heat capacity, which constrains their use. To address this challenge, researchers have investigated bio-eutectic compounds, which combine bio-based materials with supplementary substances, including water, salt solutions, or beeswax. These hybrid systems effectively enhance both phase change temperature and latent heat capacity, thereby optimizing PCM performance for CTES applications. Preliminary studies on bio-based PCM applications have demonstrated their capacity to provide sustained cold energy and maintain stable air temperatures, suggesting potential benefits for preserving product quality during storage, particularly for temperature-sensitive perishable goods.

While the current review highlights the promising thermal characteristics of bio-based materials for CTES, several critical research directions merit further investigation to advance the field:

– Performance evaluation of bio-based PCMs: future research should comprehensively assess the potential of bio-based PCMs in CTES. Beyond current applications in cooler boxes and product preservation, systematic studies are needed to evaluate their impact on energy consumption and overall system performance. Comparative analyses with conventional refrigeration technologies would provide valuable insights into the practical viability of these sustainable alternatives.

- Optimization of thermal properties: continued optimization of thermal characteristics remains crucial. Researchers should concentrate on improving key parameters such as melting temperature, latent heat capacity, and thermal conductivity. These enhancements are essential to meet the specific and demanding requirements for CTES applications.
- Advanced encapsulation techniques: research should explore innovative encapsulation methods, such as microencapsulation and nanoencapsulation. These methods can potentially improve heat transfer capabilities and thermal conductivity, thereby addressing the current performance limitations of bio-based PCMs.
- Eutectic/hybrid material: investigating eutectic/hybrid mixtures of bio-based PCMs is another point that needs to be explored. This also could overcome the limitations associated with low latent heat capacity while optimizing their phase change characteristics.
- Sustainability assessment: a comprehensive lifecycle analysis is important to fully understand the environmental impacts of biobased PCMs. Future studies should conduct rigorous evaluations of the ecological footprint throughout the production, utilization, and disposal phases. This research will provide critical insights into the sustainability advantages of bio-based alternatives compared to conventional PCMs.

Addressing these areas, the development and adoption of bio-based PCMs can significantly contribute to a sustainable, high-performance, and cost-effective solution to advance the field of cold thermal energy storage.
