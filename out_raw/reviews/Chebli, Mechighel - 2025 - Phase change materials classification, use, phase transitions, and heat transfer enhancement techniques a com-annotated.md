# **Phase change materials: classifcation, use, phase transitions, and heat transfer enhancement techniques: a comprehensive review**

**Fatiha Chebli1 · Farid Mechighel[2](http://orcid.org/0000-0002-4726-7613)**

Received: 26 May 2024 / Accepted: 21 November 2024 / Published online: 6 January 2025 © Akadémiai Kiadó Zrt 2025

#### **Abstract**

Currently, there is great interest in producing thermal energy (heat) from renewable sources and storing this energy in a suitable system. The use of a latent heat storage (LHS) system using a phase change material (PCM) is a very efcient storage means (medium) and ofers the advantages of high volumetric energy storage capacity and the quasi-isothermal nature of the storage process. In recent years, phase change materials (PCMs) have become an interesting research area due to their advantages especially in thermal energy storage (TES). Indeed, there are a large number of PCMs that melt and solidify over a wide temperature range, making them interesting thermal energy storage media in several applications. In the literature, research on PCMs and their associated applications has attracted and still attracts great interest from various researchers and scientists. Most of the research studies on phase change materials (PCMs) have been generally devoted to the development of PCM-based energy storage technologies, the promotion of PCM-based renewable energy sources, and the encouragement of sustainable/proftable (economic) use of PCM-based energy. In order to get an overview of current progress and trends, to highlight research and to identify gaps, from the literature reviews undertaken on this research topic, it is useful to review the major research studies conducted in this feld. Our analysis showed that the literature lacks many comprehensive analyses and studies on the applications of PCMs, the phase transition processes (melting and solidifcation) of PCMs and the factors that infuence these transitions, and in particular the calculation models of the thermal performance parameters of a PCM performing a phase transition and the thermal performance parameters of a PCM-based TES system (referred to as LHS unit). To address these questions, we have presented in this review article a detailed overview of the literature on (a) relevant practical applications of PCMs, (b) characteristics and performances of phase transition processes, (c) major factors infuencing PCM transition processes such as geometric design of the PCM tank and its orientation, imposed boundary and operating conditions, thermophysical properties of the material (PCM), and (d) models for calculating thermal performance parameters for a PCM performing a phase transition and for an LHS unit. In addition, several techniques aimed at improving heat transfer in PCMs have been introduced and discussed. The fndings indicate that there are three types of PCMs: eutectic, inorganic, and organic. Numerous other industries also use PCMs, such as solar energy (including thermal energy storage through the use of photovoltaic and latent heat systems); buildings; HVAC systems; textiles; the biomedical, food, and agricultural industries; the automotive sector; and desalination. Besides PCMs classifcation and use, it was found that during phase transitions of PCMs heat transfer is dominated by conduction and natural convection. During melting, conduction heat transfer is dominant in the early stages, and as the PCM melts, natural convection dominates. Unlike melting, solidifcation is dominated by conductive heat transfer. On the other hand, boundary conditions, material properties, and enclosure confguration and orientation all found having an impact on melting and solidifcation. In this context, by increasing, for example, thermal conductivity, viscosity, wall-imposed temperature, and PCM initial temperature, as well as by decreasing PCM latent heat of melting, PCM melting point, and PCM system orientation, the melting process rate increases. However, by increasing thermal conductivity, viscosity, melting point, and PCM system orientation, as well as by lowering the latent heat of melting, the initial PCM temperature, and the imposed wall temperature, the solidifcation process rate increases. Lastly, introducing external felds and adding high thermal conductivity additives like fns, metal foam, and nanoparticles can greatly increase the rate at which PCM melts and solidifes.

## **Introduction**

Over the coming decades, continued rapid development of the global economy and population growth will follow strong demand for energy supplies. According to British Petroleum, by 2035, the world's population will reach 8.7 billion and global energy consumption will increase by 37% [\[1](#page-50-0)].

Energy (heat) produced from fossil fuels has always satisfed and responded to the needs of humanity since the appearance of these resources. On the other hand, the limited availability (no abundance) or even the exhaustion of these resources and the consequences (on health and the environment) resulting from their use such as the emission of harmful gases and global warming have raised several challenges and concerns. These major issues have led to the development and adoption of renewable energy sources due to their abundance and availability. According to the International Energy Agency's prediction, by 2030, the proportion of energy production that comes from renewable sources will rise from about 29% in 2021 to over 60% [[2](#page-50-1)].

However, due to the intermittency and instability of renewable energy control, thermal energy storage (TES) can overcome the disadvantage of renewable energy systems by storing the energy generated during of-peak hours of consumption (high consumption) and returning (recovering/releasing) this energy during the period of low consumption. Thermal energy storage (TES), which uses a storage medium (a continuum medium), can occur in all three modes: sensible heat storage (SHS), latent heat storage (LHS), and thermochemical heat storage (TCS) (see Fig. [1](#page-3-0)). Sensible heat storage [[3](#page-50-2), [4\]](#page-50-3) consists of adding energy to a material (the storage medium) to increase its temperature without changing phase. Latent heat storage [[5,](#page-50-4) [6\]](#page-50-5) relies on the release or absorption (storage) of heat (called latent heat) when the phase of the storage material changes from liquid to solid or vice versa, respectively. Thermochemical storage (TCS) [\[7–](#page-50-6)[9\]](#page-50-7) occurs at the molecular level after a chemical band-breaking or dissociation reaction, thereby releasing energy and recovering it in a reversible reaction. A phase change material (PCM) is a substance made up of molecules that is primarily used for storing thermal energy. The principle behind its function is straightforward: when the temperature rises, the material undergoes a phase change from solid to liquid (melting) and absorbs energy during this process. Thus, melting (energy charging process) is an endothermic transformation. Similarly, when the temperature decreases, the PCM in the liquid phase solidifes and releases (desorbs) energy and the solidifcation (energydischarging process) is an exothermic reaction. These exceptional characteristics of PCMs make their use as a thermal energy storage medium in engineering very broad and promising. Indeed, energy storage using phase change materials is today used in numerous practical applications, for example: in latent heat storage systems (called LHS units), in construction materials (e.g., embedding PCM in brick) used in buildings, gymnasiums, etc., in *cooling* devices for electronic apparatus, in HVAC systems, in the textile industry, in the biomedical feld, etc. It should be noted here that an LHS unit is a storage system consisting essentially of a PCM tank comprising one (or more) internal channels. The internal (inner) channel is traversed by the fow of heat transfer fuid "HTF," which ensures either the release of latent heat from the LHS unit (in the event of solidifcation of PCM in the tank) or the storage of latent heat in the LHS unit (in the event of PCM melting in the tank). More details on this context are given below.

Nevertheless, despite the aforementioned advantages of PCMs (i.e., high volumetric heat storage capacity and quasi-isothermal operating conditions), it is imperative to note that PCMs have drawbacks. Typically, a PCM is characterized by poor thermophysical properties, including its thermal conductivity and thermal difusivity. The low thermal conductivity and thermal difusivity of PCM reduces the *heat transfer rate* during melting and/or solidifcation (i.e., melting rate and/or solidifcation rate), which increases the overall *duration* (time) of melting and/or solidifcation cycles; which in turn can cause large temperature diferences in the PCM, causing the LHS storage system to overheat and potentially cause hardware failure [\[11](#page-50-9)]. On the other hand, due to the low heat capacity of PCM, LHS systems see their productivity (in terms of *energy stored*) weakened. Therefore, improvements in thermal conductivity, thermal difusivity, heat capacity, and other properties/characteristics of PCM (among other means discussed below) are essential to increase the heat transfer rate and energy stored, during the phase change undertaken by the PCM, and to reduce the time of this transition; which thus leads to increasingly efcient PCM-based storage systems [\[12](#page-50-10)]. The heat transfer rate, energy stored, and time of the transition process represent the main thermal performances of a PCM-based storage system.

The use of PCM types and heat transfer enhancement techniques for LTE devices has been extensively reviewed and published. The unique thermal characteristics of PCMs, their classifcation and working principles, and the advances in PCM applications in various industries are all summarized in detail by the study of Reddy et al. [\[13](#page-50-11)]; which also highlights the enormous potential of PCMs to increase energy efciency, reduce carbon emissions, and support sustainable development. Salman et al. [[14](#page-50-12)] explored the integration of phase change materials (PCMs) with building materials and reviewed various experimental and numerical methods to evaluate the thermal performance of these PCM-containing materials. The three types of heat transfer interfaces (HTIs) between PCM and heat transfer fuid (HTF): fat surface, extended surface, and direct contact were used by Tian et al. [[15\]](#page-50-13) to calculate and evaluate the overall convective heat transfer coefcients on the PCM side. The comparison results highlight the importance of increased accessibility between PCM and HTF and demonstrate that expandable and diversifed HTIs are essential to improve heat transfer performance. Various techniques employed by researchers to improve the characteristics and thermal conductivity of PCMs are covered by Hussein et al. [[16](#page-50-14)]. The authors also discussed the factors that infuence the performance of PCM as TES, including PCM type, encapsulation type, TES geometry, and phase change temperature that should be selected based on the application. In their review of recent developments regarding the use of various types of metallic fns in LHTES, Low et al. [\[17\]](#page-50-15) summarized the experimental and numerical research for charge and discharge cycles in LHTES, with emphasis on the efects of each fn parameter (length, angle, location, pitch, material, thickness, perforation, etc.). In addition to providing insight into the logical design and controlled preparation of PCM catalysts composites, Wang et al. [[18\]](#page-50-16) highlighted new developments in phase change materials to improve the catalytic process.

It should be emphasized, however, that the literature does not contain much in-depth analysis of PCM applications, heat transfer mechanisms, or how parameters afect the phase change process. This review discusses the classifcation and main use of PCMs in various thermal engineering systems encountered in practice. Indeed, there are diferent categories of phase change materials which have been classifed according to various and varied classifcation criteria, the main criteria of which we attempt to clarify here. Also, to highlight the main transformations (melting and solidifcation) undergone by a phase change material, this review pays particular attention to the main characteristics of the melting and solidifcation phase transition processes occurring in "a system of latent heat storage" itself (LHS unit). Indeed, an in-depth analysis of the main parameters that afect these processes, including the geometric shape of the storage system (PCM tank), the conditions and fow characteristics of the heat transfer fuid (mainly the fow rate of HTF and the "initial" temperature of HTF at the inlet of the inner channel of the LHS unit), the operating conditions of the storage unit, etc. were provided. This review also highlights the thermal performance of PCM-based storage systems and discusses various techniques from the literature to enhance this performance. These performance improvement techniques are essentially based on the one hand on the association of metal foams with the PCM or on the inclusion (insertion) of dispersion of nanoparticles into the PCM; or on the other hand on the incorporation of fns in the LHS system or the application of external felds to the system.

To address the growing need for efcient and innovative energy solutions, this study aims to not only fll the gaps in existing knowledge but also provide a comprehensive exploration of the applications and phase transition processes of PCMs and reveal recent advances to enhance performance or outcomes in the thermal performance of storage systems. This study will facilitate, for example, the selection of efcient LHS units, composed of reliable phase change materials confned in compatible and optimal tanks, presenting efcient geometric confgurations.

## **Classifcation and use of PCMs**

The nature and use of the phase change material are the most important standards (criteria) that must be considered in the design of the PCM-based thermal storage system.

Inorganic phase change materials are the fundamental components used in latent heat storage. They can be classifed into two main groups: (1) hydrated salts, characterized by a combination of inorganic salts and water forming a crystalline solid (ice), and (2) metals, which have not been seriously considered taken into account in PCM-based LHS technology, mainly due to their mass-related disadvantages [\[22\]](#page-50-21).

Eutectic materials are a distinct category of phase change materials resulting from the mixture of organic–organic or inorganic–inorganic or organic–inorganic compounds with specifc atomic ratios (concentrations). These materials (called alloys) solidify (freeze) and melt as a mixture of crystals, providing unique thermal properties [\[23\]](#page-50-22).

## **Use of phase change materials**

### **Latent heat storage in an LHS unit**

As shown in Fig. [1,](#page-3-0) an LHS storage unit is a "thermal energy storage" (TES) system that uses phase change material as the storage medium. Since the phase transitions of PCMs are characterized by latent heat storage (absorption or release), a PCM-based TES unit is also called: an LHS storage unit (consisting mainly of a PCM tank crossed by a fow of heat transfer fuid (HTF)). In practice, there are several geometric shapes adopted for the physical domain of the LHS storage unit, including, for example, (a) the double-tube LHS storage unit, shown in Fig. [3a](#page-7-0), consists of two concentric horizontal elliptical pipes: the inner pipe (tube) in which the HTF fows and the outer pipe (the annular space) is flled with PCM (i.e., the PCM tank) [[26](#page-51-1)], (b) the storage unit in Fig. [3](#page-7-0)b which uses a modifed multi-tube heat exchanger (THX) flled with PCM [[27](#page-51-2)], and (c) the shell-and-tube LHS unit, which consists of a shell-and-tube heat exchanger as shown in Fig. [3c](#page-7-0) (this last storage unit can be used, for example, in the solar water heating system [[28\]](#page-51-3)). Another representative drawing of a typical LHS storage unit used, for example, in cooling/heating of a house is shown in Fig. [3d](#page-7-0). In the literature, several other geometric shapes of LHS units have been used, as shown in Table [3](#page-8-0).

### **Other applications of phase change materials as TES storage media**

Although PCMs are used as thermal energy storage media (TES-media) in several felds of engineering, such as those shown in Fig. [4;](#page-13-0) however, in this review, in addition to the storage area mentioned above in LHS units, special attention is also given, only for applications of PCMs as TES storage media in the following areas: buildings, HVAC systems, cooling of electronic components, textiles, biomedical. Table [3](#page-8-0) presents some recent studies regarding diferent applications of PCMs.

*Remark 1* In the literature, several designations have been adopted for the PCM domain in an LHS storage unit, for example: PCM tank, PCM enclosure, PCM cavity, PCM container, PCM reservoir, PCM capsule etc. In addition, in the feld of energy storage, the storage system is called an LHS unit (or also LHS module), in electronics the energy storage can be done in the cooling device, in the building sector, it represents, for example, the brick enclosure which is adapted to include a PCM inside, and so on.

**Construction materials in buildings** Energy consumption in buildings depends on commercial and residential activities. PCMs can signifcantly improve their energy performance. Indeed, when the building is heated, the PCMs (incorporated in its construction materials) melt and the energy is stored; while when it cools, the PCMs solidify and the energy is released. Generally, PCMs are integrated into buildings' construction materials by mixing them during manufacture or by encapsulation. In practice, there are several options for integrating phase change materials into building materials, for example: (1) inserting two PCMs into a brick cavity (enclosure); the frst is encapsulated in the middle of the brick (Fig. [5](#page-13-1)a) and the second is encapsulated near the exterior brick wall (Fig. [5](#page-13-1)b) [[29\]](#page-51-4), (2) by embedding the PCM inside a brick enclosure and covering the inner wall of the brick (Fig. [3](#page-7-0)c) [[30\]](#page-51-5), and (3) by flling a glazed unit of the building with the PCM (Fig. [5](#page-13-1)d) [[31\]](#page-51-6). Other examples of PCM-based construction materials in buildings adopted in the literature are given in Table [3.](#page-8-0)

**HVAC systems** In practice, there are several options for integrating phase change materials into HVAC systems, for example:(a) applying phase change material suspensions (PCMSs), consisting of microencapsulated phase change material suspension and clathrate hydrate suspension, in the secondary loop refrigeration and air conditioning system [[32\]](#page-51-7), (b) incorporating a PCM-based heat exchanger into a water-based HVAC system that uses *active* beams as terminal units [[33\]](#page-51-8), and (c) using two PCM storage tanks in the HVAC system: the frst is used during the night to cool (therefore solidify) the PCM and the heat released by the solidifcation is then used to heat the building; while the second is used during the day to heat the PCM, the heat is therefore absorbed by melting which allows the building to be cooled [[34\]](#page-51-9). A schematic representation of an LHS unit used in HVAC is given in Fig. [6](#page-14-0). Other examples of PCMbased HVAC systems adopted in the literature are given in Table [3](#page-8-0).

## **Discussions on the classifcation and use of PCMs**

As mentioned earlier, PCMs are substances that can absorb or release thermal energy during their phase transition from solid to liquid or vice versa, respectively. They are classifed according to their physical state and nature into three categories: organic, inorganic, and eutectic. These PCMs are used in various applications including latent heat storage units, buildings, HVAC systems, electronic cooling, the textile industry, and the biomedical feld, etc.

#### **Use of PCMs in thermal energy storage systems (LHS units)**

The latent heat storage system (LHS unit) is generally based on the use of a tubular heat exchanger which ensures the heat exchange between the HTF fuid and the PCM (see Fig. [3](#page-7-0)). This exchange depends on diferent parameters, such as the temperature of the HTF at the inlet of the inner tube (i.e., the temperature at the start (initial) of the charging or discharging process),*T*in,HTF , and the fow rate of the HTF, (*ṁ* in,HTF <sup>=</sup> *<sup>m</sup>̇* out,HTF <sup>=</sup> *<sup>m</sup>̇* HTF), as well as the thermophysical properties of the storage medium (the PCM). According to the literature reviewed here, the use of PCMs with appropriate properties increases the volumetric heat storage capacity of the storage unit. In addition, increasing the temperature of the HTF at the inlet of the inner tube and the fow rate of the HTF can also improve the energy stored in the PCM, hence the volumetric heat storage capacity of the storage unit.

#### **Use of PCMs in buildings and HVAC systems**

Heating and cooling of buildings using PCMs constitute a very important application sector for PCMs. The application relies on mixing PCMs with building materials encapsulating them inside bricks or embedding them between layers of bricks in walls or ceilings. The principle of this application is based on the absorption of heat by the PCM during its melting for the cooling needs of the building. Then, this absorbed (stored) heat is released by the PCM as the PCM solidifes for the heating needs of the building. This technique therefore makes it possible to regulate the temperature inside the building, reduce heat loss and thus increase energy savings. When talking about buildings, we should also mention HVAC systems, the efciency of which can be improved through the use of PCM. This is because, during of-peak periods, the HVAC system's PCM melts and absorbs energy, which is then released (upon solidifcation) when needed. This can reduce peak energy demand, matching energy demand and supply, and improving the overall efciency of the HVAC system.

### **Use of PCMs in electronic cooling (PCM‑based heat sink devices)**

For electronic cooling, PCM is used to absorb heat generated by electronic components during intense device activity to ensure a more stable temperature through advanced thermal management to prevent device overheating. The technique relies on the thermal absorption of the equipment. This is because as the temperature increases, the PCM melts and absorbs a signifcant amount of heat, which is stored as latent heat and keeps the equipment temperature constant. The use of this technique involves (i) embedding PCM capsules or PCM materials directly into the heat sink structure, (ii) applying them as a coating around heat-sensitive electronic components, or (iii) integrating PCMs into the materials that make up the housing of electronic equipment.

#### **Use of PCMs in the textiles industry**

The application of PCM in textiles is established either in textile fbers or through a fexible method involving microencapsulation in diferent parts to improve the thermal comfort of clothing and adapt to diferent environments. They thus ensure a constant body temperature by absorbing body heat when the temperature rises and releasing it when it falls. In addition, the use of PCM in textiles can reduce humidity. The technique relies on the absorption of heat by the PCM when the environment is hot and the temperature is above the melting point of the PCM; the PCM absorbs heat and stores additional (extra) heat as latent heat without any change in body temperature. As the ambient temperature decreases, stored latent heat is released into the environment, keeping the body temperature stable and comfortable. This technique is used by (1) applying PCM to the surface of textiles by coating or impregnation, (2) incorporating PCM into the fbers of the textile, (3) adding PCM-containing coatings to the fabric, or (4) directly integrating PCM into textile fbers during the fbers manufacturing process.

#### **Use of PCMs in food and agriculture industries**

In the agriculture and food industries, phase change materials (PCMs) are used to improve productivity and temperature control. By regulating the temperature in storage facilities and shipping containers, they help extend the shelf life of food and prevent spoilage. In logistics, they regulate the temperature throughout transportation. To promote the best possible plant growth in greenhouses, PCMs absorb excess heat during the day and release it at night. They can also increase the efciency of refrigeration systems in the food industry and lower soil temperatures, improving product quality, reducing waste, and increasing energy efciency. In addition, PCMs can be used to maintain constant temperatures by storing excess heat generated during the drying process and gradually releasing it.

#### **Use of PCMs in automotive**

Thermal performance and comfort in vehicles can be improved while increasing energy savings by integrating PCMs for thermal control in the automotive industry. To improve their thermal performance and stop leaks, PCMs are often enclosed in containers called microcapsules. PCMs can be integrated into several automotive parts, such as battery cases or surrounding cells to maintain ideal temperatures, seat or upholstery covers to help control cabin temperatures, and spaces between layers of vehicle body panels to control heat absorption.

### **Use of PCMs in desalination**

Phase change material (PCM) modules integrated into modular desalination systems are an efective way to increase water production. To maintain ideal temperatures, each module is composed of layers of PCM that collect heat during the day, promoting the evaporation of seawater, and release this stored energy at night. In addition to increasing water production and thermal stability, this design allows for easy expansion and customization based on demand. These systems use PCM technology to save energy and operating costs, making them a sustainable choice to solve the water crisis.

Despite all these advantages of PCMs, it should be emphasized that the use of PCMs also requires consideration of the challenges and limitations associated with their use, including cost, appropriate choice of PCM for a specifc application, management of durability of PCM and optimal design and system integration using PCM (see also the disadvantages of PCMs provided in Table [2](#page-5-2) above).

## **Defnition and principle of a phase transition**

Phase transition processes of a PCM can be performed in the following forms: solid–solid, solid–liquid, gas–liquid, and gas–solid. The transformation of a phase change material from one state to another is a nonlinear transient phenomenon that occurs with the presence of a moving solid–liquid interface (also called a mushy zone, for example [[86\]](#page-52-30)). This problem is generally referred to as the *moving boundary* problem [\[87](#page-53-0)]. In the present article, attention is paid only to solid–liquid and liquid–solid transitions which are the main phase transformations that can occur in the feld of energy storage. Figure [13](#page-17-0) gives a schematic representation of these two phase transitions (transformations).

Recall that phase change materials (also called latent heat storage materials) are specifcally used in latent thermal energy storage systems, as discussed above. They are capable of absorbing (i.e., storing) (during melting) and releasing (during solidifcation) large amounts of latent heat over a near-isothermal temperature range (interval) during phase transition [[88\]](#page-53-1). **Efect of thermophysical properties of PCM on the melting process** The thermophysical properties of PCM material such as thermal conductivity, *k*, melting temperature (melting point), *T*m, latent heat of fusion (melting), *H*L, and viscosity, *𝜇*, signifcantly afect the melting process. Using a PCM with high thermal conductivity speeds up the melting process; conversely, using a PCM with a high melting temperature results in a longer melting time, Fan et al. [\[97](#page-53-18)], which guides us to choose PCMs with low melting temperature and high thermal conductivity. On the other hand, using a high latent heat value of PCM increases the melting time (i.e., speeds up the melting), Li et al. [\[98](#page-53-19)]. It should be added that the solid–liquid interface is also afected by the viscosity of the PCM; indeed, the melting rate is proportional to the viscosity value of the PCM, Kasibhatla et al. [[99\]](#page-53-20). Note that the melting rate is defned as the energy stored in the storage system (PCM domain) divided by the melting time.

**Efect of operating conditions (mainly initial and boundary conditions) on the melting process** The type of thermal boundary conditions imposed on the wall of the domain (PCM tank) containing the solid (PCM) and from which melting must initiate, plays an important role in the performance of the PCM melting process. Among these types, the specifed temperature condition and the convective heat fux condition are distinguished. Several studies in the literature (see also Table [4\)](#page-18-0) have examined the efect of the type of thermal boundary conditions on the melting process of a PCM solid. These studies concluded that imposing a high temperature (relative to the melting point) on the wall improves the melting rate by reducing its duration (melting time), while using convective heat fux, as a boundary condition on the wall, leads more to an improvement in melting compared to the previous heated wall condition.

On the other hand, it has also been demonstrated that a high-value temperature condition (compared to the melting point) imposed on the top wall or bottom wall of the domain containing the PCM solid (PCM tank) afects its melting process. Indeed, studies carried out on this question have shown that for a domain heated from below, the melting front progresses in a planned manner and parallel to the heated wall due to the domination of conduction. ## **Thermal performance parameters for a PCM performing a phase transition**

A PCM can be characterized by its thermophysical properties, including its thermal conductivity, temperature of phase change, and latent heat of phase change. However, it should be noted that it is also characterized by its ability to achieve a phase transition in a short period (short transition time, *t*m), with a large amount of total energy stored, *E*, and with a high rate of transmitted heat (heat transfer rate, *q̇* <sup>m</sup> ≡ *q̇* PCM). Indeed, these latter parameters are essential and represent the main *criteria* for measuring the heat transfer performance of a PCM performing a phase transition (Fig. [24](#page-32-0)) and therefore of its choice for a particular application. In what follows, particular attention will be paid to these parameters.

*Remark 2* For the sake of simplicity without loss of generality, in the following subsections, attention is paid only to the heat transfer performance parameters of the melting process.

#### **Duration time or time for storage (melting time)**

The melting process duration of a particular PCM refers to the time required to transition from its solid phase to its liquid phase during the process. It depends on the shape, size, or volume of the PCM domain, the thermal conditions imposed on the domain, and the amount of PCM in the domain [[166](#page-55-16)]. To estimate the melting duration (melting time), many studies have been carried out. These studies made it possible to develop correlations based on the Fourier number (Fo) as a function of the Stefan number (Ste) and (i) the Rayleigh number (Ra), (ii) the Reynolds number (Re), or (iii) the numbers Ra and Re, depending on the type of heat transfer that dominates the transition (melting) process (i.e., conduction, natural convection, forced convection, or mixed convection), as indicated in Table [5](#page-32-1).

### **Energy stored (amount of storage energy)**

In general, energy stored (heat stored) is the amount of energy absorbed by the PCM when it melts or released by the PCM when it solidifes as latent and sensible heat. To quantify the total energy stored (or total amount of storage energy) in the PCM during its melting, for example, three stages must be considered: (1) before, (2) during and (3) after the melting of the PCM, such as:

$$E = E_{S,i} + E_{L,m} + E_{S,f}$$
 (5)

where the subscripts *i*, *m* and *f*, respectively, refer to the stages "before," "during," and "after" the phase change, and *E* is the total energy stored in the PCM, *E*S,i, *E*S,f are the sensible heats stored before and after the phase change, respectively, and *EL*,*m* is the latent heat stored during the phase change.

### **Heat transfer rate (melting rate)**

The heat transfer rate to or from the PCM, respectively, refers to the amount of heat absorbed (stored) or released by the PCM per unit time [\[174,](#page-55-24) [175](#page-55-25)]. For example, for the melting process, the amount of heat stored by the PCM per unit time (i.e., the melting rate) is:

$$\dot{q}_{\rm m} = \frac{E}{t_{\rm m}} \tag{10}$$

Note that the melting rate *q̇* <sup>m</sup> ≡ *q̇* PCM is sometimes also called the average storage rate [[176](#page-55-26)].

## **Heat transfer enhancement techniques**

As stated before, the use of PCM in thermal storage systems ofers many advantages, such as high storage capacity, excellent heat transfer performance, and near-constant operating temperature. However, despite these advantages, it should be noted that the main disadvantage of PCMs is their low thermal conductivity, thermal difusivity, and heat capacity, which increases the storage time and reduces the energy storage efciency (i.e., stored energy) in the storage system. Consequently, to remedy these drawbacks and thus improve heat transfer during phase change, improvement methods have been recommended and used by diferent researchers as shown in Table [6](#page-37-0); these methods include adding diferent types of fns to the geometry of the storage unit (mainly to the PCM tank), combining the PCM with one or more highly conductive metal foams, dispersing highly thermally conductive nanoparticles in the PCM, and the application of external force felds to the system containing the PCM. These techniques can be applied alone or in combination; for details, see Table [6.](#page-37-0) It should be noted that the nanoparticles or metal foams should preferably contain high carbon nanoparticles (a considerable amount of carbon) to improve the thermal conductivity of the resulting "nano-enhanced PCM" composite.

## **Enhancement by incorporating fns into the PCM storage system**

### **Principle and discussion**

Improving heat transfer by incorporating (integrating) fns, on the heating (or cooling) side of the PCM in the storage tank, allows for better heat transfer to (or from) the thermal storage medium (PCM) during its intended phase transition. The integration of the fns depends on several parameters such as their length and width, their number and their position in the storage system. An example of fns integration, on the heating (or cooling) side of the PCM in a storage system, is shown schematically in Fig. [26](#page-47-0).

Through literature analysis (e.g., in Table [6](#page-37-0)), we fnd that increasing the number, length and width of fns accelerates both the melting process and the solidifcation process.

On the other hand, to simplify without losing generality, we limit the discussion to the enhancement efect of fns on the melting process. Indeed, the literature analysis showed also that as the fn or fns, horizontally installed to the PCM tank, move away from the upper wall of the storage system, the melting process slows down; which is a disadvantage, so it is recommended to install fns close to the top of the PCM. Furthermore, a fn or fns inclined toward the top of the PCM constitute the best solution to improve its melting. Examples illustrating such trends are given by experimental and numerical results due to Zhang et al. [\[180](#page-55-30)] and Khatra et al. [\[181\]](#page-55-31), respectively.

## **Enhancement by combining metal foams with PCM**

#### **Principle and discussion**

The combination of metal foams with PCM is one of the most applied and efective techniques to improve the thermal conductivity and overall heat transfer of the resulting mixture (foam-doped PCM). Indeed, the integration of metal foam promotes the predominance of heat transfer by conduction during the melting process [[182\]](#page-56-0). Generally, copper, aluminum, and nickel are the most common metals used to make metal foams. However due to its high thermal conductivity, copper is more adopted than other metals. However, in high-temperature PCM applications, low thermal conductivity nickel is most commonly used due to its higher melting temperature than copper. The use of aluminum which has a low density and low melting temperature compared to that of nickel, is limited in high-temperature PCM applications [[183\]](#page-56-1). A scheme of combining metal foam with PCM, for example, of an energy storage unit is shown in Fig. [27.](#page-47-1)

In fact, the integration (combination) of metal foam into PCM (base material) is afected by various parameters such as porosity, pore size, and the nature of the base material.

## **Enhancement by inclusion (dispersion) of highly conductive nanoparticles**

### **Principle and discussion**

Heat transfer from a PCM can be efectively enhanced by fabricating a composite, consisting of a dispersion of nanoparticles, with high thermal conductivity, and the base PCM to form what is called a nano-enhanced-PCM (Ne-PCM), as schematized in Fig. [28](#page-47-2).

The efect of nanoparticles on improving the thermal (heat transfer) performance of Ne-PCM depends on their nature (type of material composing these nanoparticles) and their volume fraction (i.e., porosity). Generally, copper (Cu), alumina (Al2O3), copper oxide (CuO), titanium dioxide (TiO2) and graphene are the most widely used nanoparticles. In this context, a review of the literature, consulted as part of this study (e.g., see Table [6\)](#page-37-0), showed that the melting phase change process can be improved by increasing the volume fraction of the nanoparticles. It also showed that the inclusion of Cu and Al2O3 nanoparticles improved the melting rate and therefore the heat stored (absorbed) in the compound PCM (Ne-PCM). However, it should be noted that this trend diminishes over time in the case of Al2O3 nanoparticles. While, in the case of the inclusion of copper nanoparticles, the enhancement presents irregular and intense variations during the frst seconds of the process; then stabilizes as mentioned, for example, in the results of Nitsas et al. [\[186](#page-56-27)].

On the other hand, literature analysis also revealed that the maximum and minimum latent heat released during the solidifcation process is obtained when including dispersions of TiO2 and Al2O3 nanocomposites, respectively. Furthermore, it showed that the highest and lowest improvement, in the thermal conductivity of the composite PCM, was attributed to the inclusion of graphene and TiO2 nanoparticles in the PCM, respectively. An example of a study describing these trends is that carried out by Aziz et al. [[187](#page-56-28)].

## **Enhancement by applying external force felds to the PCM system**

## **Principle and discussion**

The application of external felds, including electric feld, magnetic feld, ultrasound, or mechanical vibration, allows dynamic manipulation of the performance of the PCM system (e.g., LHS unit, see Fig. [30\)](#page-48-1) by adjusting the additional energy input, caused by the applied external agent, which leads to an improvement in the melting and/or solidifcation processes of PCM in the system. In this context, the analysis of the literature consulted as part of this study (e.g., see Table [6](#page-37-0)) showed that: the application of ultrasonic waves to the PCM system during the solidifcation process increases the probability of nucleation and thus reduces the induction time, which favors the process. Ultrasonics can also reduce the cooling energy required for primary nucleation and stabilize the energy stored (released) during solidifcation. On the other hand, the analysis revealed that applying a magnetic feld to the PCM system can reduce the solidifcation and melting time of PCM. However, applying a high-frequency electric feld to the PCM system during the PCM solidifcation process results in a large number of crystals; this also increases cycle capacity and ensures stable stored (released) energy.

Finally, it should be emphasized that external fields applied to the PCM system can also improve heat transfer during a phase change when this transition is dominated by the natural convection mode or by boiling, as, for example, reported by Wu et al. [[188\]](#page-56-29).

- PCMs are substances capable of absorbing or releasing a large amount of energy during their phase change. They are classifed according to their nature and properties into three categories: organic, inorganic, and eutectic. Each category is designed for specifc practical applications.
- PCMs are used in a variety of applications, including thermal storage of latent heat. On the other hand, they can be used for example: In the feld of buildings to regulate the temperature of the building by mixing the PCM with the building material or by encapsulating it in the bricks. HVAC systems can be integrated with these systems to reduce energy demand and improve the efciency of these systems. In electronics, PCMs can stabilize the temperature and improve the thermal control of electronic equipment by absorbing the intense heat generated during their operation. In the textile industry, PCMs can maintain a uniform temperature for the human body and improve thermal comfort if microencapsulated in the fabric. In the medical and biomedical felds, they facilitate the storage and transport of medical and biological products by maintaining a stable and adequate temperature. In agriculture and food, to maintain greenhouse temperatures, transportation and storage of products at appropriate levels. Battery temperature regulation in automotive manufacturing to improve thermal comfort of vehicles. Desalination consumes less energy and has higher thermal efciency.
- The melting process of a PCM (storage phase) is initially characterized by heat transfer dominated by conduction. Over time, the temperature in the system increases and melting progresses, heat transfer becomes dominated by convection which occurs in the molten PCM and promotes heat transfer therein. In contrast, the solidifcation process of a PCM follows the opposite behavior; in fact, the heat transfer takes place mainly by conduction due to the high thermal conductivity of the resulting solid PCM (formed during solidifcation) compared to the liquid PCM.
- The heat transfer performance during the phase change of a PCM is afected by several parameters such as the geometry and boundary conditions imposed on the PCM system, its orientation and the properties of the material (PCM).
- The use of a PCM characterized by high thermal conductivity, latent heat, or viscosity and a low melting point (temperature) accelerates both its melting and solidifcation by reducing their duration.
- The melting process can be accelerated, leading to an increase in stored energy, both by imposing a high

- value on the wall from which the process begins and by using a sufciently heated PCM melt (at an initial temperature sufciently high). On the other hand, the solidifcation process decelerates with the increase in the temperature imposed on the wall from which solidifcation begins and with the increase in the initial temperature of the PCM melt.
- The orientation of the PCM system (e.g., LHS unit) is crucial for the melting and solidifcation processes. We found that increasing the inclination angle of the PCM system decreases the melting rate; conversely, it accelerates the solidifcation process.
- PCM melting and solidifcation rates and therefore stored energy can be signifcantly improved: by (1) the incorporation (inclusion) of high thermal conductivity additives such as metal foams or nanoparticle dispersions in pure PCM, and/or (2) the installation of fns to the PCM system, and/or (3) the application of external felds to the PCM system considered (such as the LHS storage unit for example). In these contexts, the following points should be emphasized:
  - (a) Adjusting the number, height, length, and orientation of fns installed in the PCM system can signifcantly improve its storage efciency.
  - (b) Incorporation of low porosity, large pore metal foam into pure PCM reduces the time required for the phase change process, increasing its storage efciency.
  - (c) The inclusion of nanoparticle dispersions with high thermal conductivity into pure PCM, leading to nano-enhanced PCM with increased thermal conductivity and latent heat of phase change. As a result, the melting and/or solidifcation processes (of this nano-enhanced PCM (Ne-PCM)) lead to increased storage efciency.
  - (d) The application of external force felds, particularly the magnetic feld, can be used efectively in enhancing the phase change process; this is due to the Lorentz force [[238](#page-57-26)] which serves to stabilize the convection in the liquid phase [[102](#page-53-23)] which results in a reduction in the time of phase change processes which in turn leads to an improvement in the thermal energy storage efciency of the PCM. On the other hand, the application of an electric feld has benefcial efects on the rate of heat transfer in the PCM during its phase change, which results in a clear improvement in its thermal energy storage capacity. Finally, the application of ultrasonic waves can improve the solidifcation process by increasing the nucleation possibilities and reducing the induction time which is benefcial to the storage efciency of PCM.

## **Future research and perspectives**

This work, like others before it, is limited by the low range of PCMs examined, as well as the lack of experimental investigations and validations, particularly concerning PCM solidifcation and integration in current systems. Future studies should expand the scope of PCMs examined and validate theoretical models through testing while keeping these limits in mind. To develop a more thorough understanding of PCM performance under various settings, future studies should examine a wider range of PCMs. Additionally, investigating the efects of boundary conditions and geometric layouts will improve the suitability of PCMs in actual thermal energy systems. Innovative design techniques for raising the efectiveness of thermal energy storage technologies may result from these studies.
