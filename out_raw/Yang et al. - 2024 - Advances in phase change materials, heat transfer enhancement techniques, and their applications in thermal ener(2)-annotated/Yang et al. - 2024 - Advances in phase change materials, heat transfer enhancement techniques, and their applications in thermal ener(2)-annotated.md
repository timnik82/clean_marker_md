### ABSTRACT

In recent years, phase change materials (PCMs) have attracted considerable attention due to their potential to revolutionize thermal energy storage (TES) systems. Their high latent heat storage capacity and ability to store and release thermal energy at a constant temperature make them promising candidates for TES applications. However, challenges such as low thermal conductivity, supercooling, phase segregation, leakages, corrosions, and slow charging/discharging rates have prompted the development of various PCM modification techniques and heat transfer enhancement methods. This review provides an overview of recent advances in PCM technologies from 2010 to 2023. It encompasses the development of new PCMs with high thermal conductivity, shape stability, and durability, as well as exploring novel TES storage methods to enhance charge/discharge rates. The review comprehensively discusses different types of PCMs, including paraffins, non-paraffins, sugar alcohols, hydrated salts, molten salts, and metals. It aims to understand their unique characteristics, compare their costs, sustainability, and applicability, and delve into the associated phase transitions between states and eutectics. Furthermore, the review covers shape stabilization and encapsulation of PCM, along with the elaboration on applications and the effect of process parameters during modification. While shape stabilization is generally easier to produce and control, encapsulation can provide better protection and isolation to the PCM core. To implement PCM-based heat transfer systems effectively on a commercial scale, passive methods (e.g., fins and nanoparticles) and active methods (e.g., external fields and rotation) are also explored. Additionally, it elaborates on the pros, cons, and relevant applications of emerging heat transfer enhancement techniques for PCM technologies. The review highlights that various enhancement methods can be combined in a system to achieve optimal charging/discharging rates, ultimately aiming for phase transition congruency. Overall, this review serves as a valuable resource for researchers and engineers working in the field of PCM-based thermal energy storage.

TES encompasses three key categories: sensible heat, latent heat, and thermochemical energy. Sensible heat storage relies on temperature differences, where the specific heat capacity determines the storage capacity. However, a significant drawback of this method is the considerable volume required for containment, attributed to material expansion and heat dissipation to the surroundings [\[3\]](#page-34-0). In contrast, latent heat storage, also known as phase change materials (PCM), <span id="page-2-0"></span>exploits the heat absorbed or released during a material's phase transition. This approach offers advantages such as a high energy storage density (50–100 times larger than sensible heat) and reduced temperature fluctuations, resulting in minimized heat loss and container size [[4](#page-34-0)]. Thermochemical storage systems operate through reversible reactions, wherein heat input causes the dissociation of chemical compounds into separate products for storage, and the combination of these products releases heat. Governed by pressure and temperature, this TES mode boasts the highest energy density with no anticipated energy losses. Despite its conceptual potential, thermochemical storage remains insufficiently researched for commercialization [[5](#page-34-0)]. By adopting this approach, several SDG goals, specifically Affordable and Clean Energy (Goal 7), Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure (Goal 9), and Climate Action (Goal 13), can be effectively addressed and achieved.

PCM mainly stores energy during its phase transition, where heat is absorbed or released once it reaches the melting point. As the charging/ discharging phase is nearly isothermal, minimal temperature fluctuation contributes to significantly less heat loss and volumetric expansion of the storage medium. Despite liquid-gas PCM having the highest latent heat of fusion, solid-liquid PCM is more favourable due to its limited volume variation [\[6,7](#page-34-0)]. A eutectic mixture can be formed by combining two or more PCMs, whether organic or inorganic, which exhibit congruent melting and solidification behavior during the phase change process. The selection of PCMs is a meticulous process aimed at tailoring specific properties such as an appropriate melting range, high thermal conductivity, minimal supercooling, and more. However, it is noteworthy that eutectic formulations typically exhibit lower latent heat and specific heat capacities in comparison to their individual constituents [[8](#page-34-0)]. The distinctive feature of eutectics is the manifestation of a single sharp peak during the melting or cooling process, known as the eutectic point [[9](#page-34-0)], which is crucial for their practical application. To anticipate the eutectic point of mixtures, it is necessary to construct phase diagrams based on different compositions of PCMs. Fig. 1(c) illustrates the essential properties characterizing an optimal PCM, emphasizing its significance in eutectic formulations.

In integrating PCMs into thermal systems, a pivotal consideration revolves around carefully selecting PCMs with a melting point lower than the temperature of the heat source yet higher than the discharge temperature. This critical criterion ensures the effective operation of PCM across thermal cycles. During these cycles, heat is supplied to initiate the melting phase of the PCM, and the stored thermal energy is subsequently discharged when energy is required. This strategic choice in PCM characteristics facilitates the dynamic interplay between the material's phases, optimizing its performance within the thermal system. For high storage capacity, PCM with high latent heat of fusion is desired, though the efficiency of thermal cycles is governed by thermal conductivity [\[10](#page-34-0)]. To further demonstrate physical and chemical stability, the selected PCM should withstand repetitive cycles of heating and cooling [[11\]](#page-34-0). #### **2. Classification and synthesis of PCM**

#### *2.1. Organic PCM*

Organic PCMs are most commonly used due to their wide availability. They consist of long hydrocarbon chains and possess good chemical and thermal stability. Major benefits of organic PCM are small supercooling with negligible hysteresis, corrosion-free, favourable reversibility and non-toxic, but they suffer from low thermal conductivity and are flammable [[19,20\]](#page-34-0). Organic PCMs are further classified into paraffin and non-paraffin. They are usually employed for low to intermediate temperature applications, with a temperature range of − 70 ◦C to 200 ◦C [\[21,22](#page-34-0)].

#### *2.1.1. Paraffin*

Paraffins, classified as alkanes with the general formula CnH2n<sup>+</sup><sup>2</sup> (where n represents the number of carbon atoms), are derived as byproducts during the fractional distillation of petroleum. These hydrocarbons exhibit exceptional thermal stability below 500 ◦C, display chemical inertness, minimal vapor release, and lack phase separation tendencies [[21\]](#page-34-0). At room temperature, hydrocarbons with 5 *< n <* 17 are liquid paraffins, while heavier molecules with *n >* 17 exist as solid waxes [\[23](#page-34-0)]. Paraffins release large amounts of heat upon crystallization, with latent heat of 150 to 250 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , depending on the number of carbon atoms. Heavier molecules can induce stronger dipole attraction among the chain, indicating that the longer the chain, the higher the melting point [\(Table 1\)](#page-4-0). With *n* ≤ 20, even-numbered alkanes have higher ΔHfus than odd-numbered, as the former required more energy to disrupt the more efficient packing of chains. For alkanes with *n <* 20 (odd) and 22 ≤ *n* ≤ 36, there is a solid-solid transition, which causes reduced ΔHfus [\[22](#page-34-0)].

To improve thermal conductivity and stability, thermally conductive nanoparticles (NP) such as graphite, carbon nanoparticles, and graphene compounds can be added during synthesis [[25,26\]](#page-34-0). Sun et al. [\[25](#page-34-0)] dispersed nano graphite (NG) and nano coconut shell charcoal (NC) onto paraffin-based PCM to enhance its thermal conductivity and viscosity. Pure paraffin had latent heat of 188 J g<sup>−</sup> 1 but poor thermal conductivity of 0.16 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . With 0.06 wt% NG blended in, thermal conductivity increased by 25 %, up to 0.38 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , but latent heat was reduced by 9.41 %. For better dispersibility, oleic acid and Span 80 were used as surfactants to eliminate agglomeration. Pasupathi et al. [[26\]](#page-34-0) investigated doping SiO2 and CeO2 NP at equal mass ratios in paraffin-based PCM of melting points between 57 and 64 ◦C. Optimal hybrid-nano/ paraffin had 1.0 wt% NP, with fusion enthalpy of 133.7 kJ/kg (− 4.64 %) and enhanced thermal conductivity of 0.26 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> 1 (+44.44 %). With increasing NP doping, the samples had decreasing latent heat of fusion and crystallization but could suppress supercooling with an enhanced nucleation rate. Although thermal stability increases with additional doping, agglomeration was observed above 1.0 wt%. This caused a significant decrease in latent heat, with only minor enrichment in thermal conductivity.

#### *2.1.2. Non-paraffin*

Non-paraffins mainly contain fatty acids, esters and other bio-based PCM [[21\]](#page-34-0). Among them, fatty acids are the most popular and are considered renewable sources since they are produced by animals and plants. They naturally present as esters of fats and oils, particularly these six groups: caprylic, capric, lauric, myristic, palmitic and stearic acids. Fatty acids also possess superior surface tension, providing excellent retention to host material. Although they are biodegradable and nonflammable, fatty acids usually cost 2–3 times more than paraffins [[27\]](#page-34-0). They also contain additional functional groups, such as carbonyl, carboxylic acid, ester groups, which act as reactive sites, making them inferior in terms of chemical stability compared to paraffin of similar carbon numbers. To avoid competition with edible food sources, Okogeri and Stathopoulos [\[23](#page-34-0)] highlighted waste oils, such as animal fats, plant oils, cooking oils, and genetically modified oils, as feasible PCM

Highly saturated oils are also more desirable as unsaturated oils have double bonds which are prone to oxidation, affecting thermal stability with more thermal cycles. Kahwaji and Anne [[28\]](#page-34-0) discovered refined coconut oil can be feasible as a residential-scale PCM with melting temperature of around 24.5 ◦C. With good thermal stability of 200 cycles, it had a high fusion enthalpy of 105 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , but slight supercooling of 20 ◦C was observed. Shen et al. [\[29](#page-34-0)] analyzed a binary eutectic mixture of stearic acid and n-octadecane by melt blending at different mass ratios. The findings revealed that the incorporation of 5 wt% CNT led to a remarkable increase in thermal conductivity, reaching 0.47 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , representing a substantial 56.1 % increase. ### *2.1.3. Sugar alcohols*

Sugar alcohols received attention as intermediate temperature PCM for applications between 0 ◦C and 250 ◦C. Also known as "plastic crystals", they are polyhydric alcohols that behave like polymers, whereby increased chain length can increase phase change temperature and latent heat, although non-linearly. Sugar alcohols, also known as polyols, are an important class of natural and biosynthetic PCMs with a general formula HOCH2(CHOH)nCH2OH. A comprehensive compilation of common sugar alcohols has been detailed by Shao et al. in their research [[31\]](#page-34-0). Notably, sugar alcohols are the sole PCMs demonstrating polymorphism. This unique characteristic involves the formation of distinct crystal structures during the phase transition, particularly notable during large glass transitions. Nucleation agents or mechanical agitation are essential to trigger crystallization and reduce supercooling effects [\[32](#page-34-0)–34]. Interestingly, Zhou et al. found adding 5 wt% ethanol into a xylinol accelerates its crystallization from 23 days to 30 min [\[32](#page-34-0)].

Bolmatenkov et al. [[35\]](#page-34-0) found no melting in D-sorbitol due to low crystallization tendency, while D-sorbitol and myo-inositol melt at 101 ◦C and 165.5 ◦C, with fusion enthalpies of 177.3 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , 290.93 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> and 264.21 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . Notably, the thermophysical properties of sugar alcohols can vary depending on their polymorphic states. The mechanism of polymorphism is not yet well understood. Sole et al. [\[36](#page-34-0)] also found polymorphic changes and thermal hysteresis were observed after thermal cycling, showing a high tendency of sugar alcohols to oxidize.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is also emerging as a versatile polymertype PCM. The thermal conductivity of PEG hovers around 0.3 W m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> K<sup>−</sup> 1 while having broad melting temperatures from 0 to 70 ◦C, depending on its carbon number. Detailed work on thermal analysis of PEG from 1000 to 20,000 g mol<sup>−</sup> 1 was done by Kou et al. [[37\]](#page-34-0). Zhang et al. [[38\]](#page-34-0) impregnated PEG of 1000 g mol<sup>−</sup> 1 into mica using vacuum impregnation method. With 46.24 wt% mica, thermal conductivity increased by 136 % and the composite showed remarkable formstabilized properties. Li et al. [\[39](#page-34-0)] also used more than one type of polymer in a polymer-based composite PCM. In the study, a polyethylene glycol-based energy storage polymer (PGMA) was impregnated into the porous wood structure by a high-temperature immersion method, as shown in [Fig. 2\(](#page-5-0)a). The composite PCM had a large number of crystalline polymers with a neat arrangement of lattice and reduced thermal resistance. Therefore, it had an enhanced thermal conductivity of 0.32 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , around 3 times higher than just amorphous wood. This material also had the melting and solidification enthalpy of 25.12 J g<sup>−</sup> 1 and 31.59 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively. Overall, this material can potentially serve as good building insulation and greenhouse material as it has excellent thermal insulation performance at low temperatures.

Fu et al. [[40\]](#page-34-0) synthesized a thermosetting PEG-based PCM that undergoes solid-solid phase transition via curing reaction of isocyanate and hydroxy prepolymers. Upon curing, the crosslinking reaction resulted in smaller spherulite formed in the PEG morphology at the microscopic level, which increased overall crystallinity in the PCM. When heated, the crystalline state of PCMs changes into an amorphous state and vice versa ([Fig. 2\(](#page-5-0)b)). The cured PEG with 6000 g mol<sup>−</sup> 1 exhibited decreased latent heat of 102 J g<sup>−</sup> 1 with a slight drop in melting temperatures of around 50 ◦C but had good thermal stability of up to 100 cycles. This technique endowed flexible application capability with superior thermal stability and reliability without further modification.

### *2.2.1. Hydrated salts*

The exceptional properties of hydrated salts, including their high energy density, thermal conductivity, and non-flammability, have gained significant attention from researchers. These characteristics make hydrated salts particularly well-suited for various building applications. However, a downside is that hydrated salts tend to supercool due to their inherent poor nucleation properties. This issue becomes more pronounced with repeated melting and solidification cycles, resulting in a loss of energy storage material and decreased heat storage capacity. Purohit et al. [[42\]](#page-35-0) comprehensively reviewed common issues and modifications on hydrated salts. In hydrated salts, water molecules are attached to cations and anions of the salt structure through electrostatic attractions, establishing ion-dipole or hydrogen bonds [[41](#page-34-0)[,43,44](#page-35-0)]. Rather than a core phase change, the release or absorption of heat is achieved through the dehydration and hydration of salts. During the charging process, dehydration causes hydrated salts to lose water and crystallize as it absorbs the heat of melting. The salts absorb water upon cooling, becoming hydrated salts while releasing heat in an exothermic dissolution reaction. This phase change process is represented by the Eqs. (1)–(3) [[42\]](#page-35-0).

Dehydration : *AB*⋅*nH*2*O*→*AB* + n*H*2*O* (1)

Partial dehydration : *AB*⋅*nH*2*O*→*AB*⋅*mH*2*O* + (*n* − *m*)*H*2*O* (2)

Hydration : *AB* + *nH*2*O*→*AB*⋅*nH*2*O* (3)

Since the solubility of salts in water is the basis of energy storing/ discharging, congruency in melting behavior becomes the primary concern regarding hydrated salts. Phase separation of water and salts is apparent due to density difference [[42\]](#page-35-0). The formation of lower hydrates is unfavorable as it can result in broad melting peaks, ultimately reducing the effective energy density in PCMs. Thakkar et al. [\[45](#page-35-0)] addressed this issue by integrating 2 wt% SrCl2⋅6H2O as a nucleating agent and 5 wt% NaCl/KCl as a stabilizer into CaCl2⋅6H2O, effectively eliminating supercooling. Leveraging the similar crystal structure of CaCl2⋅6H2O as the nucleating agent, the epitaxy effect was utilized to facilitate crystal growth on the surface of the crystal unit cell. Simultaneously, the inclusion of the stabilizer also promoted congruent melting, preventing the formation of lower CaCl2 hydrates. Xie et al. [\[46\]](#page-35-0) also doped SrCl2•6H2O in CaCl2•6H2O alongside cesium tungsten bronze (CsxWO3) NP as light absorbent additives; this mixture is used as fillers in building glazing windows. The combination of these additives effectively controlled supercooling to a temperature below 0.61 ◦C. A slight drop in thermal conductivity was observed with CsxWO3 loading, possibly due to higher interfacial resistance and viscosity. Nevertheless, the composite demonstrated the ability to block approximately 90 % of near-infrared radiation while able to maintain indoor temperatures of 22–28 ◦C under 4 h of solar light irradiation.

Sodium acetate trihydrate (SAT) has also gained popularity with its small phase change expansion coefficient, good chemical stability and low cost [\[47](#page-35-0)–51]. However, SAT is known for its highly corrosive nature and tendency to supercool. To address these challenges, researchers have explored the incorporation of expanded graphite (EG) into SAT. The porous structure of EG provides sites for the formation of capillary bonding with SAT, leading to a congruent phase transition and weakening of intermolecular forces [[49\]](#page-35-0). Additionally, the thermal conductivity of SAT can be substantially enhanced through EG doping, with up to a 400 % increase achieved at a 10 wt% doping level [[49,50](#page-35-0)]. Besides EG, Zhao also explored various nucleating additives and metals (copper and aluminium alloy) to investigate the compatibility of materials. Optimum supercooling, reaching 4.6 ◦C, was achieved with 2 wt% disodium hydrogen phosphate dodecahydrate (DHPD). Additionally, to address corrosion, a metal oxide layer is formed at the solid-liquid boundary, effectively preventing further corrosion into the inner parts of the metal [[48\]](#page-35-0). Wang et al. [[47\]](#page-35-0) synthesized SAT with 1.5 wt% tetrasodium pyrophosphate decahydrate as a nucleating agent to minimize supercooling (to 4 ◦C). Adding 1.5 wt% polyacrylamide as a thickening agent reduced friction between liquid molecules, enhanced flocculation, and decreased phase separation. The resulting material exhibited a melting temperature of 58.1 ◦C, retaining 97 wt% of PCM with a substantial heat of fusion of 289 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> .

#### *2.2.2. Molten salts*

Molten salts have gained significant attention for applications within the temperature range of 200–1000 ◦C. Typically composed of chloride, nitrate, carbonate, sulfate, and other salt constituents, these materials exhibit robust ionic bonds within their structure. This characteristic imparts notable attributes such as high latent heat, thermal stability, efficient heat transfer in the liquid state, and minimal volumetric expansion during phase transition. These distinctive features render molten salts well-suited for deployment as TES media and HTF in concentrated solar power (CSP) plants. However, they have low thermal

conductivity and containers can be easily corroded, causing PCM leakage in long-term applications [\[52](#page-35-0)]. Some molten salts have melting points and latent heat, as shown in Table 2 [[53\]](#page-35-0). Among them, nitrate salts generally have the lowest melting point, followed by chlorides, carbonates and fluorides [[54\]](#page-35-0). Carefully selecting eutectic combinations can be advantageous in cascading thermal storage to maximize charge/ discharge capability in CSP plants [\[55](#page-35-0)]. Solar salt is currently the most commercialized eutectic molten salt category, having 60 wt% NaNO3 and 40 wt% KNO3. Solar salt melts at around 220 ◦C, with latent heat of 92.3 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , but also has a high degree of sub-cooling at 93.7 ◦C [\[56](#page-35-0)]. Nevertheless, salt combinations can be tuned to achieve the desired melting point and latent heat, though compatibility is fundamental to achieving a eutectic system. Common eutectic salts found include KNK [[57\]](#page-35-0), HITEC [\[58](#page-35-0)], HITEC XL [\[59](#page-35-0)], Therminols [[60,61\]](#page-35-0) and other binary/ ternary combinations. Inorganic salts, specifically metals and alloys, represent a noteworthy category of phase change materials (PCM) employed for various applications. #### *2.3. Comparison between PCMs*

The potential applications of PCM are diverse, presenting a challenge in selecting the optimal PCM for specific purposes. The decision-making process is particularly complex for applications requiring large quantities of PCM for extensive energy storage, as cost considerations become a significant challenge. Paraffin has been extensively researched in recent years, establishing it as a commercially valuable PCM. Non-paraffinic and sugar alcoholbased PCMs have been proposed as renewable alternatives, yet their use competes with resources for the food and biofuel industries. Due to their hydroxyl groups, sugar alcohols provide molecular structural flexibility, enabling a broader range of thermal and chemical property manipulations. Hydrated salts exchange energy through the dissolution or precipitation of salts with water. This process exacerbates the phase segregation issue commonly observed in PCMs, thereby emphasizing the importance of additives. Salts are hygroscopic, and molten salt-based PCM systems typically operate at medium to high temperatures, attributed to their strong ionic bonds and high latent heat. Eutectic mixtures are often used to tailor thermal properties for practical applications. Metal-based PCMs exhibit exceptional thermal properties regarding latent heat, conductivity, phase transition, and cyclicity, but their high cost, weight, and scarcity present challenges for scaling up.

### *3.1.3. Solid-liquid*

Most of the PCMs discussed in the literature review are solid-liquid PCMs. They can be further classified into different natures, such as organic, inorganic and eutectic, as discussed in Section 2. Before achieving melting point, solid-liquid behaves like sensible heat storage (SHS) material, where heat energy is absorbed/released with temperature change. At melting point, it continues to absorb or release the heat without significant changes in the temperature until the PCM fully changes to distinct liquid or solid phase. With high storage density and the ability to store much heat without major temperature changes, heat loss can be reduced while energy can be stored with lower operational cost, making it favourable compared to the other phase change mechanisms. Although solid-liquid PCMs have lower latent heat than liquidgas and solid-gas PCMs, their wide variety of selections offers a wide range of phase change temperatures to suit their applications. However, they generally have low thermal conductivity and liquid leakage issues. Adding nanoparticles and using porous materials to form SS-PCMs can solve these issues respectively [\[72](#page-35-0)–74].

### *3.1.4. Synergistic phase change*

In a study conducted by Chen et al. [[86\]](#page-35-0), a form stable or SSPCM was developed by integrating a polymeric solid-solid PCM with PEG. This material exhibits the remarkable capability of simultaneous solid-solid and solid-liquid phase transitions in both constituent components, a phenomenon referred to as synergistic phase change. Due to the occurrence of both phase changes, the latent heat of the synthesized material (131.9 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ) is 21.3 % higher than that of the solid-solid PCM only. However, the study reported a 30 wt% leakage of the PEG.

Cao et al. [\[87](#page-35-0)] also developed a synergistic phase change material of poly(hexadecyl acrylate)/graphene oxide (PHDA/GO), which has a 38.8 % enhancement of the latent heat compared to only PHDA. Next, the authors also developed a synergistic PCM of mono-methacrylate(m-PEGMA)/GO composite for thermoelectric (TEG) applications. This material has enhanced the latent heat by 18.2 % (177 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ) and thermal-to-electricity conversion efficiency by 61.3 % [\[88](#page-35-0)]. To date, there are only a few studies on synergistic phase change; more studies are required to accurately determine the pros and cons of this novel phase change mechanism.

# *3.2.1. Single phase change*

Phase change diagrams are important tools to understand the changes in the enthalpy of the materials as the temperature differs. Gulfam et al. [[89\]](#page-35-0) have reported the ideal phase change diagram of the paraffin waxes, which might also be applicable to other solid-liquid PCMs undergoing solid-solid and solid-liquid phase transitions. During the initial phase transition, certain paraffin waxes undergo crystallographic structural changes or solid-solid phase change, as shown by the small peak in the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves. There are 2 phases during the solid-solid phase transition, namely β-phase and α-phase. As the temperature reaches a point well below the melting point of the paraffin, the material will transit from the solid β-phase into the solid α-phase. This transition is also known as lower excitation state of the material. During α-phase, it was stated that only hexagonal and orthorhombic structures exist regardless of the carbon number. The next state is known as the mush state, comprising α-phase and a fraction of the liquid state. Once the endpoint of melting point is achieved, the solid-state is fully converted into a fully liquid state and this latent heat throughout the solid-liquid transition is known as heat of fusion. As the temperature increases, the liquid state material undergoes further sensible heat storage to store the heat energy. The ideal phase change diagram of the paraffin is shown in Fig. 5.

### **4. Shape-stabilized PCM (SSPCM)**

In SSPCM, a porous supporting material is used to serve as a means to store PCM. With the porous structure, PCMs are bound to their skeleton through intermolecular bonds; this enables the melted liquid PCM to hold on to the structure when releasing energy, resulting in a composite solid-solid type PCM. Also known as form-stable stable PCM, the SSPCM technique allows PCM applicability as TES in various fields, which will be discussed below.

SSPCM production is much cheaper and less complex than the encapsulation method. Different morphologies, such as amorphous particles, rounded particles or fibre, can be used as a medium to store SSPCM with its porous structure. Various supporting materials have been employed in preparing SSPCM, including carbon-based, clay-based, metallic-based and polymer-based materials. The extent of supercooling has also been reduced up to 37 %, while the composite PCM has high thermal and shape stability.

Furthermore, some micron-level polymer structures such as lowdensity polyethylene (LDPE), unsaturated polyester resins (UPR), styrene-butadienestyrene and polyprrole cannot adsorb the PCM well after long-term operation and led to the leakage problem. Nanoscale polymer frameworks have been suggested to resolve this issue of the current micron-level polymer structures [[145](#page-36-0)]. Yao et al. [[145](#page-36-0)] have synthesized an SSPCM based on a nanoscale resorcinol-formaldehyde (RF) framework to contain the polyethylene glycol (PEG) as the PCM (refer [Fig. 11\(](#page-12-0)c)). The adsorption intensity towards the PCM is proven to be excellent as the PEG leakage is lower than 0.31 wt% under long-term heating up to 120 ◦C. Besides that, the composite PCM also has a high thermal conductivity of 1.26 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> 1 and latent heat of 103.13 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . The temperature and temperature variation of the battery module with the composite PCM is also maintained below 42 ◦C and 2.3 ◦C, respectively.

Organic polymers can also be the skeleton frameworks for the hydrated salt PCM as they can prevent PCM leakage, supercooling and phase segregation. Zou et al. [[146](#page-36-0)] have developed an SSPCM based on a super absorbent polymer (SAP) as the support to contain the binary eutectic hydrated salt as PCM [\(Fig. 11\(](#page-12-0)d)). SAP is compatible with the hydrated salt, thus further mitigating the PCM leakage. At the same time, its low thermal conductivity has also made it compatible with the SSPCM for applications in building insulation. The developed SSPCM has a latent heat of 172.7 J g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , good thermal stability and reliability at 100 thermal cycles and low thermal conductivity as low as 0.474 W m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> .

For instance, Liu et al. [\[147\]](#page-36-0) have prepared a shape-stabilized PCM (SSPCM) with paraffin OP70, supported by ethylene-propylene-diene monomers and enhanced by 10 wt% EG. The synthesized composite has a tensile strength of 3.6 MPa, latent heat of 126.8 J g<sup>−</sup> 1 and improved thermal conductivity of 1.106 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . Rather than undergoing direct heat absorption, the composite was exposed to photothermal irradiation and then insulated with SiO2 aerogel to prevent heat loss. It was to be used for residential hot water supply. Cheng et al. [[148](#page-36-0)] have also synthesized a ternary composite PCM consisting of EG, olefin block copolymer and paraffin wax. The composite PCM had a stable shape, high thermal conductivity (19.3 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ) and electrical conductivity (1590.5 S m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ). It was well adapted to a battery thermal management system, which can maintain the temperature of the battery at 42.2 ◦C while the battery was discharged at 35 ◦C and a rate of 2 ◦C.

### *5.1.1. Mechanical techniques*

Spray drying involves drying, dehydration and adhesion during the formation of microcapsule shells. First, the PCM core and shell materials are mixed as feed, which can be a solution, emulsion, suspension or dispersion. It is then atomized into a heated chamber with co-current drying gas. The aqueous solvent is evaporated, and a water-soluble shell deposits on the PCM droplet surface. Capsules formed air-borne are then collected using a cyclone [\(Fig. 18](#page-19-0)(a)) [\[6,](#page-34-0)[169](#page-37-0)]. Due to its simple operation, it is rapid, low-cost and able to produce homogenous microcapsules with adjustable size, distribution, shape, porosity, density and chemical composition [\[175,](#page-37-0)[229](#page-38-0)]. Additionally, several factors should be considered for achieving optimal encapsulation, including atomization size, powder flowability, drying air flow rate, and the speed of the atomizer. Methaapanon et al. [\[177\]](#page-37-0) studied the microencapsulation of methyl palmitate and n-octadecane in silica shells via sol-gel emulsion and spray drying. M-PCM shows a hollow spherical structure due to the air pocket expansion at the center of particle when dried. On the other hand, Borreguero et al. [\[178\]](#page-37-0) encapsulated Rubitherm RT27 paraffin with polyethylene vinyl acetate (PEVA) shell. Though agglomeration can occur, the collected M-PCM are homogenous and have a defined spherical shape. The addition of 2 wt% carbon nanofibers enhanced mechanical strength and thermal conductivity. The high flexibility of polymer shell prevents leakage, even after 3000 thermal cycles between 10 and 40 ◦C. Spray drying is an established process with simple control operations, but some materials (both core PCM and shell) may not be compatible with the process conditions which are necessary for successful encapsulation. First, it is necessary to form a homogenous mixture before spray drying. Spraying and rapid drying at high

The pan coating is widely applied to synthesize macrocapsules (typically *>*600 mm), mostly used in pharmaceutical, food, fertilizer, cosmetics and biomedical industries. This method is especially useful for controlled release with slow shell degradation for increased core product transport capacity [[170](#page-37-0)[,229,231](#page-38-0),[232](#page-38-0)]. Solid particles are tumbled within a rotary drum, while a shell material is atomized and sprayed onto these particles for the purpose of coating. Additionally, agitators or blades may be employed to create a more consistent mixture. To ensure uniformity, warm air circulation is utilized, simultaneously drying the coating layer and eliminating excess solvent. Following this process, the capsules are heated for drying and then cooled to solidify, culminating in producing the final capsules [[233](#page-38-0)]. Wang et al. [\[181\]](#page-37-0) encapsulated shape stabilized PCM of n-octadecane and high-density polyethylene in calcium alginate using the pan coating method. Macrocapsules of 30–50 μm were formed, but adhesion properties between the wall and PCM were poor. With the addition of chromic acid as a modifier and glycerol as a plasticizer, the formed shell became more flexible, leak-proof and

Nguyen et al. [[231](#page-38-0)] established an empirical scale-up model of the pan-coating process, encapsulating urea particles with biodegradable polymers. Considering similarities in geometry, kinetics, dynamics and thermodynamics, one dimensionless number equation was established and results were obtained from linear regression. As compared to spray drying, pan coating is a slower process with its continuous tumbling and spraying to evenly encapsulate solid materials.

Similar to pan coating, fluidized bed coating [\(Fig. 18](#page-19-0)(c)) is mainly used to coat solid particles. In the reaction chamber, particles are kept suspended by hot flowing fluidizing gas (usually air or nitrogen) through a porous distributor from the bottom. Atomized coating ingredients are then sprayed onto the particles. The high velocity of the gas improves solid-gas heat transfer, quickly evaporating excess solvent and resulting in a thin and uniform layer of coating around the particles. Droplet atomization and drying conditions are key to avoiding particle agglomeration or granulation [\[169,](#page-37-0)[234,235\]](#page-38-0). Ushak et al. [\[182\]](#page-37-0) encapsulated bischofite and MgCl2•6H2O in acrylic polymer shells using chloroform as a solvent in a fluidization column. The compatibility of shell polymer and solvents was previously investigated. With atomizer flow set at 2 kg/h and 2 min of fluidization time, high microencapsulation efficiency of bischofite (~87 %) and MgCl2⋅6H2O (~92 %) were obtained, while melting temperature was also reduced. Paulo et al. [\[183\]](#page-37-0) encapsulated carnauba wax (CW) using several coating materials in a fluidized bed and studied the rheological and adhesive characteristics. The particles also showed bubbling fluidization behavior at minimum fluidization of 0.7 m s <sup>−</sup> 1 and 60 ◦C (softening point of CW particles). With the help of plasticizers, anti-tack agents and surfactants, sodium alginate and Eudragit® L30-D55 suspensions were found to coat the CW particles with a contact angle smaller than 58◦. McMurray et al. [\[184\]](#page-37-0) coated calcined aluminium beads with pyro‑carbon (intermediate) and SiC ceramic (outer) layers using a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition furnace. Following CO alongside argon was crucial in maintaining the oxidized state of Al2O3, since high temperatures (*>*1000 ◦C) were employed in the coating process. The presence of a dense carbon layer serves as a barrier to diffusion between Al2O3 and SiC materials. Additionally, a porous carbon layer can effectively prevent SiC cracking resulting from the thermal expansion of the core material. The fluidized bed coating technique offers several advantages, including highly uniform encapsulation efficiency. However, it should be noted that the operation of this process can be complex. Similar to pan coating, the fluidized bed coating process necessitates the use of solid PCM during encapsulation and is constrained by the phase transition temperature of the PCM.

In situ polymerization involves the addition of prepolymer solution into PCM ([Fig. 19\(](#page-20-0)a)). This pre-polymer solution is then carefully added to the PCM emulsion while maintaining continuous and rapid agitation. As a result, microcapsules are formed and exist as a colloidal suspension. Subsequently, these M-PCM undergo cooling, washing, and drying steps [\[169,175](#page-37-0)]. In one specific application, the in situ polymerization method was employed to encapsulate a eutectic mixture consisting of 75 % stearic acid and 25 % capric acid within a M-PCM. Interfacial polymerization takes advantage of polymerization of hydrophilic and lipophilic monomers occurring on PCM droplet surfaces ([Fig. 19\(](#page-20-0)b)). The sol-gel method is a versatile microencapsulation method, usually applied for inorganic shell materials such as SiO2 and TiO2 [[6](#page-34-0)]. Firstly, an emulsion is formed with PCM, emulsifier and immiscible solvent. In a separate container, shell precursors and alcohol are mixed to form a homogenous solution. Then, water is added to hydrolyze the precursors, breaking them into smaller molecules and forming a colloidal sol solution. Sol solution is added dropwise into the emulsion, initiating polycondensation of shell onto PCM droplets [\[244\]](#page-38-0). [\[211\]](#page-37-0) encapsulated stearic acid with SiO2 shell using triethoxymethylsilane (TEOS) via sol-gel technique. After encapsulation, melting temperature remained at 225.1 ◦C while thermal conductivity increased by 51 %, speeding up the charging and discharging process of salt. Though latent heat was reduced by 16–19 %, subcooling extent decreased by 10 %, offering better cycling stability. Basically, sol-gel technique break down (hydrolyse) large precursor molecules such as TEOS and TBOT into small sol molecules. These sol molecules, recombine around the PCM surface in a continuous network to form a gel-like shell. Upon shell formation, the M-PCM are washed, dried and harvested.

Compared to chemical techniques, physio-chemical techniques usually utilize the change in environmental conditions (pH, temperature, pressure, initiator addition) to start the encapsulation process. During the chemical or physio-chemical encapsulation process, a common solvent acts as the continuous phase to suspend the core PCM while ensuring complete shell formation.

# *5.2. Effect of process parameters in microencapsulation*

### *5.2.1. Core/shell weight ratio*

The shell is maintained to be adequately thin to maximize core PCM content and encapsulation efficiency while still being rigid enough to create a physical protective barrier for the PCM. Insufficient shell material can cause agglomeration, irregular spheres or incomplete encapsulation, while excess shell content can result in a more uniform but thicker shell layer [[242](#page-38-0)]. Compatibility and reaction of core/shell combinations varies from one to another, so it is pivotal to tailor this parameter. In a study conducted by Nikpourian et al. [[246](#page-38-0)], they created a core/shell structure consisting of n-triacontane (C30H62) and polyurethane (PU), varying the core weight content from 0.36 to 0.82. The reduction of shell monomers resulted in the formation of thinner shells, thereby enhancing encapsulation efficiency. When the core/shell ratio was at its highest, 0.82, fusion and crystallization enthalpies reached their maximum, significantly improving energy storage efficiency and capacity to 80.2 % and 97.5 %, respectively. *Ma* et al. [\[209\]](#page-37-0) also optimized core/shell weight ratio of TiO2-encapsulated paraffin M-PCM from 60/40, 50/50 to 40/60. At higher TBOT (TiO2 precursor) content, some precursors are directly self-condensed into TiO2 nanoparticles, decreasing the encapsulation ratio.

#### *5.2.2. Effect of surfactants*

Surfactants are surface active agents and are amphiphilic, typically having a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. The head interacts

**Fig. 22.** Schematic illustration of sol-gel microencapsulation using TEOS as SiO2 precursor [[245](#page-38-0)].

with aqueous media through dipole-dipole forces, while the tail interacts with the organic phase. Emulsifiers, stabilizers, and superfatting agents are common examples of surfactants. Encapsulation relies on surfactants to diminish the interfacial tension between two phases that don't naturally mix, leading to the formation of stable emulsions or dispersions predominantly consisting of one phase. These emulsions are commonly classified as either oil-in-water (O/W) or water-in-oil (W/O), with the latter representing the external or continuous phase. The addition of surfactants, which are chosen to be compatible, progressively lowers the surface tension of the particles until it reaches a critical point known as the critical micelle concentration (CMC). Further saturation of surfactant will not affect surface tension. At CMC, a thin layer of surfactant particles spontaneously assembles around core particles to form spherical micelles, preventing direct contact with droplets. Surfactants can be classified by charge: anionic, cationic, nonionic and amphoteric (anionic in acidic environment, cationic in alkali environment) [[173](#page-37-0)[,240,247](#page-38-0)]. Furthermore, each surfactant has a hydrophiliclipophilic balance (HLB) value, which measures the relative affinity of surfactant for aqueous/organic phase. A high HLB number (*>*10) indicates high water solubility and vice versa [\[247\]](#page-38-0). In emulsions, coemulsifiers play a crucial role in enhancing system stability by preventing the clustering of monomer droplets [\[248\]](#page-38-0). The choice of surfactant depends on the surface charges of both the core and solvent, as well as its solubility. For instance, Lee and Jo [\[249\]](#page-38-0) employed Span 80 as a nonionic surfactant to encapsulate solar salt within a silica shell. Span 80 not only stabilized the water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion but also provided a hydrophobic tail that acted as an anchoring site for the attachment of shell precursors. In another study, Shi et al. [\[250\]](#page-38-0) conducted experiments using sodium dodecyl sulfonate (SDS) and sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS) as anionic surfactants to encapsulate paraffin with calcium carbonate in an oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion. The utilization of SDBS during nanoparticle synthesis led to the production of smoother and larger nanoparticles. This phenomenon can be ascribed to the presence of a benzene ring situated between adjacent head groups within the molecular structure of SDBS.

### *5.2.3. Stirring speed*

During the encapsulation reaction, stirring speed is also key in controlling sizes and size distribution of capsules. Generally, higher stirring speed causes smaller droplets/particle formation in emulsion/dispersion systems, resulting in smaller M-PCM formed. This is attributed to the applied mechanical energy, which breaks up phases in the mixture. When stirring in emulsion systems, concentration gradients can be maintained at the oil-water interface, preventing coalescences [251–[253\]](#page-38-0). Lu et al. [[254](#page-38-0)] investigated the effects of varying both core/ shell ratio and stirring speed in polyurethane-encapsulated butyl stearate M-PCM preparation. Stirring speeds were adjusted in the emulsification stages from 3000 rpm, 4000 rpm, 5000 rpm and 6000 rpm, forming M-PCM with average diameters of 9.13, 5.85, 4.41 and 3.69 μm. At higher stirring speeds, particle size becomes more uniform, resulting in higher core content and smoother shell. However, the system temperature can also increase at higher stirring speeds, which may lead to adverse side reactions or change in equilibrium.

#### **6. Heat transfer enhancement in PCM systems**

The phase transition process is inherently self-limiting due to the increasing resistance in the solid-liquid interface [[268](#page-38-0)]. This can obstruct the realization of PCM applications, especially in heat exchangers. To overcome this limitation, fins and external fields can be introduced to improve overall heat transfer in the system without any modification to the PCM medium. Fins are considered a passive enhancement as they focus on geometric or surface modifications while implementing external fields, which is an active technique due to additional power requirements [[269\]](#page-38-0).

#### *6.1. Fins*

Utilizing fins or extended surfaces is a proven method to speed up the heat transfer rate by increasing both the surface area and thermal penetration with the PCM medium. However, thermal resistance to the heat flow also offers additional thermal resistance to the heat flow. Whichever is more significant would ultimately affect the heat transfer rate [\[270](#page-38-0)]. In a heat exchanger, fin numbers, fin size, fin angle, and shell-to-tube ratio are some design parameters that affect the natural convection of PCMs, which is observed by the melting/solidification time [\[271](#page-38-0)–273]. In deriving numerical expressions for fin efficiency, Mostafavi and Jain highlighted that thermal interaction between fins and PCM is not only convective heat transfer but also includes gravity effects, free convection in melted regions, multiple phase change fronts and temperature-dependent properties [\[270\]](#page-38-0). Increasing fin volume also decreases PCM volume, affecting storage capacity. Annular fins and longitudinal fins are most commonly researched, followed by the more novel foam-fins and pin fins [\[274\]](#page-38-0).

### *6.1.1. Annular and longitudinal fins*

Annular, spiral, or circular fins can be described as circular plates attached cross-sectionally along a supporting tube perpendicular to the flow direction. Longitudinal or radial fins run axially on the outer surface of the supporting tube, parallel to the flow axis. Both fins are popular attachments in a shell-and-tube heat exchanger. With HTF flowing one-directionally, fins can also offer thermal resistance, and many researchers also focus on varying diameter/length of fins across the center axial [[275,276\]](#page-38-0). # *6.1.4. Remarks on fins*

In practical applications, copper fins remain the predominant choice owing to their notable thermal conductivity of 398 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . This starkly contrasts many PCMs, whether in solid or liquid states, which typically exhibit thermal conductivities of *<*1 W m<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . The crucial advantage lies in the fact that heat transfer in a copper-PCM interaction surpasses that observed in a PCM-PCM interaction. This is attributed to the superior thermal conductivity of copper. Essentially, the incorporation of copper fins amplifies the specific surface area, ensuring a heightened exposure of the PCM to the fins and, consequently, enhancing the overall heat transfer efficiency of the system. However, it is imperative to acknowledge the influence of buoyant-driven Rayleigh-Benard convection when configuring the density of any fin arrangement, as fins may introduce thermal resistance. Recognizing this aspect becomes pivotal in preventing local heat accumulation and ensuring efficient heat dissipation within the TES unit.

The utilization of copper foam further elevates the system's overall heat transfer capabilities. While copper fins primarily enhance thermal penetration, the buoyant-driven Rayleigh-Benard convection effect becomes a noteworthy consideration when determining the density of any fin configuration. Despite their positive contributions to heat transfer, Fins can also introduce thermal resistance.

From another perspective, the design of fins can be based on the surface area of fins. To optimize the melting rate of fins, surface area of fins can be designed according to density of PCM (finless condition) in a TES system, directly proportional in horizontal TES and inversely proportional in vertical TES. This can increase heat transfer through conduction while mitigating obstruction of heat flow from Rayleigh-Benard convection. Assuming no fins configuration and one-directional heat flow, melting rate will be determined by the direction of heat source and the aforementioned upward convection force, causing an apparent density gradient of PCM. In other words, increasing fin surface area away from heat source and towards bottom of vessel can improve the heat transfer rate on each part of TES system equally, encouraging simultaneous melting of PCM [\(Fig. 32\)](#page-29-0).

External fields are an active type of heat transfer enhancement, requiring an additional power source to stimulate movements targeting the recipient of the fields. In this regard, external fields primarily focus on melting PCM or charging TES. A typical TES system can be enhanced through different types of dynamic forces from either ultrasonic, electric or magnetic fields. Subsequently, these dynamic forces have distinct

# *6.2.1. Ultrasonic field*

The ultrasonic field works similarly to vibrational motion by encouraging particle movement and breaking down the molecular structure of PCM. The acoustic streaming effect increases the flow velocity of liquid-PCM. The cavitation effect generates tiny bubbles that expand and contract through rarefaction and compression cycles. An instant temperature and pressure gradient is formed when the bubbles eventually implode, causing a cavitation jet. Both these effects work simultaneously to create turbulent flow in PCM, enhancing heat transfer [[297](#page-39-0),[298](#page-39-0)].

Rahmanian et al. [\[298\]](#page-39-0) investigated the effect of ultrasonic waves in a numerical study with varying transducer locations and power. In any case, melting was accelerated adjacent to the ultrasonic source. With constant power, an increased area of ultrasonic transmission surface led to larger volume of PCM affected by the waves, though with lower mean power. The best performing location was located away from HTF fluid inlet and lower parts of TES due to gravity effects, reaching up to ~75 % decrement in time for complete melting. However, Guo et al. [[299](#page-39-0)] elaborated that adding ultrasound can induce superposition of natural convection and acoustic flow effects at small liquid fractions. In initial melting phase, natural convection created flow from non-isothermal fluids, but ultrasonic vibration created a uniform temperature distribution. These two effects can work against each other, diminishing initial heat transfer rate.

Cui et al. [\[297\]](#page-39-0) investigated the combined effects of ultrasonic field coupled with TiO2 NP in heat transfer mechanism of PCM. Under ultrasonic vibration, acoustic streaming and cavitation effects effectively enhance heat conduction and convection, yielding increased heat transfer solid-liquid phase interface movement (Fig. 33). Thus, the melting rate increased with higher ultrasonic power and NP concentration. Since ultrasonic field has more significance in heat convection, the author suggested initiating ultrasonic action in the latter stages of melting to minimize energy consumption while maximizing TES storage efficiency.

Liu et al. [[300](#page-39-0)] highlighted the conversion of sonic energy to thermal energy by ultrasonic thermal effect while testing on PEG/Fe3O4-GO PCM. The 3D network structure showed high acoustic-thermal conversion, which can be multifunctional in buildings for heat insulation, noise mitigation, and superwave hyperthermia. Jegadheeswaran and Sundaramahalingam [\[301\]](#page-39-0) applied ultrasonication of 100 W on PCM with varying NP and activation periods. They found the acceleration of both melting and solidification processes, with higher NP concentrations, and the application of ultrasound assisted in distributing NP, preventing agglomeration. During solidification, the NPs also diffuse towards the solid-liquid interface, resulting in a particle-rich mixture at the interface, enhancing thermal conductivity at the diffusion boundary layer.

# *6.2.2. Electric field*

Rather than mechanical vibration, electric fields induce electric body forces, namely Coulomb, dielectrophoretic and electrostrictive forces, to generate electrohydrodynamic (EHD)-based fluid flow [\[302\]](#page-39-0). An electric field creates a region where charged particles (anions and cations) are always present; materials of either excellent or poor electric conductivity experience different forms of electric force through the repulsion of anions and cations. The resultant electric force stimulates particle movement for enhanced intermolecular heat transfer.

**Fig. 33.** Schematic diagrams of nanoparticles and ultrasonic field enhanced heat transfer. (a) TES unit, (b)-(d) acoustic streaming effect, and (e)-(g) cavitation effect [[297\]](#page-39-0).

Endigeri et al. [[303](#page-39-0)] studied the effects of unipolar charge injection from a circular wire electrode under the influence of an electric field using the finite volume framework of OpenFOAM®. With the wire centered in a square cavity, counter rotating flow cells symmetric about the horizontal center were created under the consequence of Coulomb charge repulsion as PCM melts (Fig. 34). This showed that the electroconvection effect is more pronounced in the liquid phase, which reduced melting time through assisted flow circulation.

Selvakumar et al. [[304](#page-39-0)] described the phenomenon as vortex generation. In the study, total charging time was reduced by over 80 % with an applied voltage of 10 kV. The role of EHD on dielectric-type PCM was further explored by Hassan and Cotton [[305](#page-39-0)] on paraffin wax under experimental conditions and simulation of electric field strength distribution. Higher voltage input increased electric field strength, increasing the Coulomb force (dominant force on solid dendrites). For DC experiments, solid dendrites' extraction is directed by induced electric force, which is manipulated by the direction of electric field line. For AC experiments, the resultant force acting on solid dendrites is constantly changing due to changes in electric field, resulting in an up/down oscillatory motion. Upon melting, the effect of electroconvection can dampened with the natural buoyant force of convection cells.

# *6.2.3. Magnetic field*

Although an electric circuit generates both magnetic and electric fields, magnetic fields focus on magnetohydrodynamics, which involves thermal control through Lorentz and Kelvin forces [\[306,307](#page-39-0)].

Fan et al. [\[308\]](#page-39-0) doped iron particles as magnetic NP and proposed a rotating magnetic field as a technique to accelerate heat transfer. The rotating magnetic field did not affect PCM alone but caused circumferential forced convection on samples with NP. The higher the rotation speed, the closer the solid-liquid interface is towards the center vertically. Fan et al. [\[309\]](#page-39-0) also designed a TES unit whereby magnetic particles were initially dispersed at the bottom of the container. The magnet was located at the top, moving up and down, causing the magnetic particles to move reciprocally by attraction. The resultant motion contributed to forced convection, decreasing melting time by 15.8 %. Kumar et al. [[310](#page-39-0)] applied a uniform magnetic field aligned with the horizontal axis to a copper-paraffin PCM system via a numerical study. Multiple wavy isotherms were developed at 2 wt% copper NP doping and magnetic field activation, forming many counter-rotating convection cells. As melting progresses, the convection cells converge to form a larger circulation zone ([Fig. 35](#page-31-0)). Interestingly, similar streamline pattern was also observed in activation of electric fields.

Yang et al. [[311](#page-39-0)] impregnated 1-tetradecanol and Fe3O4 NP into delignified balsa wood and subjected the SSPCM to magnetic fields. Higher Fe3O4 NP loading gave the structure stronger magnetic properties, increasing the saturation magnetization of SSPCM, making it more favourable for magnetocaloric effect. Under 30 min exposure to alternating magnetic field (35 kHz, 0.06 mT), sample temperature of 8 wt% NP doping increased by 8 ◦C from 20 ◦C to 28 ◦C. Li et al. [\[312\]](#page-39-0) also utilized magnetism of Fe3O4 NP to enhance solar-thermal energy storage with applied magnetic field. With increased temperature, the NP could move faster in a magnetized environment, thus improving solar-thermal conversion by 16.7 %.

### *6.2.4. Remarks on external fields*

External fields are more significant in convection rather than conduction. Heat transfer through ultrasonic waves is similar to that of vibrations. The regular flow velocity is accelerated through acoustic streaming and cavitation effects, while the jet-like stream is occasionally introduced when cavitation bubbles are popped. A more turbulent flow drastically enhances heat transfer in the solid-liquid interface.

Meanwhile, electric and magnetic fields are believed to work simultaneously as electromagnetic fields. When electricity is applied to a metal wire/electrode in TES unit, the wire/electrode becomes a temporary magnet. This creates a directional electric field, which induces a thrust force onto the receiving particles through Coulomb forces. Both electric and magnetic fields are present, but the recipient of these fields can differ. Lorentz force can be applied in both situations whereby the direction of force can be determined using Fleming's Left Hand Rule. However, permanent magnets can be applied outside of TES units, but it is a one-directional motion that may not be repeatable for both melting/ solidification phases. Nevertheless, each external field causes different effects to stimulate particle motion during melting/solification phase. Combining several types of external field can be possible in optimizing heat transfer. However, the energy consumption of an active-type heat transfer enhancement should be considered, as it can affect effective storage capacity.

### *6.3. Rotation*

The concept of a rotating heat exchanger is relatively new despite its simple mechanism. Its continuous rotational movement enhances heat transfer by thinning the thermal boundary layer and accelerating forced convection, resulting in a greater temperature gradient. This results in a more evenly distributed heat transfer in the overall TES system, which applies to both melting and solidification processes. Although there is an upper limit, the heat transfer rate can also be adjusted according to

energy supply/demand by tuning the rotational speeds of rotating units. Nevertheless, there are several types of rotation systems, namely storage rotation, rotating fins within PCM, and scraped systems [\[274\]](#page-38-0).

### *6.3.1. Storage rotation*

In the case of storage rotation, the PCM system is rotated about a fixed, centered HTF-carrying tube. Khosroshahi et al. [\[313\]](#page-39-0) simulated a 2D horizontal double pipe TES system to explore increment of heat transfer rate using storage rotation with varying rotational modes (refer Fig. 36(a)). A step-by-step rotation method was also used, whereby the storage was rotated at an angle and stopped before another rotation. Findings revealed that constant rotation of 4.5 to 36 rpm had a negligible effect on melting time due to lack of opportunity to form natural convection vortex fronts. Stop steps with 180◦ rotation was optimal in reducing melting time. Energy exchange was more intense between inner storage layers, causing a more uniform heat distribution.

Soltaniet al. [\[314\]](#page-39-0) introduced longitudinal fins and implemented a slow rotary motion in the TES system to enhance the efficiency of the charging and discharging processes. By incorporating four fins and rotating the system at a speed of 1 rpm, the melting and solidification times were significantly reduced by 73 % and 83 %, respectively. The rotation also mitigated the sedimentation, resulting in a more uniform melting process throughout the thermal cycles. Fig. 36(b) also shows the effects of rotation speed on melting with respect to time. Through experimental studies, Fathi and Mussa [[314](#page-39-0)] looked into storage rotation to solve non-uniformity in PCM melting, especially in bottom of containers. At the optimal 6 rpm, melting time was reduced from 11 h to 4.7 h, exhibiting a drastic decrease of 57 %. Despite the relatively low

energy consumption associated with the slow rotary movement, additional analysis is needed to quantify the effective storage capacity achieved through this rotational motion.

#### *6.3.2. Rotation of fins within PCM*

Instead of rotating the entire storage system, the rotation of fins offers a potential solution for enhanced convective heat transfer. However, it is crucial to ensure that the domain of the PCM being rotated is completely melted prior to activation. This precautionary measure is necessary to prevent any potential collision between the rotating fins and the solid PCM. [\[315\]](#page-39-0). Zheng et al. [\[315\]](#page-39-0) investigated fin numbers and rotational speed of longitudinal-finned tubes in PCM melting. The increased number of fins did increase the surface area for conduction, but PCM content dropped accordingly. Increased flow velocity in a circumferential pattern was developed as forced convection replaced natural convection. At 4 rpm, reduction of complete melting time reached 30.3 %, compared to static fins. Soltani et al. [\[316\]](#page-39-0) doped copper NP to understand its synergistic effects with rotating fins in a computational fluid-dynamics (CFD) model (Fig. 36(c)). Although enhancement of melting/solidification times improved proportionally with all three factors, the productivity of NP was found to be less significant than fin numbers and rotational speeds. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize energy storage capacity and melting/solidification times further.

Sedimefendigil and Oztop [[317](#page-39-0)] applied magnetic field coupled with three rotating tubes in the PCM-NP system. Without fins, the cylindrical tube can be activated earlier during melting, while its rotational speed varies to manipulate Reynolds number (*Re*). More intense rotations

# *6.3.3. Integration of scraping with rotation*

This mechanism accelerates solidification by constantly scraping off solid PCM from the cooling rotating HTF tube. By doing so, effective heat dissipation can be achieved through increased interaction between tube and liquid PCM. Maruoka et al. [[318](#page-39-0)] attached a fixed blade to constantly remove the solidified PCM layer from a rotating cylindrical tube (Fig. 37(a)). A high rpm of 500 increased the overall heat transfer coefficient to 2000 W m<sup>−</sup> 2 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , compared to 400 W m<sup>−</sup> 2 K<sup>−</sup> 1 of the norotation setting. This shows that the scraping mechanism can improve heat utilization and heat release rate.

Meanwhile, Tombrink, Bauer and Jockenhofer designed a novel rotating drum heat exchanger with a scraping mechanism [\[319](#page-39-0)–321]. Fig. 37(b) shows that a rotating drum was partially immersed into a charged liquid PCM, absorbing heat energy. Solid PCMs adhere to the drum surface upon solidification, which is then scraped off. In their numerical analysis, a complex transient model was developed, taking into account heat transfer of PCM, HTF and even ambient air to predict the thickness of solidified layer and heat flux density [[320\]](#page-39-0). The team then experimentally investigated feasibility of the design by different rotational modes. At higher rotational speeds, more PCM is scraped, leading to reduced heat transfer through solidified PCM and increased convective heat transfer at the bare surface [[319](#page-39-0)]. In another work, a scalable drum design was introduced by drilling multiple channels into the inner surface of the hollow drum (Fig. 37(c)). When scaled up, the channels effectively reduce pressure from build-up from water (HTF) evaporation and wall thickness. Design parameters such as dimensions, immersion depth and wall thickness for drum construction were discussed, with reference to the responding heat transfer. However, there is also heat loss to ambient air, and the removed solids may still have excess heat that has not been harvested. Regardless, this showcased the potential of rotating drum heat exchangers in many applications in the TES industry and expanded the flexibility of process steam supply for electricity generation [[321](#page-39-0)].

# *6.4. Remarks on heat transfer enhancement techniques*

Several passive and active methods were reviewed to overcome the limitation of low thermal conductivity in PCM [\(Table 10](#page-33-0)). Although nanoparticles are not discussed in this section, they are a passive method commonly employed by many researchers. A combination of techniques has often been used to increase heat transfer through promoted phase transition congruency in both melting/solidification processes. Active techniques can also act as control to store/release energy on demand.

# **7. Key challenges and future recommendations**

Recent advancements in thermal energy storage materials have placed increasing demands on the amount of phase change materials (PCMs) required to achieve desired energy storage capacity. This underscores the crucial role of cost-effectiveness in PCM development alongside desirable thermophysical properties. While many studies prioritize the development of novel composite PCMs with enhanced thermal properties, affordability and readily available materials are often overlooked. This poses a significant challenge for large-scale production and implementation.

Waste material valorization emerges as a promising approach for addressing the issues of material scarcity and high costs associated with traditional PCMs. By transforming waste materials into cost-effective

Several waste materials, including melted asbestos-containing waste (ACW), ceramics, steel slags, industrial salt waste, and blast furnace slags, have been highlighted as potential sources for PCM synthesis, aligning with the principles of a circular economy [\[322\]](#page-39-0).

Furthermore, it is essential to note that solid-liquid PCMs cannot operate independently and necessitate suitable containers or supporting mediums to facilitate efficient phase change. While shape stabilization and encapsulation have demonstrated significant potential, scaling up these processes can present challenges, particularly when multiple chemicals and control parameters are involved. Therefore, it is crucial to have a comprehensive understanding of the underlying chemistry. Future research should focus on scaling up potential eutectics or PCMs in larger batches by optimizing and controlling the parameters throughout the production processes. Although there is limited emphasis on the importance of parameter control in existing studies, modifying the key parameters, as demonstrated in this review, has the potential to further enhance the overall thermal energy storage performance of various PCMs. Beyond metal-based PCMs, the inherent limitation of small thermal conductivity in other types of pure PCMs restricts their practical use. Passive methods, including the incorporation of fins and nanoparticles, offer heat transfer enhancement but impose constraints on the PCM content in TES units. Conversely, active approaches, such as applying external fields and rotation, come at the cost of energy consumption. This dilemma creates a bottleneck where both modes decrease effective energy storage capacity, presenting a trade-off in heat transfer enhancement. Each method exhibits specific advantages that become more pronounced during distinct stages of the melting/solidification process. These methods are akin to individual puzzle pieces, each possessing unique strengths yet to be fully integrated into a comprehensive picture. Future research endeavors should explore the synergistic integration of diverse methods to enhance each stage of PCM in a TES system. For instance, employing vibration and fins during the melting stage while implementing rotation during solidification could offer a holistic approach. As evidenced in CSP systems, the integration of passive and active methods has shown promise in achieving an optimal trade-off for large-scale TES applications. However, before practical implementations, it is essential to thoroughly assess the optimization and economic aspects of these integrated approaches. Conducting a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis is crucial in determining the economic feasibility of such strategies.

The process of storing heat energy through phase transition in materials, while seemingly straightforward, presents inherent complexity due to its non-stationary heat transfer nature. The thermal performance of PCMs is influenced by a myriad of factors, including material selection, modification techniques, and process parameters. Consequently, using simulation and modeling software becomes imperative for predicting and comprehending phase change phenomena, thereby optimizing cost and time [[324](#page-39-0)]. Farahani et al. [[325](#page-39-0)] applied an artificial

intelligence (AI)-based prediction approach to explore the thermal performance of PCM-incorporated microchannel heat sinks. They developed an artificial neural network model, specifically the Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), which was trained with inputs such as Reynolds number, Darcy numbers, medium type, and porosity to predict the thermal resistance of the heat sinks. Another study by Bouguila et al. [[326](#page-39-0)] employed Multi-level Reliability-Based Design Optimization (RBDO) methods to determine the optimal pair of PCM and nanoparticles. Utilizing MATLAB and ANSYS Finite Element software, the author systematically compared combinations of four PCM types and two nanoparticle types, considering factors such as cost, heat flux, and phase change patterns. Nevertheless, the predictions derived from simulation models should be validated through experimental results to account for the assumptions inherent in the simulations. Moreover, a comprehensive economic analysis should also be conducted to provide insights into the practicality and cost-effectiveness of modified PCMs in diverse applications. For example, previous research has already shown the utility of different PCMs in concentrated solar power plants, utilizing tools like System Advisor Model (SAM) software for a detailed comparison [\[323\]](#page-39-0).

In addition, it is crucial to carefully assess the environmental implications associated with implementing these novel materials and techniques. While these advancements can potentially enhance overall thermal energy performance, their specific environmental impacts remain uncertain. For example, while using these novel materials and approaches may help reduce energy consumption during various applications, it is unclear how much energy is required for material extraction and different heat transfer enhancement techniques. To ensure sustainability, a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA) should be conducted. This assessment will provide valuable insights into the environmental impacts of these technologies and help guide decision-making towards more sustainable practices. This assessment will help evaluate the environmental footprint associated with the entire life cycle of these PCMs, from production to disposal, and guide decisions that minimize adverse effects on the environment.

### **8. Conclusion**

In conclusion, the field of thermal energy storage has witnessed a significant shift in focus towards the exploration of both organic and inorganic phase change materials (PCMs), with polymers emerging as a noteworthy addition in the past decade. The solid-liquid phase change mechanism remains a cornerstone in various thermal energy storage applications due to its versatility in accommodating a wide range of phase change temperatures and its established technological maturity. Notably, challenges such as low latent heat, thermal conductivity, and liquid leakage persist, but these drawbacks hold promise for mitigation through the integration of nanomaterials, the development of shapestabilized PCMs and microcapsules, as well as innovative heat transfer enhancement techniques. Supporting matrix is crucial in shapestabilized PCMs and the latest types, including clay-based, metalbased, carbon-based, and polymer-based supports, are discussed. <span id="page-34-0"></span>Encapsulation by mechanical, chemical and physio-chemical techniques has been investigated and compared. Meanwhile, an extensive analysis of process parameters in encapsulation, such as core/shell weight ratios, role of surfactants, and stirring speeds, are provided with support mechanisms to offer new research in understanding the complexity of encapsulation. Furthermore, the incorporation of various heat transfer enhancement techniques is illustrated. Types of fins, external fields and rotational mechanisms provide great thermal transport to facilitate uniformity in heat distribution in PCM-based TES systems. The transient melting/solidification processes are governed by conduction and convection mechanisms, ultimately affecting heat transfer.

Nevertheless, the potential of solid-liquid PCM expands the potential of TES in the efforts of sustainable energy development. The continued exploration and innovation in these areas hold promise for the advancement of PCM-based thermal energy storage technologies in the future.
