#### ABSTRACT

The integration of Phase Change Materials (PCMs) as Cold Thermal Energy Storage (CTES) components represents an important advancement in refrigeration system efficiency. These materials have demonstrated significant capabilities in storing and releasing thermal energy, leading to improved system performance and reduced energy consumption. Conventional PCMs such as water/ice, hydrated salts, and paraffin are commonly used in CTES applications due to their favorable thermal properties and/or cost-effectiveness. This review paper explores the benefits and performance of integrating conventional PCMs in various refrigeration systems. It thoroughly discusses the effects of PCM integration on energy consumption, temperature stabilization, storage product quality, and greenhouse gas emissions.

While conventional PCMs are widely used in CTES applications, there is growing interest in exploring sustainable alternatives. Bio-based PCMs, derived from biomass and bio-waste materials, have shown promise as thermal storage candidates. Although these materials have been extensively studied for building applications, their potential in CTES applications remains largely unexplored. This paper also provides a detailed evaluation of bio-based materials based on their phase change temperature and latent heat, assessing their suitability for use in CTES applications.

Through this extensive review, the paper provides insights into the current state and future potential of PCM technology in CTES, highlighting both the proven benefits of conventional PCMs in enhancing refrigeration system efficiency and the potential sustainability of bio-based PCMs.

CTES systems with PCM represent an innovative approach for efficient and sustainable cooling applications. This technology entails a cyclical process involving two sequential stages: charging and discharging. During the charging stage, the refrigeration unit absorbs heat from the PCM, causing it to transition from liquid to solid phase (solidification), and thus stores cold thermal energy as latent heat. Once the PCM is completely solidified, the refrigeration unit is not working, allowing the stored cold energy to be re-used and cool the refrigerated case by absorbing heat. This causes the PCM to reverse the process and transition back to the liquid phase (melting/discharging process). Through continuous repetition of these cycles, a stable temperature can be maintained inside the refrigeration chamber, even when the refrigeration unit is not operating [\[35,36](#page-35-0)]. Fig. 2 provides a schematic representation of the operating principle of PCM during the phase change process. The overall energy is determined by: sensible heat, which affects the material temperature without changing its phase; and latent heat, which represents the energy required to change the material's physical state from one phase to another, all while maintaining a constant temperature (*Tm*), assuming that the material is pure. While, for an impure material, this transition occurs over a temperature interval (*Tm*1, *Tm*2) [\[37](#page-35-0)–39]. The total amount of stored energy is formulated by the following formula [39–[41\]](#page-35-0).

*QPCM* = *QS*(*l*) + *QL* + *QS*(*s*) = *mcp*(*l*) ∫ *Tm*<sup>1</sup> *Tl dT* + *m* ∫ *Tm*<sup>2</sup> *Tm*<sup>1</sup> *dh* + *mcp*(*s*) ∫ *Ts Tm*<sup>2</sup> *dT* (1)

where *QS*(*s*) and *QS*(*l*) represent the sensible energy at solid and liquid states. While *QL* is the latent energy. In addition, *Ts*, *Tl*, *cp*(*s*), and *cp*(*l*), are temperature and specific heat of the material at solid and liquid states, respectively. The notation (*Tm*1, *Tm*2) represents the Phase Change Temperature (PCT).

[Fig.](#page-3-0) 3 gives an overall comparison of the thermal properties of PCMs categories: organic, inorganic, and eutectics, each evaluated based on different key properties. Each PCM classification performance is represented on a scale of 0 to 3 points, with 0 being the lowest score and 3 the highest. Organic PCMs consist of carbon chains and are further categorized into paraffin and non-paraffin. Paraffin PCMs are comprised of alkane chains (C*n*H2*n*<sup>+</sup>2) derived from petroleum [[42\]](#page-35-0). On the other hand, and as its name indicates, Non-Paraffinic PCMs constitute the rest of the organic PCMs. They are mostly derived from bio-sources materials, such as fatty acids, sugar alcohols, and fatty esters [[43](#page-35-0)]. Inorganic materials are classified into two subgroups: hydrated salt (Salt-*n*H2O) and metallic [\[44,45](#page-35-0)]. The third category is Eutectics materials, which are a mixture of organic and/or inorganic substances [\[46,47](#page-35-0)]. These materials are available at a wide range of temperatures and can be used for low-temperature applications such as cold chain and refrigeration sector in which a PCT varies from − 70 ◦C to 15 ◦C [\[46](#page-35-0)–48]; Medium temperature PCMs (20 ◦C to 80 ◦C) commonly used in building and electronic application [\[46](#page-35-0)–49]. Finally, the high-temperature PCMs characterized by a high melting temperature that is higher than 80 ◦C are generally used for high-temperature applications including solarthermal power plants [46–[48\]](#page-35-0).

The cost of final PCM products is influenced by several essential factors. The primary elements affecting the overall expense include the type (organic, inorganic, bio-based, etc.) and processing of raw materials, as well as encapsulation methods (macro, micro, or nanoencapsulation). Additionally, the volume purchased and associated shipping fees play significant roles in the final pricing. Among the organic materials, petroleum-based paraffin is generally considered an economical option [51–[53\]](#page-35-0), while high-purity laboratory-grade paraffin is more expensive [\[54](#page-35-0)]. Salts are recognized as the most cost-effective raw material for PCMs [\[55](#page-35-0)–57]. Despite this, the final price of commercial hydrated salts PCMs is considerably higher due to the added costs of production and processing [\[51](#page-35-0),[53](#page-35-0),58–[60\]](#page-35-0). Bio-based PCMs, which are increasingly gaining attention [\[54](#page-35-0)], provide a moderately priced alternative [61–[63\]](#page-35-0).

The encapsulation process significantly influences the final product cost. The price varies based on both the encapsulation material (plastic or metal-based) and the chosen technique: macroencapsulation (*>*1 mm), microencapsulation (0–1000 μm), or nanoencapsulation (0–1000 nm) [\[64,65](#page-35-0)] (Fig. 4). Macroencapsulation, the simplest method, involves containing PCMs in plastic (HDPE) or metal containers (aluminum, copper) and represents approximately 20 % of total costs [[55\]](#page-35-0). Microencapsulation, which involves coating PCM particles with polymer shells at the microscale, is more expensive, accounting for 50 % of the final product cost [[37,55,66\]](#page-35-0). While microencapsulation can increase PCM viscosity, researchers are exploring nanoencapsulation as a potential solution to this limitation [\[67](#page-35-0)].

While the cost and encapsulation methods of PCMs are important considerations for their production and commercialization, these materials also face significant technical challenges. Addressing these challenges is essential to improving their performance and expanding their practical applications. Numerous reviews have extensively covered the practical challenges associated with PCMs and the various strategies employed to mitigate these issues, more details on these matters can be found in the following papers [[17,](#page-34-0)[39,71](#page-35-0)–73]. Key challenges include low thermal conductivity, the supercooling effect, phase segregation, and the corrosiveness to encapsulation materials.

The low thermal conductivity of PCMs presents a significant challenge. Researchers have primarily studied two strategies to address this drawback [\[17](#page-34-0)[,37,42](#page-35-0),[72\]](#page-35-0). The first approach involves introducing high thermal conductivity nanoparticles, which can be either carbon-based or metal-based [[68,](#page-35-0)74–[86\]](#page-35-0). The second strategy focuses on enhancing the heat transfer surface area of the PCM through various techniques. These methods include: implementing structured fins, integrating heat pipes, utilizing porous materials such as metal or carbon foams, and employing advanced encapsulation technologies like micro- and nanoencapsulation [[37,66](#page-35-0)[,87](#page-36-0)–95].

Supercooling and phase segregation further complicates PCM performance by causing delays in latent heat release due to the metastable nature of liquid PCMs that solidify below their freezing temperature [[17](#page-34-0)[,96](#page-36-0)]. Inorganic PCMs, specifically hydrated salts are characterized by a high supercooling degree compared to the organic PCMs. These latter are characterized by less to no supercooling effect [[72](#page-35-0)[,96](#page-36-0)]. To mitigate these issues, nucleating agents have been employed to stabilize the phase change process and minimize supercooling and segregation [[17](#page-34-0)[,72](#page-35-0)[,97](#page-36-0)–99]. Research into various nucleating agents, including thickening agents [\[100,101](#page-36-0)], nanocomposites [\[102](#page-36-0)–107], porous materials [[88,89,108\]](#page-36-0), and microencapsulation [\[109\]](#page-36-0), has provided promising results.

Material compatibility between PCMs and their encapsulation presents another critical challenge. Studies highlight that while metals like aluminum, brass, copper, and steel exhibit varying degrees of compatibility for different salt hydrates PCM, stainless steel is the most suitable material for all types of PCMs, organic and inorganic [[17,](#page-34-0)[68](#page-35-0)[,110](#page-36-0)–112].

While the strategies above have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing PCM properties, they also introduce additional challenges. For instance, the addition of nanoparticles and thickening agents can lead to a reduction in latent heat capacity – due to the non-phase changing nature of nanoparticles [\[46](#page-35-0)[,113,114](#page-36-0)] – and increased viscosity, which hampers heat absorption and natural convection [[100](#page-36-0),[104,115\]](#page-36-0). Fig. 5 comprehensively analyzes the effect of different additives on the thermal conductivity and latent heat of different PCMs. It can be seen that the concentration and type of the additives are the key factors in enhancing or reducing thermal conductivity and latent heat, respectively. This insight is crucial for the targeted design and optimization of PCMs for specific applications, where a trade-off between enhancing thermal conductivity and keeping latent heat capacity may be required. More details are presented in [Table](#page-28-0) 1 in [Appendix](#page-28-0) A.

[Fig.](#page-5-0) 6 gives an overview of the type of PCMs available in the market for CTES (see [Table](#page-29-0) 2 in [Appendix](#page-29-0) B for more details). The distribution of data points shows that the majority of the commercial PCMs fall within the hydrated salts and paraffin. These PCM types have gained popularity due to their low melting point. The melting point of paraffin depends on the number of carbons in the carboxylic chain, ranging from − 52 ◦C to 5 ◦C. On the other hand, the melting point of salt solution PCMs decreases as a function of the concentration and type of salt. It ranges between − 86 ◦C and − 1.6 ◦C. In addition, these materials – paraffin and hydrated salts – exhibit sharp melting points and good latent heat that varies between 155 and 300 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for paraffin and 116–314 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for salt solutions [[39](#page-35-0)[,116,117](#page-36-0)]. However, it is important to note that they also have some drawbacks such as flammability, corrosiveness, high supercooling degree effect, and phase segregation issues [[39,72,](#page-35-0)[116](#page-36-0)]. It is worth noting that numerous research efforts have focused on water/ice as a PCM alternative, given its advantageous properties such as high latent heat capacity, cost-effectiveness, widespread availability, and environmental compatibility [\[21](#page-34-0)[,118](#page-36-0)–129].

In recent years, bio-based PCMs have attracted the attention of researchers as they offer a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to petroleum-based paraffin PCMs. These materials are derived from plant or animal sources, making them available and affordable. Moreover, they possess desirable characteristics such as nontoxicity, less flammability, thermal and chemical stability, biodegradability, and oxidation resistance [\[140,141\]](#page-37-0).

#### **2. Application and effect of PCM in refrigeration systems**

Many researches have focused on integrating PCMs in different refrigeration systems such as domestic and commercial refrigerators, cold chains, walk-in freezers, and warehouses. They can be incorporated into different parts of the refrigeration system: in outer walls as thermal insulators, as CTES systems, and as heat exchangers. Unfortunately, biobased PCMs have not yet gained significant traction to be used as CTES. Despite this, other varieties of PCMs have demonstrated their effectiveness.

This section presents an extensive analysis of the efficacy demonstrated by various strategies, highlighting the substantial benefits of incorporating PCMs into refrigeration systems. The integration of PCMs shows promising results in multiple key performance indicators, including the enhancement of coefficient of performance (COP), reduction in energy consumption, and mitigation of temperature fluctuations. [Table](#page-31-0) 3 in [Appendix](#page-31-0) C provides a summary of the findings reported in the various studies examined in this section.

### *2.1. Effect on energy consumption and COP*

# *2.1.1. PCMs as thermal insulation (as thermal shield)*

PCMs have been used as Thermal Energy Storage systems (TES) to enhance the thermal insulation of refrigeration systems [[146,147\]](#page-37-0). In this approach, PCMs act like a thermal shield, absorbing surrounding heat and storing it as latent heat through the process of changing from solid to liquid phase. This transition helps to minimize and shift the peak rate of heat transfer and temperature during the day, thereby reducing the power consumption of refrigeration systems. The PCM's optimal melting temperature for this approach should be close to the surrounding temperature.

Fioretti et al. [\[146\]](#page-37-0) examined the impact of the paraffin layer (Commercial PCM; 35 ◦C) added to the external surfaces of a walk-in freezer as a thermal insulator as illustrated in Fig. 8(a). The objective was to minimize and shift the peak heat transfer. The experiment took place over two summer days under outdoor conditions. The results showed that the PCM acts as an energy storage system that absorbs and shifts the heat flow, effectively, enhancing the thermal insulation of the walk-in freezer. The addition of PCM resulted in a significant difference in peak temperatures between the PCM inner surface (denoted as RN5 in Fig. 8(b)) and the PCM outer surface (designated as RN4 in Fig. 8(b)). Specifically, the peak temperature at RN5 was found to be 12–14 ◦C lower than the peak temperature at RN4. Additionally, this peak was shifted by approximately 4–5 h due to the presence of PCM. They also compared the temperature value at RN5 with that of the external surface of the reference case (represented by R4 in Fig. 8(c)). The temperature in this case was 7–8 ◦C lower than the reference one. On the other hand, there was no significant difference between the internal air temperature of the reference case (at R5) and the PCM case (at RN6), as the refrigeration system effectively regulated and maintained a constant internal temperature. Fig. 8(d) provides a comparative analysis of the heat flux on the walk-in freezer wall equipped with phase change material (PCM) compared to the reference scenario for two distinct days. The results demonstrate a significant reduction in the heat flux peak as well as a delay in its occurrence when employing PCM. Specifically, the value of the peak heat flux was decreased by 5.55–8.57 % and shifted by 3.5–4.5 h relative to the reference case.

Copertaro et al. [\[147\]](#page-37-0) developed a numerical model investigating the energy behavior of a refrigerated container envelope incorporated with nine different PCMs laminated between steel and polyethylene panels to choose the suitable PCM for the Italian climate. Fig. 9 illustrates the evolution of the heat load within the container during 24 h. We can see that using PCMs reduces and shifts the peak heat gain. The PCM RT35HC (PCT 35 ◦C) is found to be most effective in the Italian climate. It offers significant benefits by reducing and shifting the heat load peak by 20–25 % and 2–3 h, respectively. As a result, the daily energy usage is reduced by 4.55–4.74 % compared to a standard container.

### *2.1.2. PCMs as cold thermal energy storage system*

Many studies have been conducted using PCMs to store cold energy when it is in excess and used when needed. This strategy can reduce energy usage according to two main operating approaches: *Full storage* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(a)), in which the refrigeration system is completely shut down and the cooling load is provided by the PCM. This shifts the energy consumption from on-peak to off-peak periods. The PCM is then charged during off-peak hours using a refrigeration system cooling load. *Partial storage*: In this case, the PCM provides a part of the cooling loads, and the rest is covered by the refrigeration system. Partial storage is further subdivided into two types: *Load leveling* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(b)) and *Load limiting* ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 10(c)). In load leveling, the refrigeration system is running all day, and the PCM provides a cooling load when the demand is greater than the refrigeration system output. The PCM can be charged when the demand is less. For the load limiting strategy, the PCM provides a part of cooling loads during on-peak hours and the rest is covered by the refrigeration system. The PCM is charged again during off-peak hours.

In this strategy, it is ideal that the material's PCT closely matches the refrigeration system set point. This temperature depends on the type of the stored product, for deep-frozen products like meat, a temperature around − 30 to − 25 ◦C would be suitable, while for fresh products such as tropical fruits a temperature around 8 to 15 ◦C would be appropriate [[72](#page-35-0)[,148\]](#page-37-0).

*2.1.2.1. Full-storage.* Studies have proven that the full-storage approach can reduce energy use by 18–86.7 % compared to the baseline, depending on the type of PCM used and the refrigeration system.

ASWB Engineering [\[149\]](#page-37-0) investigated the performance of two distinct walk-in freezers 1 and 2 integrated with PCM. The freezers were maintained at − 15 ◦C and − 19 ◦C, respectively. The primary objective of this investigation was to evaluate the feasibility of incorporating PCMs into these cold storage environments to optimize energy efficiency and reduce cooling costs. The PCM was installed above the storage rack in both walk-in freezers where the supply air fans were able to recharge the PCM. The results revealed that using PCMs can minimize the energy consumption for both facilities. Fig. 11(a–c) presents a comparative evaluation of the performance of the freezer 1 with and without PCM. The results demonstrate that incorporating PCM into the design significantly alters the freezing process. Specifically, it is observed that the inclusion of PCM shortens the duration of each refrigeration cycle compared to the base model, which operates for longer intervals between cycles, as shown in [Fig.](#page-9-0) 11(a). This reduced run time translates to fewer defrost cycles, resulting in a more consistent temperature profile ([Fig.](#page-9-0) 11(b, c)). Notably, this resulted in a remarkable 30 % reduction in total energy consumption. For the freezer 2 case, the PCMs were charged during the daytime relying on photovoltaic energy, and released the stored cold energy at night, enabling the refrigeration system to be entirely turned off for 10 h. As depicted in [Fig.](#page-9-0) 11(d), before the installation of the PCMs, the refrigeration unit operated continuously for 24 h (represented by the blue line), whereas afterward, it only ran for 10 h (represented by the red line). This innovative approach resulted in a remarkable decrease in daily energy consumption of 27.42 % and an annual reduction of 39 %.

PCMs have been applied in cold chain transportation including refrigerated containers. Tong et al. [\[30](#page-35-0)], applied 10 PCMs (Commercial PCM; 5 ◦C) plates to a refrigerated container. The PCMs plates are embedded with coiled tubes connected to the refrigeration unit, allowing heat transfer fluid (− 10.7 ◦C) to circulate during the charging process before transportation (as shown in Fig. 12(a)). Subsequently, the container was fully loaded with fresh fruits and vegetables and then transported for approximately 2500 km in 96.4 h. The energy consumption, operating costs, and CO2 emissions of the PCM-based container were compared to those of a diesel-powered refrigerated container to evaluate their performance during the same delivery process. The authors reported that by charging the PCMs only once, the container with PCMs could maintain the internal temperature stable for 94.6 h under outdoor conditions ranging from 13 to 35 ◦C. *2.1.2.2. Partial-storage.* Research focusing on the partial-storage approach has shown that it can reduce the energy usage of refrigeration systems by varying amounts, depending on the system and PCM types, ranging from 4.4 % [[150](#page-37-0)] to 77.5 % [\[118\]](#page-36-0). Hence, the COP was enhanced by 8 % to 60 % [\[151\]](#page-37-0).

Viking Cold Solutions, Inc. (commercial study) [[152](#page-37-0)], installed plastic containers filled with salt-water PCM on the ceiling next to the evaporator fans of a cold room. This position was chosen to ensure that the PCM plates could charge quickly and provide natural convection cooling when the compressor is off. The PCMs were fully charged during the night and released cold energy during the daytime, thus supplementing the refrigeration system and reducing the energy consumption of the walk-in freezer. During the 10-day study period, the results were favorable, with net consumption reduced by 18 % compared to the baseline. Power consumption during peak periods dropped from 6 kW to *<*1 kW representing an 85 % reduction. Correspondingly, the operation time was significantly shortened by 60 %, equating to a total saving of 6.5 h of energy consumption.

In another study, Viking Cold Solutions, Inc. [[153](#page-37-0)] implemented PCMs at the topmost rack of a frozen food storage facility. During the evening hours (off-peak period), the PCMs were charged by losing their latent heat to the refrigeration unit. Meanwhile, during the daytime (onpeak period), these materials are employed to partially substitute the refrigeration system and supplement the cooling process by absorbing heat generated from freezer infiltration and thus controlling temperature fluctuations. Fig. 13 represents the daily energy consumption of the warehouse without and with PCMs. The findings indicate that during the on-peak period, the inclusion of PCMs resulted in a partial reduction in energy consumption, which lasted for nearly 12 h. This led to a decrease of 35 % in the overall daily energy usage. Moreover, the integration of PCMs also significantly lowered the peak period energy consumption by approximately 43 %, indicating their potential to optimize energy efficiency in warehouses.

### *2.2. Effect on air temperature*

### *2.2.1. During normal operating conditions*

The studies agreed that PCMs have a substantial impact not only on the refrigeration system efficiency but also on the internal air temperature within these systems. Experimental and numerical research proved that PCMs act as supplementary evaporators, keeping the temperature as stable as possible at all times [\[119,](#page-36-0)[154,155\]](#page-37-0). This stabilization effectively reduces temperature fluctuations within the system, in contrast to conventional systems without PCM ([Fig.](#page-12-0) 14(a)). Additionally, PCM reduces the sudden temperature rise caused by door openings, and defrost periods [[120](#page-36-0),156–[162\]](#page-37-0) leading to fewer compressor on-off cycles and thus less energy consumption. This section assesses the impact of integrating PCM on the internal temperature and energy performance during typical operation.

Principi et al. [\[163\]](#page-37-0) placed a PCM heat exchanger with 6 containers filled with paraffin (Commercial PCM) and was placed next to the evaporator fans of the same cold room as used in the previous study [[146](#page-37-0)]. The material was chosen with a PCT close to the operating temperature of the cold room, set at 5 ◦C. The experimental results show – as [Fig.](#page-12-0) 14(b) illustrates – that during the compressor off time, PCM acted as an evaporator absorbing heat gain infiltrating from outside sources through the walls of the cold room, thereby avoiding rapid temperature rise and reducing fluctuations. As a result, fewer compressor on-off cycles were achieved. Specifically, over 2 h, the number of compressor cycles decreased from 13 cycles for the reference case to just 6 cycles for with PCM case ([Fig.](#page-12-0) 14(c)). This has resulted in a 16 % reduction in electrical power consumption.

Visek in his work [[164](#page-37-0)], directly placed the PCM slab in a household refrigerator to optimize the energy consumption. The measurements were taken during 24 h of operation under ambient conditions. [Fig.](#page-12-0) 14 (d) illustrates the effect of the PCM on the internal temperature profile of the refrigerator. One can see that adding PCM has a favorable influence on reducing temperature fluctuations. This mitigation is achieved through prolonging the compressor off time. During this period, the PCM keeps cooling the compartment for 11.8 h. Consequently, the daily energy usage has been decreased from 197 Wh (Reference) to 186 Wh (With PCM), representing a reduction of 5.6 %.

Gado et al. [[165](#page-37-0)] developed a mathematical model of a mini cold storage room. The studied PCM properties were those of a commercial PCM (hydrated salts), which has a PCT of − 30 ◦C. The PCM plates were considered to be next to the evaporator fan. The internal temperature of the reference case fluctuates between − 29 ◦C and − 31 ◦C more frequently than the new case ([Fig.](#page-12-0) 14(e)). This phenomenon can be primarily attributed to the thermal energy storage capabilities of the PCM. The PCM functions as a supplementary evaporator, absorbing heat as it transitions from its solid to liquid state. This phase transition process contributes to a more stable thermal environment and significantly extends the compressor off-time from approximately 4 min to 24 min. As a direct result, the number of on-off cycles was significantly decreased by 82 %, from 256 cycles per day without the PCM to 46 cycles per day when adding the PCM [\(Fig.](#page-12-0) 14(f)). Consequently, this reduction is translated into a decrease in daily energy consumption (10.53 %).

### *2.2.2. During opening door, defrost cycle, and power outage*

The effectiveness of PCMs on internal temperature as well as energy consumption for different scenarios: normal operation (door is closed), door openings, defrosting, and electrical power loss were studied in

### [[157](#page-37-0),[158,160,161\]](#page-37-0) and is discussed below.

Gin et al. [\[157\]](#page-37-0) conducted an experimental study to assess the thermal and energy performance of a household refrigerator under various conditions (e.g., door opening and defrost cycle) throughout integrating PCMs. Ammonium chloride with a PCT of − 15.4 ◦C was used as PCM and placed against the inner walls of the refrigerator. The results indicate that incorporating PCMs slows the rapid increase in internal air temperature within the refrigerator during compressor-off periods for both test scenarios. During the defrost phase (illustrated in [Fig.](#page-13-0) 15(a)), the internal air temperature was found to be 11 ◦C lower in the novel refrigerator compared to the reference case. Furthermore, the defrost heating system runtime was observed to be shorter in the first scenario, lasting only 26 min compared to 28.5 min in the conventional case. The effect of PCM on the internal and product temperatures during power loss is illustrated in [Fig.](#page-13-0) 15(b). The air temperature reaches − 3 ◦C in only 4.4 h when no PCM is implemented, while it took 11 h to reach the same temperature for the new refrigerator. In terms of energy consumption, the authors reported that defrost and door openings increase power consumption by 15–21 % and 11–17 %, respectively. However, upon incorporating PCMs, the compressor was observed to run less frequently than in the standard freezer, leading to an 8 % and 7 % reduction in energy consumption during the defrost cycle and door opening tests, respectively.

Oro´ et al. [\[160\]](#page-37-0) evaluated the impact of a PCM container on the thermal performance of freezers during door-opening and power outage scenarios. A sodium nitrate-based PCM (commercial PCM) characterized by a PCT of − 22 ◦C was encapsulated in a stainless-steel plate and placed inside the cabinet. A series of door openings of 30 s each, were conducted to study the internal temperature behavior of the freezer with and without PCM. The results showed that during 3 h of power interruption test, the presence of PCM allows significant thermal stabilization effects. The PCM-equipped freezer maintained the internal temperature within a range of 4–6 ◦C, as illustrated in [Fig.](#page-13-0) 15(c). Moreover, the temperature of the stored products in the PCM-equipped freezer remained approximately 2 ◦C lower than those in the reference freezer, as depicted in [Fig.](#page-13-0) 15(d). In addition, the authors reported that the PCM could slow down the rise of internal temperature during door opening tests by an average of 3 ◦C [\(Fig.](#page-14-0) 16(a)). These findings underscore the PCM's efficacy in thermal regulation during power disruptions.

A study conducted by Khan and colleagues [[161](#page-37-0)] investigated the thermal performance of a domestic refrigerator enhanced with PCM panels under two critical scenarios: door opening events and power outages. The household refrigerator was integrated with organic PCM (with a PCT of 5 ◦C) encapsulated in a stainless-steel container. The experimental results demonstrated enhanced thermal stability in the PCM-equipped refrigerator prior to door openings, as illustrated in [Fig.](#page-14-0) 16(b). After opening the doors, the internal temperature in the PCMenhanced system peaked at approximately 9 ◦C, compared to 12 ◦C in the reference unit without PCM. During power outages, the PCM panels act as an additional evaporator and absorb heat inside the cabinet. This resulted in a temperature differential of 3 ◦C lower in the PCM-equipped unit compared to the reference case. [Fig.](#page-14-0) 16(c, d) illustrates the thermal profiles of stored food items. One can see that the presence of PCM panels contributed to significantly reduced temperature fluctuations of the products, ensuring a better quality of the products.

### *2.3. Effect of PCM's phase change temperature*

The PCT and latent heat capacity are crucial factors in selecting a PCM to ensure the desired food preservation temperature and the effectiveness of the adopted strategy.

If the PCMs are installed inside the refrigeration systems or on the evaporator, it is essential that their PCT align with the operational temperature range of the system, rather than being lower. This ensures that the PCM can effectively charge/discharge at the desired temperatures, thereby maintaining consistent temperature conditions. Alternatively, if the PCM is installed on the condenser, the PCT must correspond to the temperature range of the condenser to absorb heat efficiently and enhance the system's overall performance. Studies have proven that PCT has a positive influence on air temperature fluctuations within various refrigeration systems. They reported that the lower the PCT of the PCM, the lower the temperature fluctuations [\[119,121,122](#page-36-0),[147,151,159,166\]](#page-37-0).

[Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(a) illustrates the effect of PCT on the temperature within a refrigerated truck [[24\]](#page-34-0). The case with a lower PCM melting point continues to cool the cabinet for long periods. However, if the PCT is not selected correctly, it would have a negative effect on the energy consumption and COP of the system. [Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(b) presents the results of a study conducted by Azzouz et al. [\[151,162](#page-37-0)], in which they numerically evaluated the effect of the melting temperature of the PCM on the internal air temperature and the COP of a domestic refrigerator. The PCMs were placed behind the evaporator as shown in [Fig.](#page-14-0) 17(c). The results show that the temperature inside the system and its COP increase as the PCM's PCT increases. The decrease in COP is due to the compressor working harder to drop the internal temperature and achieve the freezing point of the PCM. Therefore, the PCM must be carefully selected to meet the required cooling loads.

#### *2.4. Effect on condensing/evaporating temperature*

Condenser and evaporator are heat exchangers that release heat from the refrigerant to the outdoors and absorb heat from the environment being refrigerated, respectively. PCMs have been successfully integrated as supplementary heat exchangers in refrigeration systems, functioning as condensers and evaporators. This novel strategy globally enhances heat transfer efficiency at the refrigeration system's main heat exchangers leading to lower condensing temperature, and a higher evaporation temperature, as well as less fluctuation [[117](#page-36-0),[121,122,](#page-36-0)[164](#page-37-0),[166](#page-37-0)]. The PCT of the PCMs used in this tactic is recommended to be close to the condensing and evaporation temperatures. In addition, this approach enhances the COP of the refrigeration system by a percentage ranging between 17.6 % to 27 % [\[121,122](#page-36-0),[166](#page-37-0)–168], as well as reducing energy consumption by 7 % to 21 % [\[122,](#page-36-0)[154](#page-37-0),[167,168\]](#page-37-0).

Sonnenrein et al. [[122](#page-36-0)] experimentally evaluated the influence of using PCMs on the thermal behavior and power consumption of household refrigerators. Four PCMs (water + aluminum encapsulation, paraffin + high-density polyethylene (HDP) encapsulation, paraffin + aluminum encapsulation, and copolymer PCM) were placed on the refrigerator condenser tubes. The impact of using PCMs on the refrigerator condenser temperature is shown in [Fig.](#page-15-0) 18(a). The condenser inlet, mid, and outlet temperatures were significantly reduced. The maximum condenser inlet temperature was reduced by 3 ◦C and 8 ◦C using water and copolymer compound, respectively. The maximum temperature at the center of the condenser was also reduced by 5 ◦C when using water as PCM and 10 ◦C for the copolymer compound, while, the condenser outlet temperature was reduced by 5 ◦C (water) and by 8 ◦C (copolymer). In addition, and compared to the reference refrigerator, the energy consumption was reduced by 3 %, 5 %, 7 %, and 10 % when using water, paraffin + HDP, paraffin + aluminum, and copolymer, respectively.

In other studies, Khan et al. [[121](#page-36-0)[,166\]](#page-37-0) incorporated three different PCMs (water, sodium chloride with temperatures of − 5 ◦C, and − 10 ◦C) into the evaporator of a domestic refrigerator. The experimental results are represented in Fig. 18(b, c, d). It can be seen from Fig. 18(b, c) that the use of PCM effectively stabilizes the temperature fluctuations of both the internal and the evaporation temperatures leading to a reduction in the compressor run time (Fig. 18(d)). It was found that in comparison with the reference case, the compressor run time was reduced by about 2–36 % and 5–30 %, depending on the type of PCM and thermal load.

### *2.5. Effect of PCM thickness*

Ezan et al. [\[123\]](#page-37-0) developed a 3D CFD numerical model to investigate the influence of PCM slab thickness on the compressor run-time of a closed beverage cooler. Five PCM plates (water as the PCM; 2 to 10 mm thickness) were placed behind the evaporator of the cooler. The results were compared to the experimental measurements to validate the numerical model. The said predicts that a 2 mm thick PCM plate prevents the sudden temperature rise and keeps the air temperature inside the refrigerator almost constant at 3 ◦C, thus prolonging the compressor off period. [Fig.](#page-16-0) 19(a) summarizes the effect of PCM thickness on the compressor functioning behavior. One can see that as the PCM thickness increases the off-time increases, from 98 min to 494 min for 2 mm to 10 mm PCM. However, the compressor running time has increased to charge the PCM, from 47 min to 202 min. In addition, the first temperature drop takes more time as the thickness rises, from 80 min (2 mm) to 280 min (10 mm). The model predicted that the compressor runtime ratio decreases as the PCM thickness increases, from 36 % (no PCM) to 26 % (6 mm PCM slab), which is approximately 28 % less. However, beyond 6 mm thickness, the PCM slab led to a higher compressor runtime ratio.

For the same reason, Azzouz et al. [[151\]](#page-37-0) modeled the PCM on the evaporator of a domestic refrigerator. This work modeled three PCMs with thicknesses of 2.5, 3.5, and 5 mm. The refrigerator's operating time and autonomy as a function of thickness were evaluated. The authors reported that the system's relative running time decreases as the PCM thickness increases, reaching a gain of 28.5 % to 32.6 % for the 2.5 mm and 5 mm PCM panels, respectively [\(Fig.](#page-16-0) 19(b)). The figure also indicates that the system autonomy was 4.7–8.5 h when using 2.5–5 mm PCM, respectively.

In a comprehensive parametric investigation, He et al. [\[169](#page-37-0)] examined the influence of PCM volume fraction on energy consumption within a refrigerated warehouse. The numerical study employed an onshelf configuration, positioning PCM plates (hydrated salts; PCT − 19/ − 17 ◦C) atop warehouse shelves. The study compared two operational control scenarios: a temperature-based control (Case 1) and a temperature-price-based control (Case 2), both cases without PCM integration. [Fig.](#page-16-0) 19(c) illustrates the operational costs and percentage reductions for the refrigerated warehouse under these different conditions. Case 4 represented the PCM-integrated scenario, while Case 1 and Case 2 provided comparison points. Notably, the results revealed a significant relationship between PCM volume fraction and operational cost reduction. The analysis demonstrated that operational costs decreased substantially up to approximately 2 % PCM volume fraction, after which the improvements stabilized. In Case 4, the operational cost savings were remarkable — approximately 34 % lower compared to Case 1 and 22 % lower than Case 2. A similar trend emerged in electricity consumption, as shown in Fig. 19(d). Increasing PCM volume fractions led to noticeable consumption reductions, with improvements most pronounced up to around 2 %. At the highest PCM volume fraction (4 %), electricity consumption decreased by up to 15 %. These findings underscore the potential of PCMs when integrated with an energy management strategy for improving refrigerated warehouse energy efficiency. By enabling the storage and strategic utilization of cold energy during low-cost periods, PCMs offer a promising approach to optimizing energy performance and reducing operational expenses.

### *2.6. Effect of container's material and fins*

The choice of PCM container material plays a crucial role in improving the heat transfer in PCM. For this manner, Rahimi et al. [[170](#page-37-0)] conducted an experimental evaluation of the thermal and energy performance of a domestic refrigerator by using different encapsulation materials as well as incorporating fins. In these experiments, a metal container was filled and placed underneath the evaporator [\(Fig.](#page-17-0) 20(a)). [Fig.](#page-17-0) 20(b) shows the comparison between the internal temperature of the reference refrigerator and the novel refrigerator with copper, aluminum, and steel PCM containers. It can be seen that placing a Metal-PCM slab affects the internal temperature fluctuation and thus the compressor's on-off cycles. Specifically, copper-PCM container yields optimal results by reducing the compressor run time ratio which is defined as the ratio between On-time and the On-off cycle time [\[171\]](#page-37-0). As a result, energy usage has decreased by 7.33 %, 3.21 %, and 2.86 % for copper, aluminum, and steel containers respectively [\(Fig.](#page-17-0) 20(c)). Furthermore, the experimental investigation revealed that incorporating fins onto the PCM container (as depicted in [Fig.](#page-17-0) 20(d)) led to a significant reduction in energy consumption, with decreases of 4.37 % (Config. 1), 7.23 % (Config. 2), 9.56 % (Config. 3) compared to the copper-PCM configuration [\(Fig.](#page-17-0) 20(e)). These findings demonstrate that the thermal conductivity of PCMs plays a crucial role in their efficiency, as it directly impacts the heat transfer rate and, consequently, the charging/discharging time [\[43,72](#page-35-0)[,172\]](#page-37-0).

In the study by Zarajabad et al. [[124](#page-37-0)] both numerical and experimental investigations were conducted by placing PCM (water) on the evaporator of a domestic refrigerator. The PCM container was numerically modeled both with and without fins. The simulation results revealed that during periods of compressor downtime, the PCM can maintain the refrigerator temperature at the standard temperature (between 1 ◦C and 6 ◦C) for 30, 60, and 68 min for the no-fin, 1-fin, and 3-fin containers, respectively. In addition, the researchers found that the incorporation of fins enhanced the heat transfer within the PCM container as they increased the heat transfer surface area, leading to a shorter time required for the charging/discharging process. To validate the numerical model, the 3-fin container was experimentally tested in a domestic refrigerator. The experimental results showed that by using the 3-fin PCM container, the compressor running time was reduced by 45 min/day compared to the case without PCM. Specifically, it dropped from 4.32 h to 3.57 h. This corresponds to an energy savings of 17.4 %, as the new refrigerator (equipped with PCM) consumed 193.93 kWh⋅year<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , whereas the reference refrigerator consumed 234.72 kWh⋅year<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> .

### *2.7. Effect of the PCM position and orientation*

A series of experiments were conducted by Wang et al. [\[31](#page-35-0)–33] by installing different PCM-based heat exchangers in a refrigeration plant prototype. As shown in [Fig.](#page-18-0) 21(a) the Eutectics PCM heat exchangers were installed between the system's compressor and condenser (PCMA; 21 ◦C), the condenser and expansion valve (PCMB; 21 ◦C), and the evaporator and compressor (PCMC; 8 ◦C). The results showed that PCMA heat ex-changer acts like an extra condenser and decreases the temperature and pressure of the refrigerant before entering the condenser, thus, enhancing the COP of the refrigeration plant by 6 %. Using PCMB as a heat exchanger can improve the COP of the refrigeration system by 8 %. This gain is due to is due the decrease of the refrigerant temperature by the PCM (increases super-cooling) before entering the expansion valve. The PCMC has more benefits in terms of temperature stabilization and superheat reduction, factors that could potentially enhance the system's COP. However, this configuration also results in a decreased refrigerant pressure at the compressor inlet. This reduction effectively negates any COP improvements, ultimately rendering the PCMC configuration the least efficient one.

Marques et al. [\[125\]](#page-37-0) also conducted a CFD simulation of the previous

refrigerator configuration to investigate the temperature distribution inside the compartment. The PCM slab (water) was placed next to the evaporator in 3 different orientations: vertical, horizontal, and combined. [Fig.](#page-18-0) 21(b) shows a comparison of the predicted temperatures inside the refrigerator with the 3 PCM orientations. A clear difference can be observed in the temperature profiles of each configuration. Specifically, the vertical orientation leads to a non-uniform temperature profile characterized by a cooler region on the bottom and a warmer area top part of the refrigerator. The average temperature of this configuration was 11.2 ◦C. The horizontal orientation, on the other hand, leads to a more uniform temperature distribution, with an average temperature of around 8.8 ◦C. Notably, the combination of both orientations proved to be the most effective configuration, leading to a highly uniform temperature profile with an average temperature of approximately 6 ◦C.

### *2.8. Effect on stored products*

Refrigeration plays a crucial role in food preservation by providing a stable and cool environment to inhibit spoilage and extend shelf life. As previously discussed, incorporating PCMs into refrigeration systems helps to significantly reduce temperature fluctuations within the cabinet and regulate relative humidity levels, which are key factors affecting food quality [\[173,174](#page-37-0)]. Studies proved that PCMs are capable of minimizing ice crystal formation, reducing weight loss, and maintaining the texture and color of stored products [[30](#page-35-0)[,158,175](#page-37-0)].

Gin et al. [\[158\]](#page-37-0) used the same configuration as in [[157](#page-37-0)] and investigated the effect of PCM panels on the internal temperature and temperature of the stored products (meat and ice cream) during repeated power losses (3 h) every 24 h over 2 weeks. The results showed that the PCM-based freezer had lower fluctuations in air and product temperature than the no-PCM freezer, leading to better food preservation. [Fig.](#page-19-0) 22(a) represents the product temperature during a 3 h power loss. The PCM panels reduce the rate of temperature increase of the products, reaching − 3 ◦C for the reference case, and − 11 ◦C for the PCM-based freezer. [Fig.](#page-19-0) 22(b) represents the effect of PCM on the meat exudate; one can see that without PCMs, the meat loses its quality quicker than in the presence of the PCM. The authors reported that after two weeks of PCM use, the meat drip loss was reduced from 17 % to 10 %. They also evaluated ice cream crystal size, which is proportional to temperature fluctuation amplitude. Specifically, larger temperature fluctuations lead to greater crystal size and affect the overall texture of the ice cream [\[26](#page-35-0)]. With PCM, there was a reduction in crystal size from 70–80 μm to 40–50 μm.

The previously reviewed paper in [[30\]](#page-35-0) has also examined the effect of PCMs in preserving the quality of perishable goods. Specifically, the quality assessment of four types of produce leaf lettuce, strawberries, mangoes, and lettuce. As shown in [Fig.](#page-19-0) 22(c), visual assessment revealed no apparent deterioration or rot in the produce following transportation. In addition, the results revealed that the PCM container maintained a high internal relative humidity of approximately 80–90 %, which is optimal for preserving freshness. The qualitative inspection indicates minimal changes in key quality parameters across all products. Specifically, hardness values remained stable for leaf lettuce, lettuce, and strawberry, with a slight increase observed for mango (from 3.44 to 4.76). Soluble solid content, a critical indicator of fruit ripeness and flavor quality [[176](#page-37-0)], exhibited minimal changes, with the most notable decrease in mango (from 15.3 % to 14.3 %). pH levels remained consistent across all products, indicating maintenance of product acidity.

Rahimi-Khoigani et al. [[175](#page-37-0)] studied the effect of tetradecane as PCM (paraffin; 6 ◦C) on mushroom quality preservation during a fourday storage period. The study evaluated three packaging scenarios: a reference package without PCM (LHSB), a package incorporating PCM (LHSW), and a PCM-enhanced package with fins (LHSI). The experimental results, as depicted in [Fig.](#page-20-0) 23, demonstrated significant improvements in various quality parameters. Notably, the color index analysis [\(Fig.](#page-20-0) 23(a, b)) revealed less color changing in PCM-integrated packages, with LHSI packaging yielding optimal results. Moreover, a higher moisture loss rate was observed for the reference case than that observed for both PCM-base packages ([Fig.](#page-20-0) 23(c)). Specifically, mushrooms have lost 79 % of their weight when no PCM is used, compared to 17 %, and 13 % for LHSW, and LHSI cases, respectively. This observation underscores the efficacy of PCMs in regulating temperature and humidity. Additionally, texture analysis indicated that the mushroom softness increases over the storage duration, with LHSB maintaining the highest hardness value at 53.3 %, followed by LHSW at 47.2 %, while LHSI kept the softness at the optimal value at 33.6 % [\(Fig.](#page-20-0) 23(d)).

These findings collectively highlight that PCMs have a critical role in the preservation of perishable goods. PCMs have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in maintaining consistent temperatures, humidity regulation, and overall quality enhancement of stored products. This improvement in preservation directly contributes to the reduction of food waste, making the integration of PCM a promising technology for enhancing the efficiency and sustainability of the global food supply chain.

## *2.9. Effect on CO2 emissions*

Phase Change Materials have emerged as a promising technology with the potential to indirectly contribute to greenhouse gas reduction. As mentioned in the introduction electricity generation has been identified as the primary sector responsible for generating GHGs. Therefore, by reducing the energy usage of refrigeration systems through installing PCM, we can help mitigate GHG emissions [[30,34,50](#page-35-0),[124](#page-37-0)]. These GHG reductions are often calculated by multiplying the energy reduced by the electricity emission factor, which is defined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change as the amount of GHG emitted per unit of electricity generated. It ranges between 578 and 800 kgCO2⋅MWh<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [[34](#page-35-0)[,124,177](#page-37-0)]. Oro´ et al. [[34\]](#page-35-0) have estimated the CO2 emission reduction achieved through the use of PCMs in the refrigeration sector. The research focused on the European case, considering an emissions factor of 578 kgCO2 for every MWh consumed. The potential CO2 reduction obtained by the assessment is represented in [Fig.](#page-21-0) 24(a). Results show that installing PCMs can lead to a decrease in total annual CO2 emissions ranging from 15.6 ktCO2 (low estimation) to 75.4 ktCO2 (high estimation) corresponding to approximately 5 % to 22 %, respectively.

Chandran et al. [\[50](#page-35-0)] conducted a numerical study to assess the energy efficiency, economic benefits, and environmental impacts of incorporating PCMs in a refrigerated vehicle. The model predicted a significant decrease in GHG emissions. [Fig.](#page-21-0) 24 illustrates the annual CO2 emissions savings for different PCM types and with different masses. It can be seen that the GHG emissions of all PCM types decrease as the PCM mass increases from 45 kg to 105 kg. Among the PCMs evaluated, A28 PCM exhibited the most significant impact, reducing annual emissions from 1818 kg CO2 to 3060 kg CO2/year. While the minimum reduction was observed for PT28. Overall, this work highlights the potential of PCM integration to contribute significantly to GHG mitigation in the cold chain transportation sector.

While the benefits of PCMs in reducing electricity consumption are well recognized, further efforts should now focus on the materials used in these PCMs. It is crucial to move beyond petroleum-based products like paraffin and shift towards more sustainable options by exploring bio-based materials. Transitioning to bio-based PCMs offers additional environmental advantages, such as biodegradability and nonflammability. The following section delves into the various bio-based materials that have been identified as promising alternatives for cold thermal energy storage applications.

# **3. Bio-based PCM for cold thermal energy storage system**

Non-paraffin PCMs are mostly manufactured from bio-sources materials and used either directly in their raw form or after processing. These materials are classified into two categories: plant and animal sources. Many studies have examined the thermal properties of these materials, focusing primarily on substances from the fatty acids, esters, and sugar alcohols families ([Fig.](#page-21-0) 25). These materials are selected due to their advantageous properties, including biodegradability, less flammability, and non-toxicity [[140](#page-37-0),[141](#page-37-0)]. This section gives an overview of the bio-based materials that could be employed as PCM for CTES. [Table](#page-33-0) 4 in [Appendix](#page-33-0) D presents a summary of the thermophysical properties, including the melting point and latent heat, of the materials discussed in this review.

In this review, the selection and analysis of materials focus on specific criteria to ensure their suitability for CTES applications, as illustrated in [Fig.](#page-22-0) 26. The selection process was guided by several key criteria, with particular emphasis on bio-based materials as the primary consideration. These materials are further assessed for their compatibility with CTES requirements, particularly their phase change temperature (PCT), ensuring they exhibit low or sub-zero PCTs. Additionally, the latent heat capacity is evaluated for each selected material, this will give a first idea of the material's efficiency of thermal energy storage. Other essential properties, such as non-toxicity, are also considered to ensure safety and practical applicability. This approach aims to identify materials that combine environmental sustainability with optimal thermal performance for CTES applications.

### *3.1. Fatty acids and esters*

Fatty acids (FAs) and esters (also called fatty acid esters, FAEs) are organic materials, commonly derived from vegetable and animal fat [[62](#page-35-0)[,178\]](#page-37-0). FAs are a carboxyl group attached to a long hydrocarbon chain, which is represented by the formula A-COOH (where A is hydrocarbon chain CH3(CH2)*n*) [[179](#page-37-0)[,180\]](#page-38-0). While FAEs are compounds derived by the esterification of FAs with alcohol. See [Table](#page-33-0) 4 [Appendix](#page-33-0) D for details on thermal properties.

Knothe et al. [[181](#page-38-0)] have characterized the melting points of different FAs and FAEs using the differential scanning calorimetry technique (DSC). The analysis showed that the melting temperature of FAEs is appropriate to CTES, as their melting temperature decreases with the carbon atoms number in the carbon chain ([Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(a)). The FAE melting temperature ranges between − 45 ◦C (for the n-octane carbon chain) to − 4 ◦C (for the n-dodecane carbon chain) [[181](#page-38-0),[182](#page-38-0)]. Ravotti et al. [[183](#page-38-0)] studied the thermal characteristics of five commercial lactone esters. The DSC analysis revealed that among these esters, ε-caprolactone and γ-valerolactone exhibit appropriate melting temperatures for CTES applications. It has been observed that the phase transition for ε-caprolactone and γ-valerolactone starts around − 6.9 ◦C and − 40.3 ◦C, respectively. Further analysis revealed that the melting peaks of these compounds occur at temperatures of − 1.5 ◦C and − 32.1 ◦C, respectively. It is also important to note that the latent heat of these esters was found to be 121.7 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> and 109.8 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , respectively, which is lower compared to the latent heat of the salt solution PCMs. Triglycerides are identified as a category of esters, where three FAs are bonded glycerol molecules. Ravotti et al. [\[184\]](#page-38-0) have reviewed different triglycerides that can be employed as PCM in different applications. Among them, three compounds – α-tricaprylin, α-tripelargonin, and α-tricaprin – were the only compounds that characterized a sub-zero melting temperature. Specifically, the melting points of these materials were reported as − 54 ◦C, − 30 ◦C, and − 10 ◦C, along with corresponding latent heat values of 36 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , 69.4 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and 103 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> (see [Table](#page-33-0) 4). On the other hand, FAs are characterized by a positive melting temperature. This latter depends on the number of carbon within the hydrocarbon chain (as depicted in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(a)). It varies from 10 ◦C to 80 ◦C for 8 to 24 carbon atoms, respectively. Nevertheless, studies have investigated the properties of fatty acids containing carbon double bonds. These include myristoleic (C14:1 D9c) [\[181\]](#page-38-0), oleic acid [[190](#page-38-0)], linoleic [\[191\]](#page-38-0), and linolenic [[192\]](#page-38-0). The melting temperature of these substances was determined to be between − 4 ◦C and − 11 ◦C. Additionally, the latent energy values of these fatty acids were found to be between 45 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> and 115 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> (more detailed in [Table](#page-33-0) 4).

# *3.2. Edible and non-edible oils*

Vegetable oils are a composition of diverse fatty acids and triglycerides and have been employed as TES in many applications and for different temperature ranges (buildings applications; cold applications) [193–[196\]](#page-38-0). Soybean, canola, and corn oils are characterized by a low melting temperature of − 3.53 ◦C, − 20 ◦C, and − 24.6 ◦C, respectively, making these PCMs appropriate to CTES [\[197](#page-38-0)–199]. However, their latent heat is low, 47.4 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for soy oil, and 57.5 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for canola oil [[199](#page-38-0),[200](#page-38-0)]. Studies demonstrate that this limitation can be overcome by blending these oils with other substances to create a eutectic PCM with promising thermal properties. Studies show that eutectic mixtures based on these oils show promising thermal properties concerning both melting temperature and latent heat.

Rasta et al. [185–[187\]](#page-38-0) found that mixing corn or soy oils with tap water enhances this latter's thermal properties, allowing it to be employed as PCM for cold applications. The experimental results proved that soybean and corn oils act as nucleating agents, promoting quick water crystallization. This leads to a decrease in the supercooling degree by 6 K for 10 % oil (from 7.5 K to 1.1 K). In addition, the DSC analysis – represented in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(b) – shows that the melting temperature of the water-oil mixture decreases with oil concentration. For 10 % oil the melting point reaches − 7.5 ◦C and − 8 ◦C for corn and soy oil, respectively. However, for the same oil mass fraction, the latent has decreased by 42 % and 37 %, corresponding to 171.7 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and 185.9 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for corn, and soy oil, respectively.

In their work, Lorenzo et al. [\[188\]](#page-38-0), explored the development of a bio-PCM with a low melting temperature suitable for CTES applications. The PCMs were synthesized by blending canola oil and beeswax in different mass fractions (4 %, 6 %, 8 %, and 10 %). The DSC technique was used to determine the melting temperature and latent heat of the mixture. Results indicated that PCMs exhibited a high latent heat capacity, which increases as a function of the beeswax mass fraction. Specifically, they reported that the 10 % beeswax PCM was characterized by a melting temperature of − 13.6 ◦C. However, no specific details were provided for other PCMs concerning their melting temperatures. However, by analyzing [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(c) one can see that the melting peak is similar to that of 10 % beeswax.

Coffee ground waste valorization was investigated in a study by Jin Ong et al. [\[189\]](#page-38-0), where they extracted coffee waste oil (CWO) and used it as a sustainable PCM. The CWO was collected through extraction using three different solvents (acetone (A-CWO), ethanol (E-CWO), and hexane (H-CWO)). The chemical composition analysis showed that the oil contains 60–80 % fatty acids, including oleic acid, octadecanoic acid, and n-hexadecanoic acid, among others. The DSC analysis, presented in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 27(d), revealed that the CWO exhibits melting/freezing at temperatures of 5 ◦C and − 1.5 ◦C respectively, corresponding to a low latent heat value of 50 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> .

Olive mill waste could be valorized by extracting the olive oil, also known as secondary oil. It is estimated that for every ton of oil recovered, approximately 3 tons of waste are generated, including a solid waste known as pomace and a liquid waste called margins. Pomace wastes contain 5–13 % of oil [201–[203\]](#page-38-0), while margins contain 7–11 g⋅L<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [\[204](#page-38-0)–206]. Because of this significant percentage of oil, this waste could be used as a PCM. The process of melting occurs at temperatures ranging from − 4.5 ◦C to 10.4 ◦C, while freezing occurs between − 11 ◦C and − 8 ◦C. The enthalpies involved in these processes are measured at 105 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for melting and 97 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for solidification [\[207\]](#page-38-0). In addition, the secondary oil has a low thermal conductivity ranging from 0.15 to 0.17 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [\[208,209](#page-38-0)].

Another waste that can be recycled is date seeds that contain 4–13 % oil that can be extracted [210–[212\]](#page-38-0). The amount of oil in date seeds is determined by the kind of date and the extraction process. Ourradi et al. [[213](#page-38-0)], investigated the oil content of eight different dates seed types and reported that it ranges from 6 to 10 %. Date seed oil is characterized by a melting temperature of around − 3 ◦C. However, this oil has a low melting enthalpy, between 60 and 70 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [214–[216](#page-38-0)].

# *3.3. Animal wastes*

Fatty acids are compounds that can be derived from sources like chicken, pork, and fish wastes, these materials present promising opportunities as PCMs. While various animal wastes can serve as PCMs, our primary interest lies in materials suitable for low-temperature applications. Fish waste oils, in particular, demonstrate notable potential for cold storage, with melting points at − 70 ◦C for salmon oil, − 44.3 ◦C for catfish, and − 35 ◦C for Pollock oil. However, their low melting enthalpies – 40 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , 75.1 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , and 35 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> for salmon, catfish, and Pollock, respectively – limit their effectiveness for energy storage purposes [\[217](#page-38-0)–219]. Additional details on the thermal properties of fish waste oils can be found in [Table](#page-33-0) 4 [\(Appendix](#page-33-0) D).

Other animal wastes, such as pork and chicken waste, have been investigated as potential PCMs. These materials demonstrate promising thermal characteristics that make them attractive for various thermal management applications. Research has highlighted their effectiveness in systems such as building and photovoltaic temperature regulation [[220](#page-38-0),[221](#page-38-0)]. Notably, these fatty acid-based PCMs exhibit a PCT typically ranging between 25 ◦C and 55 ◦C. Additionally, their melting enthalpies have been reported to fall within a relatively narrow range of 155 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> to 180 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [220–[223\]](#page-38-0).

### *3.4. Amino acid*

Glycine, recognized as the smallest and simplest amino acid [[224](#page-38-0),[225](#page-38-0)], has emerged as a widely studied bio-based phase change material (PCM) due to its unique combination of favorable properties. The material's growing popularity in thermal energy storage applications can be attributed to two key factors: its cost-effectiveness and the remarkably high latent heat exhibited by its aqueous solutions [[226](#page-38-0)]. The thermal properties of glycine solutions have attracted considerable research attention, with multiple studies [\[226](#page-38-0)–229] evaluating their characteristics for potential applications in CTES.

Liu et al. [[226](#page-38-0)] studied the glycine-water-based (GW) and glycinewater-graphite-based (GWG) as PCM, with expanded graphene being incorporated to enhance the thermal conductivity of the base GW PCM. As shown in [Fig.](#page-23-0) 28(a), three different glycine concentrations (14 %, 16 %, and 18 %) were investigated, revealing distinct trends in both latent heat and phase transition temperature. The latent heat values exhibited a gradual decrease with increasing glycine concentration, from 246.30 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> at 14 % glycine to 241.50 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> at 16 %, and further declining to 233.33 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> at 18 % concentration. Similarly, the PCT showed a decrease from − 5.06 ◦C at 14 % glycine to − 5.12 ◦C at 16 %, reaching − 5.29 ◦C at 18 % concentration.

On the other hand, the GWG PCM is also characterized by a high

melting/freezing latent heat of 243.8/198.8 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . While its phase change temperatures were recorded at − 5 ◦C for melting and − 14.6 ◦C for freezing. In addition, the thermal conductivity of the mixture was enhanced by 216 % compared to that of GW-PCM.

Zhan et al. [\[228\]](#page-39-0) have developed a PCM based on glycine combined with a 1 % sodium chloride (NaCl) solution in varying concentrations to identify an optimal mixture. Among the tested formulations, the 5 % glycine–NaCl solution exhibited superior thermophysical properties, including a PCT of − 5.94 ◦C, and a latent heat of 291.15 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . [Fig.](#page-23-0) 28- (b) gives an overview of the experimental results. Furthermore, the thermal reliability of the 5 % glycine-NaCl solution was evaluated through 50 thermal cycles. The results indicated minimal degradation, with the PCT decreasing by only 0.78 ◦C and latent heat by 15.36 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , confirming its excellent stability and suitability for repeated use.

### *3.5. Polyols*

Sugar alcohols, also known as polyols, are organic substances that can naturally be found in fruits and vegetables [\[230\]](#page-39-0). These materials are commonly used as sweeteners in food and pharmaceutical production. According to [[230](#page-39-0)], Gerd Hormansdorfer suggested that sugar alcohol be used as PCM for solar energy storage applications [[231](#page-39-0)]. These substances are known for their high melting point temperatures, which are between 18 ◦C (for glycerol) and 200 ◦C (for inositol). Furthermore, they are characterized by high latent heat values ranging between 159 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> to 350 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> [\[230,232](#page-39-0)]. However, research has shown that the polyols can decrease the melting temperature of a polyols-water mixture from 0 ◦C to − 114 ◦C [\[233](#page-39-0)], depending on the polyols concentration levels [[234,235\]](#page-39-0). [Fig.](#page-24-0) 29(a) gives an overview of the effect of different polyol concentrations on the water freezing temperature.

Uraji et al. [[234](#page-39-0)] have measured the freezing temperatures of different sugar solutions with different concentrations varying from 30 % to 60 %. The results showed that for sucrose, the freezing point decreased from − 2 ◦C at 30 % concentration to around − 13 ◦C at 60 % concentration. Corn syrup demonstrated a significant freezing point depression, with values ranging from − 6 ◦C at 30 % to − 23 ◦C at 60 %. More freezing temperatures for the rest of the solutions are summarized in [Table](#page-33-0) 4, [Appendix](#page-33-0) D. The study was focused solely on the analysis of freezing points and did not provide any information regarding the latent heat of the solutions.

Mulawarman et al. [\[236\]](#page-39-0) investigated the thermal properties of the water-glycerol solution and the potential of using it as PCM for lowtemperature applications. Glycerol (C3H8O3). The T-history analysis shows that glycerol acts as a nucleating agent and reduces supercooling in water. In addition, the results showed that 10 % and 20 % glycerol decreased the solution freezing point from 0 ◦C to − 5 ◦C and − 10 ◦C respectively.

Sorbitol aqueous solution was also investigated by Vikram [\[237\]](#page-39-0), in which thermal properties of deionized water with varying concentrations of sorbitol (0.5, 1, and 1.5 wt%) were examined using DSC. [Fig.](#page-24-0) 29 (b, c, d) displays the experimental results demonstrating that adding sorbitol barley changes the melting point (melting curve). On the other hand, it is noticeable from the exothermic curve (freezing curve) that the PCM exhibits a significant hysteresis effect, thereby expanding its melting temperature range. Furthermore, a careful examination of the freezing peak of the solution reveals a non-monotonic trend, wherein the former decreases upon decreasing concentrations of sorbitol content. The freezing occurs at temperatures of − 15.6 ◦C, − 16.1 ◦C, and − 18.1 ◦C for 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt% of sorbitol, respectively. However, there was also a decrease in the latent heat by approximately 13 % (269.1 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ), 21 % (244.7 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ), and 23.6 % (236.2 J⋅g<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ).

The aforementioned studies have demonstrated that numerous biobased materials exhibit a wide phase change temperature range. These bio-based materials can grouped as follows: **Unsuitable bio-based materials**: such as animal fats and coconut oil, do not have appropriate melting temperatures for cold storage applications, particularly in building-related refrigeration systems. **Suitable bio-based materials**: materials such as corn oil, soy oil, and fatty acids have suitable phase change temperatures for cold storage, but their low latent heat limits their use. On the other hand, esters and α-tricaprin, have both appropriate phase change temperatures and high latent heat, making them promising candidates for CTES. **Eutectic bio-based materials**: combining bio-based materials with other substances like water, salt solution or beeswax can enhance both the phase change temperature and latent heat, resulting in improved thermal properties suitable for CTES applications. Water is a commonly used component in formulating bio-eutectic PCMs. Studies indicate that incorporating bio-based materials into water-based PCMs can enhance their PCT depending on the concentration of the bio-based additives. However, this improvement is accompanied by a reduction in latent heat capacity. As illustrated in Fig. 30, the latent heat of eutectic bio-based PCMs is generally lower than that of pure water. To address this challenge, further research is needed to optimize the composition of these bio-eutectic mixtures, aiming to minimize the loss in latent heat capacity while maintaining or improving the desired thermal properties.

## *3.6. Practical implementation of bio-based PCMs for CTES*

Bio-based PCMs have been extensively studied in building applications. However, their use in CTES applications remains underexplored. Therefore, the current literature on this specific topic is quite limited, with only a handful of preliminary studies available [\[227,238](#page-39-0)–240]. These studies primarily focus on the integration of bio-based PCMs into cooler boxes, with a specific emphasis on evaluating their potential to extend the shelf life of perishable food products.

Liu et al. [[239](#page-39-0)] investigated three distinct storage scenarios for strawberries to evaluate the effect of glycine‑potassium sorbate-water PCM (designated as 6-PCM5) and water-based PCM on the strawberries quality compared to the reference scenario (Without PCM). In each scenario, the strawberries were stored in a cooler box and exposed to the ambient temperature (15 ◦C) for 32 h. The results showed that the first scenario involved the use of 6-PCM5 – which exhibits a PCT of approximately − 5.78 ◦C – was capable of maintaining the temperature within the box between 0.45 ◦C and 3 ◦C for 22.83 h. This scenario resulted in a minimal weight loss rate of 0.74 % and a rotting rate of 7.69 %, indicating an optimal preservation of fruit quality. The second scenario used ice as the cooling medium, yielding a slightly higher average temperature compared to 6-PCM5. Consequently, this scenario exhibited a higher weight loss rate of 0.88 % and a rotting rate of 17.65 %. This highlighted the limitations of ice-based PCM in maintaining ideal storage conditions. The third scenario, exhibits significant deterioration in strawberries quality, with a weight loss rate of 36.8 % and a rotting rate of 58.14 % after the 32-h storage period.

Liu et al. [\[240](#page-39-0)] have also evaluated adding a glycine-expandedgraphite-water mixture (G:EG:W) on the quality of strawberries under room temperature (15 ◦C). The experimental results showed that after 21 h of storage, the weight loss rates of the strawberries were observed to be 5.92 %, 1.03 %, and 0.01 %, for the reference scenario (without PCM), the scenario using ice as PCM, and the scenario using G:EG:W PCM, respectively. The author also reported that the rotting rate when using G:EG:W PCM was 10.11 % and 65.76 % lower than ice and reference scenarios. Measuring 11.63 %, 21.74 %, and 87.5 %, respectively.

Nicolalde et al. [\[238\]](#page-39-0) evaluated the potential application of avocado seed oil as a PCM. The authors conducted numerical analyses to evaluate various PCM distribution configurations within an insulated container, comparing the thermal performance of four 75 mL packages against an alternative arrangement consisting of two 75 mL packages plus one 150 mL package. The results indicated that the configuration using four 75 mL packages exhibited superior performance. In assessing the impact of this bio-based PCM on food temperature regulation, the results revealed enhanced cold temperature preservation compared to the control condition. The data showed that the bio-based PCM maintained lower temperatures for longer durations, reaching 12 ◦C approximately 2 h after the control case. Furthermore, following an 8-storage period, the internal air temperature of the container incorporating the PCM was observed to be 6 ◦C lower than the container without PCM. These results suggest promising applications for bio-based PCMs in temperaturecontrolled storage systems.

The recent research on bio-based PCMs demonstrates promising potential for sustainable food preservation applications, particularly in extending the shelf life of perishable products. Although the number of studies investigating bio-based PCMs for CTES applications remains notably limited, and the implementation of these materials is still in its early stages, the reviewed studies highlight significant improvements in maintaining product quality, with notably reduced weight loss and rotting rates compared to without PCM cases. These findings not only support the feasibility of bio-based PCMs but also indicate their potential role in advancing sustainable cold chain solutions.

### *3.7. Life Cycle Assessment*

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a widely recognized method for evaluating the environmental impact of materials and products across their entire lifecycle, from production to end-of-life, as shown in Fig. 31 [[241](#page-39-0)]. This methodology considers multiple factors such as human health, greenhouse gas emissions, energy consumption, etc. to assess a product's overall sustainability [\[144](#page-37-0)]. Applications of bio-based PCMs remain in the early stages for CTES. As a result, the environmental impacts of bio-based PCMs are challenging to evaluate due to the limited number of LCA studies available [[62\]](#page-35-0). Existing research predominantly focuses on the use of bio-based PCMs in building applications [\[62](#page-35-0),[144](#page-37-0)]. These studies demonstrate that incorporating PCMs in construction can offer more ecological benefits compared to conventional insulation materials [\[144\]](#page-37-0). However, the environmental footprint of PCMs varies significantly depending on their type.

For instance, Fabiani et al. [[242](#page-39-0)] reported that raw palm oil has a higher carbon footprint (2.772 kg CO2-eq) compared to paraffin (0.207 kg CO2-eq). Conversely, palm oil waste is more sustainable, with lower emissions (0.053 kg CO2-eq). Another study [\[243\]](#page-39-0) compared the environmental impact of used cooking oil-based PCMs (UCO-PCM) to commercial paraffin and found that UCO-PCM had a higher environmental impact. This was attributed to its lower latent heat, which requires larger quantities of UCO-PCM to achieve the same heat storage capacity as paraffin, consequently increasing encapsulation material requirements and overall environmental impact.

Noel ¨ et al. [\[244\]](#page-39-0) highlighted potential reductions of GHG emissions of dodecanoic fatty acid derived from palm oil for use in a domestic solar water heater. The study concluded that bio-based PCMs are a viable energy-saving solution, with a payback period of less than two years, and a potential greenhouse gas emission reduction of approximately 1600 kg CO2 annually for a typical American home. However, environmental considerations must account for associated challenges like potential deforestation in palm oil production.

A comparative study by Menoufi et al. [\[245\]](#page-39-0) evaluated the environmental impact of esters as PCMs compared to paraffin and hydrated salts. The analysis included scenarios using polyurethane foam alone, foam combined with PCM, and a reference case without PCM or foam. The results showed that adding polyurethane foam increased the environmental impact by 20 %. Incorporating PCMs into the foam led to environmental score increases of 6.1 %, 7.7 %, and 18.5 % for esters, hydrated salts, and paraffin, respectively. However, using esters and hydrated salts resulted in 10.5 % and 9.1 % lower impacts, respectively, compared to paraffin.

These studies emphasize the need for more comprehensive LCA studies particularly exploring bio-based PCM applications for CTES applications, to better understand their environmental trade-offs and optimize their sustainability.

### **4. Conclusion**

The integration of conventional PCMs in refrigeration systems has proven to be a promising approach to improving energy efficiency and environmental sustainability. This review has demonstrated that PCMs can be effectively incorporated into various refrigeration applications, ranging from domestic refrigerators to industrial cold storage facilities, through two main strategies: thermal insulation and cold energy storage systems. As a thermal insulation method, PCMs effectively reduce heat transfer through system walls, thereby decreasing the energy consumption required to maintain desired temperatures. The cold energy storage approach uses PCMs to store excess cooling capacity for later use, offering benefits during compressor off-cycles, door openings, defrosting, or power outages. Moreover, this method enables innovative energy management strategies, such as storing cold energy during lowcost electricity periods and utilizing alternative energy sources like photovoltaic power for PCM charging. The effectiveness of PCM integration critically depends on material selection, placement, and orientation. For example, placing PCMs near evaporator fans facilitates natural convection and promotes more uniform temperature distribution. Conversely, improper positioning can negatively impact air circulation and overall system efficiency.

Bio-based PCMs, derived from renewable plant and animal sources, have gained significant attention as eco-friendly and cost-effective alternatives to petroleum-based options. These materials offer a wide range of phase change temperatures, from temperatures suitable for building applications to low/subzero temperatures appropriate for CTES. However, some biomaterials are characterized by low latent heat capacity, which constrains their use. To address this challenge, researchers have investigated bio-eutectic compounds, which combine bio-based materials with supplementary substances, including water, salt solutions, or beeswax. These hybrid systems effectively enhance both phase change temperature and latent heat capacity, thereby optimizing PCM performance for CTES applications. Preliminary studies on bio-based PCM applications have demonstrated their capacity to provide sustained cold energy and maintain stable air temperatures, suggesting potential benefits for preserving product quality during storage, particularly for temperature-sensitive perishable goods.

While the current review highlights the promising thermal characteristics of bio-based materials for CTES, several critical research directions merit further investigation to advance the field:

– Performance evaluation of bio-based PCMs: future research should comprehensively assess the potential of bio-based PCMs in CTES. Beyond current applications in cooler boxes and product preservation, systematic studies are needed to evaluate their impact on energy consumption and overall system performance. Comparative analyses with conventional refrigeration technologies would provide valuable insights into the practical viability of these sustainable alternatives.

- Optimization of thermal properties: continued optimization of thermal characteristics remains crucial. Researchers should concentrate on improving key parameters such as melting temperature, latent heat capacity, and thermal conductivity. These enhancements are essential to meet the specific and demanding requirements for CTES applications.
- Advanced encapsulation techniques: research should explore innovative encapsulation methods, such as microencapsulation and nanoencapsulation. These methods can potentially improve heat transfer capabilities and thermal conductivity, thereby addressing the current performance limitations of bio-based PCMs.
- Eutectic/hybrid material: investigating eutectic/hybrid mixtures of bio-based PCMs is another point that needs to be explored. This also could overcome the limitations associated with low latent heat capacity while optimizing their phase change characteristics.
- Sustainability assessment: a comprehensive lifecycle analysis is important to fully understand the environmental impacts of biobased PCMs. Future studies should conduct rigorous evaluations of the ecological footprint throughout the production, utilization, and disposal phases. This research will provide critical insights into the sustainability advantages of bio-based alternatives compared to conventional PCMs.

Addressing these areas, the development and adoption of bio-based PCMs can significantly contribute to a sustainable, high-performance, and cost-effective solution to advance the field of cold thermal energy storage.
