#### ABSTRACT

Renewable energy plays a pivotal role for mankind in the times of adverse climate change and global warming. However, renewable energy such as solar energy comes with inherent drawbacks of limited or varying availability in terms of time, space and power. Consequently, it will lead to poor performance of numerous solar thermal technologies. To overcome these constraints of solar energy, Thermal Energy Storage (TES) can play a pivotal role in improving performance and feasibility of solar thermal technologies. TES using Phase Change Material (PCM) is one of the effective techniques of charging, storing, and discharging thermal energy as and when required. PCM stores thermal energy in the form of latent heat by undergoing phase change at constant temperature. However, PCM suffers with drawbacks of low thermal conductivity, poor solar to thermal conversion efficiency, and risk of leakage during phase transition. These thermo-physical properties limit the applicability of PCM as a potential TES material. In view of the above facts and findings, this study comprehensively analyses the above mentioned thermo-physical bottlenecks of PCMs. It briefly discusses TES, various materials for TES, PCM, and properties of PCM. In detail, it presents various methods and mechanisms of improving solar to thermal performance and thermal conductivity of the PCM. Additionally, this study presents a robust discussion on techniques of minimizing the leakage of the PCM during phase transformation.

# **1. Introduction**

Renewable energy technologies and its capacity building will play a major role in mitigating the effect of global warming and climate change. Renewable energy, such as solar energy, wind energy, ocean energy, and geothermal energy, plays a crucial role in fulfilling the rising demand for energy in a sustainable way and helps in minimizing emissions caused due to over-exploitation of fossil reserves [[1](#page-23-0)]. Among all the available renewable sources of energy, solar energy is the most abundant source of available energy [\[2\]](#page-23-0). Also, it is clean and easily available at almost every location on the earth. However, due to limited availability in terms of time, location, and power, solar thermal technologies such as solar collectors, solar concentrators, solar desalination, and solar driers have poor efficiency and low utilization rates [[3](#page-23-0)], [[4](#page-23-0)]. For example, after sunshine hours, there is no utilization of solar thermal technologies without storage. The intermittent nature of solar energy has caused poor performance of solar thermal technologies [\[5\]](#page-23-0). To overcome these drawbacks related to solar energy, researchers have

come up with a solution called Thermal Energy Storage (TES) [[6](#page-23-0)]. TES is the technique of storing heat/cold when available in abundant quantity and utilizing it when it is not available. TES can be classified as sensible heat storage, latent heat storage, and thermochemical energy storage [[7](#page-23-0)]. Among all these three types of TES, latent heat storage (LHS) is widely used because of its various merits such as high energy density and minimum temperature change. LHS is the technique of storing thermal energy due to the phase transition of the storage material. Such material, which undergoes phase transformation while storing and releasing thermal energy, is called Phase Change Material (PCM). In recent years, TES using PCM (solid-liquid) has evolved as one of the prominent techniques for storing excessive heat/cold and utilizing it as and when required [[8](#page-23-0)]. PCM stores and releases thermal energy at an almost constant temperature, have a high energy density, available in wide temperature ranges, and are easily available. For example, rock has a specific heat capacity of 0.9 kJ/kg K, and paraffin wax has a heat storage capacity of 174.4 kJ/kg. It means that to store 174.4 kJ/kg of energy, rock temperature needs to increase by almost 175 K. Also, the storage volume needed to store 1 GJ of energy for rock is 9.9 m<sup>3</sup> , and

Because of these merits, PCMs are widely used for temperature regulation in textiles [[9](#page-23-0)], automobiles [\[10](#page-23-0)], [[11\]](#page-23-0), batteries [[12\]](#page-23-0), [\[13](#page-23-0)], [\[14](#page-23-0)] buildings [[15\]](#page-24-0), [\[16](#page-24-0)], aerospace [\[17](#page-24-0)], [[18\]](#page-24-0), district heating [\[19](#page-24-0)], electronics, [[20\]](#page-24-0), [[21\]](#page-24-0) refrigeration [[22](#page-24-0)] and cold chain supply [[23\]](#page-24-0), [[24\]](#page-24-0), [\[25](#page-24-0)].

Despite the above advantages and applications of PCM in various thermal systems, PCM suffers from four significant drawbacks, i.e., poor thermal conductivity [\[26](#page-24-0)], [\[27](#page-24-0)], [\[28](#page-24-0)] leakage during phase transition [[29\]](#page-24-0), and poor solar to thermal conversion [[30\]](#page-24-0), [[31\]](#page-24-0), [[32\]](#page-24-0). These drawbacks limit PCM applicability in varieties of thermal systems. Poor thermal conductivity reduces the heat transfer from and to the PCM, causing poor charging and discharging of the PCM [\[33](#page-24-0)], [[34\]](#page-24-0), [[35\]](#page-24-0). The risk of leakage during phase transition leads to loss of PCM from the TES systems. Reduction in quantity due to leakage will hamper the LHS capacity of the TES systems [[36\]](#page-24-0). Poor light-absorbing capacity of the PCM causes low solar-to-thermal conversion efficiency [\[37](#page-24-0)] [[38](#page-24-0)], which degrades its thermal performance. Several attempts have been made in past by the scientists to overcome these drawbacks of PCM. Nanoparticles are widely used to improve thermal conductivity of the PCM. Carbon based nanoparticles specifically 1D, 2D, and 3D materials are significantly used by the researchers to improve thermal conductivity of PCM. Additionally, metal-based nanomaterials such as Ag, Ni, Cu, Al, and Ti were also commonly used to enhance heat transfer rate of PCM. Apart from having improved thermal conductivity, nano-enhanced PCM also shows high photo to thermal conversion efficiency [[39](#page-24-0)]. It has been found that PCM loaded with nanoparticles have excellent photothermal conversion efficiency in comparison with bare PCM [\[40](#page-24-0)]. Apart from nanoparticles, porous material such as porous carbon, graphite scaffolds, polyurethane foam, and porous clays were also used to improve heat transfer of PCM [[41\]](#page-24-0). These porous materials not only improve thermal conductivity but also act as supporting material to reduce leakage of the PCM. Due to porous structure of the materials, they have larger pore size and PCM settles easily in those pores. Every application of PCM takes advantage of specific properties of PCM. For e.g., solar thermal systems need better solar to thermal conversion along with thermal storage whereas buildings need better heat transfer rate along with thermal storage. Thus, to investigate and understand about various methods, mechanism and materials used to improve thermal performance of the PCM along with anti-leakage characteristics, this comprehensive and updated review study was conducted. Several attempts have been made in the past to review these drawbacks related to PCM. However, none of those studies have investigated all these demerits combined in a single study. Also, there is a scarcity of studies related to detailed analysis of improving solar to thermal conversion and storage performance of PCM for TES systems. The objective of this study is to conduct a comprehensive review of various methods, materials, and characterization techniques used to overcome the drawbacks of poor thermal conductivity, leakage of PCM, and poor solar to thermal conversion efficiency.

### **2. Thermal energy storage**

TES is one form of energy storage technique which is used by mankind since ancient times. TES is the three-step process i.e. charging, storing, and discharging. TES is one of the prominent techniques of increasing the thermal system's efficiency and helps in overcoming the gap between demand and supply [\[42](#page-24-0)]. There are three types of TES i.e. sensible heat storage, thermo-chemical heat storage, and latent heat storage. The detailed classification of TES is shown in [Fig. 1](#page-1-0).

### *2.1. Sensible heat storage (SHS)*

Sensible heat storage is the simplest and most economical way of storing thermal energy, which stores the heat energy in its sensible heat capacity under the change in temperature, as represented in Fig. 2(a) [[44\]](#page-24-0). For e.g., water can store heat energy either by raising its temperature or changing its phase, but it is known as sensible heat when it stores energy due to rising temperature. The sensible heat energy is

expressed as,

*Q* = *m.Cp.*Δ*T* (1)

The sensible heat can be stored in a solid or liquid storage medium. The high-temperature SHS materials are the focus area of the researchers for intermittent solar energy storage applications [[45\]](#page-24-0). The major drawback with SHS is that the amount of heat that can be stored is based on the rise of temperature. To store large energy the temperature rise is also larger. This makes it ineffective for various thermal applications. Commonly used sensible heat substances for building applications are listed in the Table 1.

# *2.2. Thermo-chemical energy storage (TCES)*

Thermochemical materials (TCM) store and release heat by a reversible endothermic/exothermic reaction. TCES technology can be divided into chemical reaction heat storage (CRHS) and sorption heat storage (SOHS). Both technologies offer the following advantages: high TES capacity, low-temperature thermal energy storage, minimal heat losses, and small storage systems [\[47](#page-24-0)]. There are three stages for storing thermal energy by TCM charging (reaction/sorption), storage (low temperature-open/close system), and discharging (reversible reaction/ sorption), as shown in Fig. 2(b). In a sorption process, the sorbent (usually solid or liquid) and the sorbate (often vapour or gas) are separated during the desorption process using a heat source. The heat energy is released when the sorbent comes back into contact during the sorption process. In this instance, the thermal energy is stored through the chemical potential. The general equation of TCES can be given as

*AB* + *Q*(*Heat*) ↔ *A* + *B*

The ability to store energy with practically negligible energy losses is the real benefit of thermochemical energy storage, making it an attractive option for long-term or seasonal energy storage. However, its principal drawbacks include high costs, high complexity, poor heat transfer, and low stability that, with time, reduces storage efficiency. Schmidt et al. [\[48](#page-24-0)] used a thermochemical reaction for storing seasonal thermal energy by converting calcium hydroxide into calcium oxide and water. The reaction can be given as Ca(OH)<sup>2</sup> ↔ CaO + H2O. The forward reaction needed a temperature of around 450 ◦C. The addition of zinc or aluminum can enhance the rate of reaction. In a recent development, MgO microparticles with ultrahigh porosity can be used to create environmentally friendly heat storage systems with an efficiency of 90.3 % from the theoretical rate of reaction, which is considerably good [\[49](#page-24-0)]. However, further analysis is needed before this material's real-time use for building applications.

# *2.3. Latent heat storage (LHS)*

The latent heat storage has primarily stored the heat during the phase-change process (almost constant temperature) and is firmly linked to the substance's latent heat as shown in [Fig. 2\(](#page-2-0)c). The most effective way to store the latent heat for building applications is by using phase change materials because it has high energy storage density at constant temperature [[50\]](#page-24-0). The heat energy stored by a PCM can be given as,

*Q* = ∫ *Tm Ti m*Cpi dT + *mf* Δ*q* + ∫ *Tf Tm m*Cps dT (2)

*Q* = *mCpi*(*Tm* − *Ti*) + *mf* Δ*q* + *mCps*(*Tf* − *Tm*) (3)

PCMs are often used for temperature regulation in various thermal applications due to their advantages like high energy density, low cost, ease of incorporation, availability for a wide range of temperature applications, and isothermal energy storage. Due to the incorporation of PCM, the latent heat capacity of the building increases. In LHS, there are three primary modes in which the phase shift process occurs: solid-solid, liquid-gas, and solid-liquid. Heat is retained in the first scenario by switching between several crystallization forms. Although the latent heat for liquid-gas systems is relatively large, the high-volume variations during phase change provide some issues with storage control. The most common is the solid-liquid PCMs, which have a relatively high melting latent heat and a low volume variation during latent heat exchange

(often less than 10 %) [[51\]](#page-24-0). A suitable PCM with a melting point within the appropriate temperature range, a suitable heat exchange surface, and a suitable container compatible with the PCM are the minimum three components that any latent TES system must have [[52\]](#page-24-0).

### **3. Phase change materials (PCMs)**

PCMs can store latent heat when undergoing a solid-to-liquid or liquid-to-gas phase transition. PCMs are energy-saving products with a low energy cost that falls under clean energy sources. PCMs are generally categorised based on their physical transition for heat absorption and desorbing characteristics. PCMs are typically divided into four groups according to their phase transition processes: solid to solid, solid to liquid, gas to solid, and liquid to gas. Solid to liquid PCMs are widely used for thermal applications due to various advantages like high energy density, low volume change, availability, environmentally friendly and low cost. Fig. 3 shows a wide range of classifications and a brief description of PCMs.

Various PCMs are available in different transition temperature ranges and have varied thermo-physical characteristics which affects its TES capacity. Therefore, selection of PCM for any TES system is the crucial factor in improving its thermal performance. The selection of PCMs can be made based on several thermophysical, technical, environmental, and economic considerations. Al-Yasiri et al. [\[53](#page-24-0)] conducted a study where five parameters were considered for selecting PCMs: phase change temperature, thermal conductivity, latent heat of phase change, compatibility with encapsulation materials, and the material's flammability. The phase change temperature of PCMs is the primary parameter of the selection of PCMs. Based on other literature, it can be concluded that the choice of PCMs for building applications should depend on working temperature range. Working temperature range varies with application such as for buildings it must be 20 ◦C to 40 ◦C, for solar water heating it must be around 50 ◦C to 70 ◦C, for electronics cooling it must be around 20 ◦C to 30 ◦C. The environmental conditions and the different thermophysical properties can influence the performance of the PCMs [\[54](#page-24-0)] [[55\]](#page-24-0). The classification of the selection of PCMs is summarized in Fig. 4. The use of eutectic PCMs for TES has increased as eutectic PCMs can be prepared at any desired transition temperatures and have properties similar to that of the starting components.

### *3.1. Incorporation of PCMs*

Phase change materials (PCMs) can be incorporated into building materials in various ways to improve energy efficiency and thermal comfort, summarized in [Fig. 6.](#page-7-0) Each method has its advantages and disadvantages depending on the type of building, application, and material. It is essential to consider the specific requirements of the building, including cost, durability, and energy efficiency, when selecting the appropriate method for incorporating the PCMs.

The challenges associated with PCMs as potential TES materials are low thermal conductivity, leakage, volume change during phase transition, flammability, supercooling, corrosiveness, and poor stability [[95\]](#page-25-0). However, leakage and thermal conductivity are the most challenging issue to be addressed. When PCM gets embedded in any thermal system, TES must be leakproof. The leakage of PCM during phase transition from the TES systems will degrade the TES capacity of the system and, consequently, inversely affects its thermal performance. The leakage issue of PCMs was addressed using different incorporation techniques like encapsulation and shape stabilization [[96\]](#page-25-0). The use of incorporation techniques must ensure minimal leakage during the melting of the PCM [\[97](#page-25-0)]. PCMs incorporated into thermal systems using different techniques are briefed in the [Fig. 7](#page-8-0).

### *3.1.1. Direct incorporation*

Direct incorporation technique is primarily used in building envelopes. In direct incorporation, the PCM in powder or liquid form is added directly to the construction material, such as gypsum mortar, cement mortar, and concrete [\[99](#page-25-0)]. There is a particular disadvantage with direct incorporation, such as leakage causing material incompatibility and increasing the risk of fire (for flammable PCMs) along with loss of PCM. Furthermore, because the PCM is added to the mixture in a liquid state, the water content ratio is reduced, which weakens the mechanical properties of constructed elements at high temperatures [\[100\]](#page-25-0). The durability and bonding strength of aggregates and binder paste of building materials were also reduced [[101](#page-25-0)]. [\[102\]](#page-25-0) conducted an experimental investigation on the thermal performance of walls with voids filled with cellulose insulation augmented with PCM. The findings revealed that individual walls had daily average peak heat flux reductions of 25.4 % and hourly average peak heat flux reductions of 20.1 % for all the walls combined. In another work, Kontoleon et al. [[103](#page-25-0)] investigated the thermal and mechanical properties of the reinforced concrete wall covered with PCM-enhanced plastering by directly mixing the obtained capsules with the mixture of cement CEM II 32.5 and natural sand. The result showed that the interior thermal comfort increased and heat flux through the wall was reduced, and the interval between the peak temperatures inside and outside was increased. Tian et al. [[104](#page-25-0)] evaluated the effectiveness of a wall coated with plastering mortar that included 50 % ceramsite PCM to a control wall coated with pure mortar. Results showed that the inner surface of the wall had a 55 % reduction in temperature variations, demonstrating the usefulness of PCM-enhanced mortar in achieving interior thermal comfort in the summer.

# *3.1.2. Impregnation of PCM*

In this method, the supporting materials are dipped in the liquid PCM, where the material absorbs the liquid through capillary action, an illustrative image shown in [Figs. 8](#page-8-0) & [9](#page-9-0) [\[105\]](#page-25-0). The impregnation process has three stages. First, air and water are removed from the base material with the help of a vacuum pump. Then the base materials are dipped into the liquid PCM under a vacuum. Finally, the PCM-dipped material is mixed with the concrete [[106](#page-25-0)]. The main disadvantages of this method are leakage, incompatibility with base material, and corrosion of reinforced steel when incorporated with concrete elements, which reduces its service life [[107](#page-25-0)]. Qu et al. [\[108\]](#page-25-0) created a variety of samples with various paraffin and fume silica in different mass ratios. It was suggested to use a raw material mass ratio of 45:55 for paraffin and fumed silica, respectively. The high capillary pressure of the nanopores and the van der Waals interaction between the methyl groups rendered the composite stable at this proportion. In another work, Chen et al. [[109](#page-25-0)] investigated carbon layer-modified mesoporous silica (CLMS).

### *3.1.3. Encapsulation of PCMs*

Encapsulation of PCMs refers to the process of enclosing the PCM within a shell or capsule. Due to leakage, environmental reactivity, and toxicity issues during phase change, PCMs cannot be directly incorporated into the thermal envelope. Encapsulation provides a coating or covering over the PCM. Encapsulation is the preferred method to incorporate a PCM in thermal systems because it effectively prevents PCM leakage, which can result in a loss of efficiency and performance. Encapsulation can also help improve the PCM's stability and durability, making it more resistant to degradation over time. This method also increases the heat transfer area and, as a result, the thermal conductivity of PCM to use its storage capacity fully. There are various methods for encapsulating PCMs, including spray drying, emulsion polymerization, and coacervation. These methods involve creating a shell or coating around the PCM, typically using a polymer material, to create a stable and contained unit. Three encapsulation types are commonly used, macro, micro, and nano-encapsulation. The scale range of capsules for different encapsulations is shown in [Fig. 10.](#page-9-0) The choice of encapsulation method depends on the specific properties of the PCM, the desired encapsulation material, and the intended application. Encapsulation of PCMs is an important technology for a wide range of applications, including building and construction, transportation, and energy storage. By encapsulating PCMs, it is possible to create highly efficient and effective TES systems that can be used to reduce energy consumption and improve overall system performance.

### **4. Different techniques for enhancing the thermal conductivity of PCMs**

### *4.1. Nanoparticle enhanced PCMs*

Nanoparticles were used to enhance the thermal conductivity of organic PCMs because of the high surface-to-volume ratio. The thermal conductivity improvement of shape-stabilized PCM loaded with nanoparticles depends on several variables, including nanoparticle concentration, size of particles, intermolecular interaction, aspect ratio, Brownian motion, clustering, purity, and interfacial thermal resistance [[168](#page-27-0)]. In general, including nanoparticles can produce impressive heat transfer but will lose latent heat capacity when combined with a PCM. A variety of nanomaterials, including stable metals (Au), ceramic oxides (Al2O3), metallic oxides (zirconia, silica, and alumina), metallic carbides (SiC), metallic nitrides (SiN, AlN), and different carbon morphologies are commonly used for fabrication of nanocomposites [[169](#page-27-0)]. ### *4.2. Solar to thermal performance of PCM*

Photothermal materials are nanomaterials with well-defined dimensions, shapes, compositions, and surface functions that allow lowdensity light energy to be converted to thermal energy. The fundamental working principle of photothermal conversion is that photothermal fillers can be activated by light, causing electrons to jump to the excited state. Then, nonradiative relaxation, energy transfer, or quenching will allow the excited electrons to release their energy and return to the ground state [\[240\]](#page-28-0). The most critical considerations for photothermal applications are material and structural design. Several Near Infra-Red (NIR) responsive photothermal materials and structural engineering techniques have recently been created to suit various applications [[241](#page-28-0)]. Photothermal materials can be classified into four basic categories based on their functionality, semiconductors, plasmonic metals, carbon, and polymer-based materials, as shown in Fig. 25 [[242](#page-28-0)]. The three basic photothermal mechanisms for conversions are electronhole generation and relaxation of semiconductors, as shown in Fig. 26 (b), plasmonic localized heating, as shown in Fig. 26(a), and HOMO-LUMO excitation and lattice vibration of molecules, shown in Fig. 26 (c). The highest energy molecular orbital with electrons is called the HOMO, while the next highest energy molecular orbital with no electrons is called the LUMO. The excitation and lattice vibration between these two molecular orbitals work as the photothermal conversion. Photothermal materials have a high rate of energy conversion efficiency and require less preparation. According to their constituent parts, they can also be classified as carbon-based materials, metal-based materials, organic materials, and other photothermal materials [\[243](#page-28-0)]. Due to their capacity to absorb a broad spectrum of sunlight, carbon-based materials are a significant focus in the recent study of photothermal materials. It strongly absorbs portions of visible and near-infrared light and simultaneously benefits from excellent stability and low cost [[244](#page-28-0)]. Another class of photothermal material that exhibits the local surface plasma resonance (LSPR) phenomenon that may absorb longer-wavelength infrared light and transform it into thermal energy is metal-based materials [[245](#page-28-0)]. If the frequency of the incident light and the resonance frequency of the free electrons on the surface of the metal particle match, coherent oscillations will cause the collective excitation of the electrons to occur when a light ray strikes a metal-based nanoparticle. Organic photothermal conversion materials have high photo absorption, photostability, and photothermal conversion efficiency. Compared to carbon-based materials, they are simpler to create and process and their ability to control light absorption may be altered across a large range [[246](#page-29-0)]. Some newly developed photothermal materials, such MXene, due to the LSPR effect, display remarkable photothermal performance [[247](#page-29-0)].

The performance of photothermal materials is measured by thermal flux and photothermal efficiency. The photothermal conversion efficiency is determined by the fraction of the absorption in the extinction [[248](#page-29-0)]. ## **5. Research outlook**

The development in nanotechnology played a significant role in improving thermal and physical performance of PCM. Carbon-based nanomaterials are in trend for improving the thermal conductivity of PCM because of significantly high thermal conductivity, nearly five times greater than metals and almost fifty times more than metal oxides. Nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, graphite, expanded graphite, graphene nanoplatelets, and metallic nanoparticles, are incorporated into PCMs to enhance their thermal conductivity. These nano additives create a conductive network within the PCM, facilitating faster heat transfer and improving the overall performance of the thermal energy storage system. Research should focus on understanding the interaction mechanism between nanomaterials, surfactants, and PCM in nanomaterials-based composite PCM. By studying these interactions, optimizing the properties of nanomaterials-based composite PCM and improving their thermal performance will be possible.

The current trend of improving shape stability of the PCM involves incorporating PCM within a matrix or host material to enhance mechanical strength, stability, and heat transfer properties. These methods include embedding PCM in polymers, foams, or porous materials to create composites with improved thermal characteristics and mechanical integrity. Further investigation of the porous media and PCM interaction in composite PCM systems is needed. The geometric parameters of the porous media, such as pore density and porosity, significantly influence the thermal properties of composite PCM. Optimizing the porous media configuration will enhance the thermal properties of TES systems. Despite the considerable efficiency of photothermal materials, some challenges such as finding low-cost, nontoxic, environmentally friendly, readily available, and recyclable materials must be addressed. Additionally, the intermittent nature of solar radiation, weather changes, and scaling of solar absorbing materials need to be considered to optimize photothermal materials performance in TES systems.

# **6. Conclusion**

TES using PCM can helps in mitigating the challenges of poor performance of solar energy technologies. High energy density and minimum temperature change makes them one of the potential TES material. PCM stores the excess amount of thermal energy available during the sunny days and releases it as and when required. However, there are few major thermo-physical limitations of PCM which limits the appicability. Literature review shows that almost all the PCM shows poor solar to thermal conversion performance, low thermal conductivity, leakage during phase transformation and high degree of super cooling. Numerous attempts has been made to overcome these drawbacks to improve thermo-physical performance of PCM for improved TES. Leakage during phase transition leads to loss of the PCM from the TES system. To improve stability and minimize leakage, micorencapsulation and macroencapsulation techniques are commonly used. Encapsulation of PCM has significantly reduces the leakage of the PCM and also improves heat transfer performance because of increased in surface area Various poros materials were also used to stablized the shape of the PCM during phase transformation. Poor thermal conductivity of the PCM causes slow charging and discharging. Development of composite PCM loaded with metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanparticles, and carbon based nanoparticles have given a significant boost in improving thermal conductivity. Solar to thermal performance of the PCM can be improved by loading photothermal fillers. Photothermal materials have high solar to thermal conversion efficiency. Thus, when these photothermal fillers loaded in the PCM they form composite PCM with high solar to thermal performance. It is evident that the composite PCM thus prepared have low TES capacity as compare to bare PCM.
