#### ABSTRACT

Research indicates that molten salt phase change materials (MSPCMs) represent a promising alternative for thermal energy storage (TES), effectively addressing the energy supply-demand imbalance. These salts typically have a range of excellent properties, such as high energy storage density, easy availability, and minimal environmental impact. ## **1. Introduction**

As the economy continues to grow, energy consumption has been rising steadily, leading to growing concerns about potential energy shortages. Currently, global reliance on fossil energy sources like coal and oil remains predominant; however, these resources are nonrenewable and pose significant challenges, including resource depletion and environmental pollution. As a result, sustainable energy development has become a focal point of attention [[1](#page-29-0)]. For most regions, the variability of energy sources like solar, wind, and tidal power is a key factor influencing the development of sustainable energy [[2](#page-29-0),[3\]](#page-29-0). Due to their dependable physical and chemical properties, along with sustainable thermal characteristics during both charging and discharging processes, TES technologies have experienced continuous expansion. TES systems offer practical and efficient solutions for ensuring a sustainable energy supply, primarily focusing on latent heat storage (LHTES), sensible heat storage (SHTES), and thermochemical energy storage (TCTES) in PCMs [[4\]](#page-29-0). Among these, LHTES in PCMs is advantageous for storing substantial amounts of thermal energy with minimal temperature fluctuations, and has thus garnered significant attention from researchers.

PCMs can be primarily classified into two categories: organic and inorganic, based on their chemical composition. Organic PCMs typically include paraffin waxes and non-paraffin compounds, such as those with alcohol, ester, and acid functional groups. These materials are known for their safety and stability, high latent heat of fusion, and suitability across a wide temperature range (15–45 ◦C). The most typical types of organic PCMs are paraffins [\[5](#page-29-0)] and fatty acids [\[6](#page-29-0)]. However, their strong flammability poses a limitation for high-temperature applications. Inorganic PCMs encompass metals, hydrated salts, and molten salts, all characterized by their predominant ionic bonding. Metallic PCMs exhibit higher TC and less volume change than organic PCMs. However, metallic PCMs often have a low latent heat of fusion per unit weight, which can be a drawback, as they may add significant weight to thermal storage systems. Hydrated salts could be problematic due to their susceptibility to supercooling and phase separation, affecting thermal storage stability and limiting their practical applications. As a result, Molten salt PCMs (MSPCMs) are particularly notable for their higher latent heat of fusion, excellent TC, negligible volume change, and wide range of phase transition temperatures. These attributes make MSPCMs highly versatile, allowing for a broader range of applications and enhancing their effectiveness in TES solutions compared to both organic

and other inorganic PCMs.

Despite the remarkable attributes of MSPCMs, their practical application is hindered by intrinsic challenges, including limitations in TC and leakage issues. In response to these obstacles, various solutions have been proposed by researchers. Primarily, the TC of PCMs was improved through the incorporation of high TC substances, such as metals and metal oxides [\[7,8\]](#page-29-0). However, the introduction of these materials results in an increase in the overall density of the PCMs, which is disadvantageous for applications with stringent constraints on system weight. Conversely, carbon-based materials, including carbon nanotubes, graphene, and expanded graphite (EG), are favored due to their superior TC, low density, and stable chemical properties. Moreover, with the ongoing advancement of novel technologies, the production costs of these carbon-based materials are progressively decreasing. To effectively address the leakage issue associated with PCMs, researchers have developed two primary encapsulation strategies: shape-stable encapsulation and capsule encapsulation [\[9,10\]](#page-29-0). Shape-stabilized encapsulation involves embedding a PCMs within a porous substrate, metal foam, or composite material to maintain its structural integrity throughout the phase change process. This technique effectively mitigates leakage during phase transition while enhancing the thermal stability and mechanical strength of the material. Nevertheless, it remains challenging to completely prevent leakage when PCMs are adsorbed by porous materials, and a substantial amount of supporting material is required, potentially compromising the TES performance of the composites. In comparison, microencapsulation technology encapsulates PCMs within polymer or inorganic shells through chemical methods, forming coreshell microcapsules. The core advantage of this structure lies in its excellent structural integrity, with encapsulation efficiency typically exceeding 90 % [[11](#page-29-0)]. Research shows that some microcapsules can maintain over 90 % latent heat retention even after 3000 thermal cycles (500–650 ◦C) [[12\]](#page-29-0). However, the high production costs of capsules limit their widespread application. As operating temperatures increase, the heat resistance requirements for the capsules also escalate, necessitating modifications to the shell, adjustments to the core-shell ratio, and structural optimization to improve the heat transfer rate and meet the specific demands of diverse applications.

Currently, low-temperature PCMs have been widely applied in various fields, such as smart textiles [\[26\]](#page-30-0), thermal management for electronic devices [[27\]](#page-30-0), and enhancing building energy efficiency [\[28](#page-30-0)]. At the same time, medium and high-temperature MSPCMs are playing a vital role in TES systems, especially in solar thermal power generation [[29\]](#page-30-0). These materials are highly efficient in storing and releasing thermal energy, which significantly boosts the thermal energy utilization efficiency of such systems. However, research on medium and hightemperature encapsulated PCMs lags behind, primarily due to the challenges posed by the higher temperature conditions. These include the need for superior thermal stability, corrosion resistance, and the precise control of volume changes at elevated temperatures, making the material development complex and demanding [[30\]](#page-30-0). Therefore, advancing the development of high-efficiency encapsulated PCMs that can withstand medium and high temperatures is crucial. Such advancements would be instrumental in enhancing heat storage technologies in this temperature range and optimizing the effective use of renewable energy sources.

also have inherent limitations. Table 1 provides a comprehensive assessment of the relative merits and demerits of various MSPCMs techniques. It is evident that several reviews focus on the role of nanoparticles in enhancing the properties of MSPCMs [13–[18](#page-29-0)]. These reviews highlight the promising potential of nanoparticles to augment material performance and identify systematic deficiencies in current research, particularly with respect to model validation, nanofluid dynamics, particle stability, and compatibility with saline systems. Additionally, they address technical challenges, including elevated costs and the inadequacy of process optimization. However, the focus remains primarily on material enhancement, with insufficient exploration of the role of molten salts in energy conversion systems or the sustainability considerations associated with these materials. Porous encapsulation materials summarized the the selection principles, types, and current status of support materials in salt-based composites [[18,19\]](#page-29-0). Inorganic salt-based composites provide an overview of the thermal properties and fabrication processes of molten salts [\[20](#page-29-0)]. While these reviews offer valuable insights for the structural design of composite materials, the limited research on the practical application of skeleton materials and the underexplored potential of molten salt latent heat underscore the necessity for the further development of innovative models and materials. Moreover, these reviews do not address the reuse of solid waste in molten salt-based CPCMs, nor do they consider the role of molten saltbased composites in energy conversion processes. As a result, important dimensions such as sustainable development and energy efficiency are largely overlooked.

In terms of application scenarios, a review of molten chlorides [\[21](#page-29-0)] reveals that, while these molten salts are well-suited for hightemperature environments, they have also introduced various anticorrosion methods and strategies, thereby facilitating their potential application in specific energy conversion systems. However, the issue of dependence on costly corrosion-resistant alloys remains an urgent challenge, as this not only escalates the cost of practical implementation but also restricts the widespread adoption of this technology. Concerning corrosion inhibition technology [\[17](#page-29-0),22–[24\]](#page-30-0), the underlying corrosion mechanisms and various anti-corrosion strategies have been proposed. Nevertheless, there is a notable lack of comprehensive data and validated models within the current research, making it difficult to accurately predict the performance and behavior of MSPCMs in realworld applications. This limitation hampers the effective application and further development of this technology within the energy sector. While these studies offer depth in specific fields, they fail to analyze the comprehensive impact of MSPCMs on TES systems from a broader perspective. Furthermore, several reviews discuss the application of MSPCMs in TES systems [[25\]](#page-30-0). However, the issue of mechanical stress remains an area requiring optimization through multiphysics coupling simulations. These reviews predominantly focus on shell-and-tube latent heat storage (LHTES), neglecting the diverse potentials and advantages of MSPCMs across various TES scenarios. Consequently, they constrain our understanding of the broader applicability of MSPCMs in the energy storage domain. Table 1 summarizes the findings from these studies. The results indicate that while considerable progress has been made in improving material properties, exploring specific applications, and studying corrosion issues, the majority of the reviews remain narrowly focused on individual aspects of MSPCMs. There is a lack of comprehensive and in-depth analysis that integrates material preparation, performance optimization, multi-scenario applications, and energy conversion perspectives.

To address this gap, the purpose of our review is to provide a thorough and detailed synthesis of the latest advancements in MSPCMs, offering a more holistic analysis of the topic. This review draws from 188 references, including 31 review papers, 1 report and 156 research papers. This study specifically focuses on 156 research papers published between 2010 and 2025, which highlight the performance and applications of MSPCMs, offering a comprehensive reflection of the most recent technological progress. These articles were sourced from databases such as ScienceDirect and Web of Science using key terms such as "PCMs," "molten salts," "TES," and "porous skeleton materials." The selected papers were systematically categorized based on key characteristics of the carrier material, thermal conductivity, phase transition temperature, latent heat, efficiency, and other relevant factors.

Through a thorough and systematic analysis of 156 research papers, this review provides a comprehensive review of the mechanisms through which porous matrix materials influence the thermal and physical properties of MSPCMs. The review specifically focuses on the structural support provided by carbon-based materials (graphene, EG, carbon nanotubes), oxide-based materials (silica, alumina), and novel silicon carbide-based porous structures, while also evaluating the potential for utilizing solid waste as a porous carrier. In the realm of packaging technologies, the review not only examines traditional porous matrix packaging systems but also delves into recent advancements in capsule packaging technologies, encompassing microcapsules, nanocapsules. Furthermore, it presents a comparative analysis of key performance indicators, such as thermal conductivity enhancement, cycling stability, and the scalability of various packaging methods, by developing a multidimensional evaluation framework. Meanwhile, the paper explores the energy conversion potential of MSPCMs, including photothermal and magnetocaloric processes, and provides a comprehensive overview of their applications in various heat exchanger energy storage systems such as shell-and-tube, packed bed, rotary drum, and electric heater systems. Furthermore, we provide valuable perspectives on the future research directions and opportunities in this emerging field, particularly the application of MSPCMs in oil extraction, while also discussing the challenges and opportunities MSPCMs face in advanced applications.

## **2. Research progress of preparation technology of molten salt CPCMs**

Intensive research on molten salts has led to the widespread

development of various types. Molten salts are typically classified based on the anion type, including nitrates [[35\]](#page-30-0), carbonates, chlorides, fluorides [\[34](#page-30-0)] etc. To better accommodate complex operating conditions, it has been demonstrated that combining two or more molten salts can yield eutectic mixtures, which exhibit enhanced heat storage capacity and broader applications [\[20](#page-29-0)]. However, it is crucial to note that these salts must be blended in precise ratios to achieve the eutectic state. Furthermore, although MSPCMs offer high latent heat, their widespread use is hindered by inherent drawbacks, including corrosion, low TC, and leakage. To overcome these obstacles, researchers have proposed various strategies, including the incorporation of highly conductive materials or porous media to enhance TC, as well as the use of encapsulation techniques to contain molten salts and mitigate issues related to corrosion and leakage. Zhu et al. [\[36](#page-30-0)] explored the influence of six preparation techniques on the properties of solar salts (SS). It was revealed that the selection of preparation methods for eutectic mixtures varied according to the experimental conditions observed during conventional studies. With extensive research on MSPCMs, the types of carriers have significantly diversified. Currently, the carriers for molten salt CPCMs primarily encompass carbon-based porous materials, oxides, as well as various porous ceramic and non-oxide ceramic materials. Fig. 1 illustrates a range of encapsulation materials employed in molten salt CPCMs in recent years.

### *2.1. Preparation of porous skeletal CPCMs*

Molten salts are usually encapsulated through a porous carrier composite approach, in which the capillary force, surface tension and hydrogen bonding of the porous material absorbs the PCMs into the porous channels, effectively inhibiting leakage. During the preparation process, molten salts are embedded within the microporous structure of the skeleton material, ensuring that salt leakage and shape deformation are avoided during phase transitions [\[37,38](#page-30-0)]. In general, for CPCMs to be successfully prepared, it is imperative to select backbone materials that possess exceptional physical and chemical compatibility with the molten salts. Notably, the thermal stability of the porous material at elevated operating temperatures, along with thermophysical properties, must be considered when choosing suitable materials. Presently, common methods for fabricating PCMs skeleton composite molding materials include melt impregnation and hybrid sintering techniques.

### *2.1.1. Melt impregnation method*

The initial step in the fabrication of stabilized composites is the penetration of PCMs in the molten form into the pores of the skeletal material, utilizing either unpressurized or depressurized methods. It is widely used due to its relative simplicity of execution and moderate requirements for the skeleton material. However, the extent to which it improves the PCMs properties determined by the type and content of the supporting bone material. Li et al. [[39\]](#page-30-0) prepared composites of 80 wt% KCl-LiCl/20 wt% EG by melt impregnation method using EG as adsorbent material for PCMs. It was revealed that the PCMs were evenly dispersed throughout the porous structure of EG, with any signs of aggregation. However, the melt impregnation technique is not universally effective for all combinations of skeleton material and salt. Specifically, When the proportion of skeleton material is minimal, the pores may become obstructed with air, leading to a significant reduction in salt adsorption efficiency. In an attempt to ensure more nitrate penetration into the surrounding pores of EG, Ren et al. [[40\]](#page-30-0) tried a novel process called "solution + melt impregnation". This method results in the uniform adsorption and dispersion of nitrate particles within the porous network of EG. Consequently, the hybrid impregnation technique demonstrates superior adsorption efficiency compared to traditional melt impregnation.

#### *2.1.2. Mixed sintering method*

Hybrid sintering is employed to achieve morphologically stable CPCMs by integrating PCMs with specific additives, compacting them into preformed blocks, and subsequently sintering these assemblies to amalgamate the PCMs with the framework materials, resulting in the formation of morphologically stable CPCMs.

Salt-based composites are typically formed into the final product through cold pressing and hot sintering processes. The various precursors are physically ground and blended to facilitate efficient molding and pressing, subsequently undergoing sintering in a muffle furnace [[41\]](#page-30-0). A significant limitation of this technological is that porous skeleton materials (CSMs) often exhibit low structural integrity. Furthermore, when a significant proportion of PCMs is incorporated into the composite, the thermal contraction of the molded skeletal structure at elevated temperatures may cause the expulsion of liquid PCMs. This phenomenon significantly heightens the risk of PCMs volatilization and adversely impacts the performance during subsequent thermal cycling processes. As a result, researchers have proposed that porous skeletal materials should initially be fabricated through cold pressing and hot sintering, after which liquid salts can be introduced via straightforward immersion or vacuum infiltration. Since the fabrication of the skeletal materials is independent of the subsequent immersion or infiltration processes, the sintering technique is unconstrained by the melting points of the PCMs in terms of both time and spatial considerations. As a result, the skeletal material produced in this manner not only satisfies densification criteria but also possesses the requisite structural strength.

Liu et al. [\[42](#page-30-0)] fabricated CPCMs with interconnected pore structures by incorporating SS as the PCMs and using steel slag-veinstone-based porous ceramics as the structural backbone. Their study demonstrated that the abundant interconnected pores within the backbone facilitated efficient molten salt flow under full load conditions. In addition, the skeleton's high porosity and strong structural integrity played a key role in boosting its resistance to cyclic failure, while simultaneously enhancing its thermal performance during thermal cycling. However, this method has several limitations, including high energy consumption and the risk of molten salt loss or decomposition. To mitigate these challenges, a more energy-efficient cold sintering approach was utilized. Experiments demonstrated that NaNO3 could be effectively encapsulated within a cold-sintered MgO framework [\[43](#page-30-0)], resulting in CPCMs with outstanding mechanical properties, chemical stability, and thermal performance. In contrast to conventional melt impregnation techniques, the hybrid sintering technique can accommodate both mesoporous and microporous structures, thereby enhancing the adsorption capacity and stability of the system. However, despite its benefits, the hybrid sintering approach necessitates a more intricate fabrication process and imposes higher demands on both the backbone material and the PCMs. Therefore, ongoing research is essential to develop innovative preparation techniques that are well-suited for CPCMs.

## *2.2. Preparation of other CPCMs*

The most commonly used encapsulation techniques for CPCMs are spray-drying techniques (physical methods), coagulation (physicochemical), and in situ and interfacial polymerization (chemical) spraydrying. However, for molten salt CPCMs, the sol-gel method stands out as a promising approach for nano/microencapsulation because it is affordable and relatively easy to implement.

The material encapsulated during the sol-gel synthesis process [[44,45](#page-30-0)] is referred to as the core or active component, whereas the material surrounding it is known as the shell. It is worth remarking [\[46](#page-30-0)] that, when encapsulating PCMs, an increase in the total surface area of the capsule typically results in a reduction in the wall thickness of the capsule, provided the shell-to-core ratio is kept constant. This alteration can impact the overall performance and efficiency of the encapsulation. However, a larger specific surface area may also enhance the heat transfer between the PCMs and its surroundings. Consequently, it is crucial to take into account both the package ratio and the total surface area when designing PCMs encapsulation for a given application. Striking an optimal balance between these factors can improve the TES system's performance and its ability to release heat effectively, ensuring that the encapsulated PCMs offers both adequate capacity and efficient heat transfer.

# **3. Classification of molten salt CPCMs**

A diverse array of carriers is employed for encapsulating molten salt CPCMs, which can be categorized based on raw materials into carbon materials, oxides, polymers, and others. With the in-depth research on MSPCMs, their properties including TC, energy storage density, and load-carrying capacity have become one of the focuses of scholars. Meanwhile, other CPCMs have been extensively investigated to achieve the fulfillment of superior energy storage, stability and TC.

# *3.1. Carbon-based /molten salt CPCMs*

Carbon skeletons, as a new class of functional materials, have garnered considerable research interest for their ability to enhance the properties of PCMs. These materials are characterized by high structural stability, excellent TC, and chemical inertness, enabling them to provide strong support and efficient thermal conduction channels for PCMs. As a result, carbon skeletons enhance both the TC and thermal cycling stability of PCMs. Additionally, by employing techniques such as surface functionalization and nano-modulation, the properties of carbon skeleton can be further optimized to extend the thermal cycling lifespan and improve the overall stability of PCMs. Despite the significant potential of carbon CSMs in augmenting PCMs performance, several challenges remain to be addressed. Notably, the selection of appropriate carbon CSMs with judicious structural design and surface functionalization is crucial for achieving optimal compatibility with various PCMs.

#### *3.1.1. Graphene*

Graphene has attracted considerable interest because of its remarkable properties, including low density, high porosity, excellent TC, and chemical stability. To fully leverage graphene as a support material for PCMs, much of the current research has concentrated on enhancing its mechanical properties and improving its TC.

The mechanical properties of graphene can be significantly enhanced through a variety of approaches, including modifications to its intrinsic properties, the design of porous network structures, and the incorporation of hybrid materials. Qi et al. [\[47](#page-30-0)] proposed functionalization modifications to adjust the layer spacing of graphene, which effectively created heat transfer pathways within the PCMs (Fig. 2a). The results revealed that the hydroxylation process effectively expanded the interlayer spacing of graphene, thereby enhancing its dispersion within MSPCMs. This structural modification facilitated a more efficient heat transfer in the graphene-infused molten salts. Additionally, the incorporation of hydroxylated graphene significantly enhanced the TC of the MSPCMs compared to pure graphene. Similarly, Yu et al. [[48\]](#page-30-0) discovered that the formation of a graphene sandwich structure effectively restricted the motion of ions in a simulated MSPCMs system. Their molecular dynamics (MD) simulations showed that this structure decreased the self-diffusion coefficient and increased the shear viscosity, suggesting that the graphene sandwich could enhance the stability and performance of MSPCMs by modulating their ionic behavior. Notably, the sandwich structure led to an average TC increase of 32.51 %, with a maximum enhancement of up to 51.37 %. By the reactive molten salt method, Xin et al. [[49\]](#page-30-0) constructed eutectic reactive molten salt systems with three-dimensional porous graphene (NS-3DPG). The experimental findings revealed that the CPCMs incorporating a three-dimensional porous architecture demonstrated markedly enhanced stability. This structural configuration is believed to contribute to the material's superior mechanical integrity and thermal performance, thereby ensuring greater reliability and prolonged durability throughout repeated thermal cycles.

Additionally, it was noted that an optimal doping ratio could notably enhance both the TC and latent heat capacity of CPCMs. Lyu et al. [\[50](#page-30-0)] developed CPCMs by doping graphene nanosheets (GNS) at concentrations varying from 0.5 to 3.0 wt%. In comparison to pure NaNO3, the TC of the GNS@NaNO3 composite increased by up to 245 %. However, it is crucial to highlight that as the doping concentration rises, the phase change properties of GNS@NaNO3 tend to deteriorate. Consequently, careful selection of the doping ratio is essential to maximize TC while minimizing any adverse effects on the phase transition enthalpy. Ruse et al. [\[51](#page-30-0)] enhanced the TC of the eutectic NaCl-KCl mixture by incorporating GNS via in situ stripping (Fig. 2b). This modification led to a more than tenfold increase in TC compared to the pure salt matrix. To further bolster the structural integrity of the CPCMs under external stresses, Li et al. [\[52](#page-30-0)] synthesized graphene/MnCO3 and carbon nanotubes/MnCO3. Notably, graphene, with its flake-like morphology, provided greater coverage of MnCO3 than carbon nanotubes. This

characteristic facilitated superior fusion among the particles during charging and discharging cycles, thereby enhancing the stability of the composite and preventing structural collapse and pulverization.

# *3.1.2. Carbon nanotube*

As an important allotrope of carbon, carbon nanotubes are nanomaterials with relatively high TC, ranging from 1100 to 7000 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . Consequently, incorporating carbon nanotubes into molten salts to create Nano-CPCMs has garnered considerable attention.

Tao et al. [[53\]](#page-30-0) employed the solution evaporation technique to fabricate binary carbonate/single-walled carbon nanotube CPCMs. The experimental findings indicated that single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT) with their columnar morphology, were the most effective additive for enhancing TC, achieving an improvement of up to 56.98 %. Yu et al. [\[54](#page-30-0)] explored the impact of charged SWCNT on CPCMs, particularly those based on KNO3-SWCNT. Their experimental findings demonstrated notable increases in both TC and specific heat capacity as the mass fraction of SWCNTs increased. Subsequently, Zhao et al. [\[55](#page-30-0)] dispersed multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) into NaF-NaCl-Na2CO3 base salt matrix. Although the latent heat of phase transition for the CPCMs was lower than that of the base salt, the introduction of 1 wt % MWCNTs led to a significant enhancement in TC, which increased by up to 23.67 %. It is demonstrated that for molten salt CPCMs, MWCNTs outperform single-walled carbon nanotubes.

To predict the TC of CPCMs more accurately, Yuan et al. [\[56](#page-30-0)] utilized the finite volume method. This approach was used to calculate the effective TC of molten salts reinforced with carbon nanotube composites based on the TC and thermal resistance of carbon nanotubes combined with the three-dimensional random rotational structure. Liu et al. [\[57](#page-30-0)] fabricated CPCMs using both the melt and two-step processing techniques, examining how the preparation method influenced the thermophysical characteristics of the resulting materials. Their findings indicated that while both methods offer distinct advantages depending on the specific type of CPCMs, the melt process generally results in a higher TC compared to the two-step method.

#### *3.1.3. EG*

The superior TC of carbon-based materials is essential for improving the heat transfer properties of TES materials. EG, characterized by its unique porous, worm-like structure, is among the most commonly widely employed carbon materials for enhancing the TC of thermal storage systems. This material facilitates the adsorption of PCMs into its porous network through capillary forces and surface tension.

Xiao et al. [[58\]](#page-30-0) prepared CPCMs of nitrate/EG by a simple ultrasonic solution method. Following ultrasonication, the EG was disintegrated into nanoscale slices. At an EG concentration of 2.0 wt%, the resulting sample exhibited a specific heat of 2.600 J/(g⋅K), a thermal diffusivity of 0.930 mm<sup>2</sup> /s, and a TC of 4.884 W/(m⋅K), which was 2.15 times greater than that of the binary molten salt. In another investigation, Lu et al. [[59\]](#page-30-0) explored the impact of incorporating EG into ternary nitrate systems, revealing that the peak temperature of the CPCMs remained relatively stable. However, EG possesses an inherent characteristic in TC, which may influence the anisotropic behavior of the CPCMs. Based on this, Ren et al. [\[60](#page-30-0)] carried out a detailed study into the complex structural characteristics and anisotropic TC of molten salt/EG. Their study revealed that EG micro-fragments could self-assemble into hierarchically structured porous materials, exhibiting multilayer stacking characteristics. Moreover, the anisotropic TC was observed to increase as the stacking density of the EG layers increased (Fig. 3a).

In order to meet various heat storage temperature needs, researchers often combine different inorganic molten salts. Chloride salts, owing to their high thermal storage capacity, offer a distinct advantage in the domain of TES. Li et al. [[61\]](#page-30-0) found that the newly synthesized ternary mixed molten salts have lower phase transition temperatures, higher latent heat values, and greater thermal decomposition temperatures compared to LiNO3-NaNO3. Meanwhile, the supercooling of LiNO3- NaNO3-NaCl was approximately half of LiNO3-NaNO3. However, chlorides cause corrosion of TES system components. To address this issue, the use of a barrier between the salt and the system has proven to be an effective strategy for mitigation. With this in mind, Zhong et al. [\[62](#page-30-0)] developed three composite materials consisting of EG and binary molten salts, creating porous, non-homogeneous CPCMs through the solution impregnation method. The results demonstrated that the corrosion induced by chloride salts could be effectively mitigated by utilizing EG as a carrier. Xu et al. [\[63](#page-30-0)] found no chemical reaction between EG and

the eutectic LiNO3-KCl-NaNO3 mixture. Furthermore, a linear correlation between TC and compression density of CPCMs was observed. Huang et al. [\[64](#page-30-0)] examined the performance of LiNO3-KCl/EG composites, demonstrating that EG-based TES materials maintain remarkable stability and reliable functionality over prolonged periods. Li et al. [[65\]](#page-30-0) prepared ternary CPCMs (Fig. 3b) by using the static mixed melting method and heating and stirring. The findings revealed that the latent heat of the ternary NaNO3-LiNO3-NaCl mixture exceeded that of pure NaNO3 by 59.4 J/g, corresponding to a 34.8 % increase. When the EG content reached 20 %, the TC of CPCMs was significantly enhanced, reaching 5.017 W/m⋅K, which represented a 3.72-fold improvement compared to the pure PCMs. Meanwhile, it was worth noting that when EG-based CPCMs were applied to latent heat energy storage systems, they typically require compression into blocks with predetermined shapes [[66\]](#page-30-0), allowing the CPCMs to be adapted to the application situation, while improving their TC and volumetric heat storage density (Fig. 3c).

Numerous scholars have investigated the impact of particle size and content of EG on heat transfer capability. Li et al. [[67\]](#page-30-0) examined how variations in the particle size of EG influence the thermal property of NaNO3-NaCl/EG composites. The adsorption capacities for NaCl varied with different EG particle sizes. Specifically, a decrease in particle size resulted in enhanced specific volume, internal porosity, and adsorption capacity. Notably, 80 mesh EG exhibited an extraordinary 1003 % adsorption of NaCl. Furthermore, the adsorption rate of EG was also contingent upon the dimensions of the TES material. The TC of the composite material exhibited a slight increase as the EG particle size decreased, as smaller particles contributed to a denser network structure and consequently a superior heat transfer capacity. Tian et al. [\[68](#page-30-0)] synthesized CPCMs through the melting and impregnation of eutectic NaCl-CaCl2. Their findings indicated that the binary eutectic chlorides were confined to at least 7 wt% EG content without leakage during melting. Moreover, the specific heat capacity of the CPCMs obviously diminished as EG content increased. Ren et al. [\[69\]](#page-30-0) introduced a novel approach for developing SSPCMs by utilizing low-cost binary nitrateimpregnated EG. This process involves a two-step method, consisting of impregnation followed by sintering. The prepared CPCMs suffered from severe thermal expansion, cracking, and leakage when the EG mass content was 1, 2, 3 and 4 wt% ([Fig. 3d](#page-6-0)), due to insufficient capillary forces to stabilize the liquid PCMs at lower EG levels. However, at 7 wt% EG content, the composites exhibited excellent shape stability, with TC reaching 5.659 W/(m⋅K). This value is nearly 7.3 times higher than that of the eutectic binary nitrate. Wang et al. [[70\]](#page-30-0)used sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) as PCMs, with EG and modified CFA serving as TC enhancers and ceramic matrices, respectively. It was shown that the thermal storage density of the CPCMs decreases slightly with EG loading (Fig. 4a). Huang et al. [\[71](#page-30-0)] similarly demonstrated that the presence of EG did not cause a significant change in the phase transition temperature of PCMs. However, the latent heat of phase transition of CPCMs decreased from 178.10 J/g to 142.41 J/g, which was influenced by the mass content of EG. Additionally, a substantial body of research has examined the role of molding pressure in the thermal properties of heat storage materials, revealing that molding pressure did not significantly influence the heat transfer characteristics [[72\]](#page-30-0).

To enhance specific heat, TC, and overall TES performance, considerable research has been dedicated to the development of molten salt nano-composite, achieved through the integration of EG. Hou et al. [\[73](#page-30-0)] explored the use of MgCl2-KCl-NaCl as PCMs owing to its elevated specific heat capacity and latent heat value. They incorporated MgO and EG into the PCM. Their findings indicated that a formulation of 15 wt% EG and 40 wt% MgO was nearly ideal for enhancing TC while reducing energy density. In this composite, the TC reached 8.86 W⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> ⋅K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> . In separate studies, Yu et al. [[74\]](#page-30-0) prepared shape-stabilized nano-SiO2/ NaNO3-KNO3/EG complexes using an aqueous solution combined with ultrasonic oscillation. The incorporation of SiO2 notably augmented the specific surface area of the molten salt, resulting in enhanced TC of the CPCMs. When the mass fraction of EG was set at 15 %, the average specific heat of the CPCMs was observed to be 1.69 times greater than that of the nitrate salts. Ji et al. [[75\]](#page-30-0) utilized a solution impregnation method to prepare EG composites, enabling the effective adsorption of EG onto the PCMs. They then created CPCMs of NaNO3-KNO3/EG/Al2O3 through a process of mixing, cold pressing, and sintering. Experimental results showed that salt bridges were formed between the salt and Al2O3 particles during sintering at elevated temperatures, which overcame the swelling effect of EG and promoted the rigidity and densification of the composite structure. In addition, the PCMs can be embedded in the laminar structure of EG and the gaps between the Al2O3 particles, which effectively improves the leakage resistance and compressive strength of the CPCMs. Yu et al. [\[76](#page-30-0)] employed a mechanical dispersion technique to fabricate SiO2/MgCl2-NaCl-KCl/EG materials (Fig. 4b). The incorporation of nanoparticles resulted in a uniform distribution within the chloride salt, leading to the formation of a nanostructure. This modification considerably strengthened the interaction between the chloride salt and EG, thereby enhancing the overall efficacy of the composite materials. Ran et al. [[77\]](#page-31-0) utilized uniaxial compression technique to fabricate NaCl-KCl-MgCl2/α-Al2O3/EG CPCMs for solar thermal storage systems (Fig. 4c). Among them, α-Al2O3 served as the supporting matrix material, effectively preventing the leakage of the chloride salts. The incorporation of 15 wt% EG led to a substantial improvement in TC, increasing it over six-fold. In parallel, Yu et al. [\[78](#page-31-0)] found that the TC increased with increasing content of SiO2. However, further increases to 2 wt% and 3 wt% resulted in decreased thermal conductivity, highlighting a non-linear relationship that suggests complex interactions within the material matrix. Additionally, unlike organic PCMs that readily mix with EG, molten salts are hydrophilic, which complicates their adsorption by EG. As a result, composites formed from molten salts and EG often struggle to achieve a consistent distribution of the molten salt within EG matrix. To overcome this limitation, Chen et al. [\[79\]](#page-31-0) enhanced the hydrophilicity of EG by coating it with hydrophilic SiO2. Their findings revealed that EG modified with 15 wt% SiO2 exhibited the highest hydrophilicity, resulting in a saturated adsorption capacity of 82.9 % for ternary nitratebased PCMs. This was nearly double the adsorption capacity of unmodified EG, which was 48.2 %. This modification significantly improves the compatibility and distribution of molten salts in the composite materials.

#### *3.2. Silicon carbide/molten salt CPCMs*

Ceramic materials, particularly porous silicon carbide (SiC), exhibit a wealth of remarkable capability, including excellent TC, hightemperature oxidation resistance, and exceptional chemical stability. Consequently, porous SiC emerges as a compelling candidate for use as a support material in MSPCMs.

Wang et al. [\[81](#page-31-0)] demonstrates significant enhancements in the heat storage density of CPCMs by introducing 1 % Nano-SiC into carbonates. This modification increased the heat storage density from 542.7 to 990.7 J/g, attributed to the development of nanostructures that improved the surface energy and specific heat capacity of the CPCMs. Further research highlighted that both the content and particle size of SiC influence the heat transfer coefficient [[33\]](#page-30-0).Specifically, the TC of the CPCMs was found to be directly proportional to SiC content and inversely proportional to particle size within a certain temperature range. Yu et al. [[82\]](#page-31-0) used MD simulations to investigate the thermal properties of LiCl composites. The findings revealed that the incorporation of SiC resulted in a 2.43 % average increase in TC and a 5.4 % rise in shear viscosity. Furthermore, they observed an 8.85 % reduction in the enthalpy of melting, while the specific heat capacity improved by 0.8 % over the examined temperature range. Li et al. [\[83](#page-31-0)] prepared CPCMs with SiC ceramic honeycomb (SCH) as the backbone structure by using the vacuum infiltration method. Their results demonstrated that molten salt was successfully embedded and uniformly distributed within the porous walls formed by SCH. This uniform distribution significantly enhanced both the storage and release rates of the CPCMs, improving their overall thermal performance.

To gain a deeper understanding of the heat transfer behavior during the phase transition of MSPCMs, Zhou et al. [[84\]](#page-31-0) employed MD simulations to investigate the heat transfer mechanisms of SiC as the backbone material during the phase transition of binary chloride salts. Their findings demonstrated that the introduction of porous SiC frameworks created an auxiliary heat conduction path within the composite, which significantly enhances the TC of the CPCMs. Zhang et al. [[85\]](#page-31-0) carried out visualized experiments in which SiC ceramic foam was integrated with a SS mixture. The results demonstrated a 40.3 % increase in energy storage efficiency compared to pure SS. Additionally, their model simulations indicated that heat transfer in SS is primarily governed by natural convection, whereas in CPCMs, heat transfer is predominantly controlled by heat conduction. Furthermore, the study showed that doping nanoparticles in molten salt could improve its thermophysical properties. Specifically, CPCMs demonstrated remarkable thermophysical improvements when LiNO3/NaCl molten salts were co-encapsulated with layered ultralight SiC foams and SiO2 nanoparticles [\[86](#page-31-0)]. Among them, when the volume fraction of SiC foam was 3 %, the TC could be increased by 159 % and the solar energy absorption could be increased by 200 % compared with the pure LiNO3/NaCl.

However, despite the advantages of SiC porous materials in reducing leakage issues associated with MSPCMs, they still face limitations. The open structure of these materials poses challenges in maintaining thermal stability, which could affect their performance and longevity under operational conditions. Further research may be needed to enhance the design and stability of SiC structures in practical applications.

## *3.3. Oxide-based /molten salt CPCMs*

Although metal oxides typically exhibit lower TC than pure metals, their superior chemical stability and advantageous properties, such as low production costs, have nonetheless attracted significant interest. Among these materials, TiO2, CuO, and Al2O3 have been recognized as effective carrier materials, owing to their high specific surface areas and exceptional thermal stability.

Myers Jr. et al. [[87\]](#page-31-0) found that incorporating small amounts of CuO nanoparticles into nitrates enhanced both the diffusivity and conductivity of molten salts. Notably, CuO demonstrated superior stability in highly oxidizing nitrate environments when compared to conventional conductivity-enhancing additives like graphite or metals. In a separate investigation, Son et al. [\[32](#page-30-0)] explored the thermal properties of CPCMs made from LiNO3 and porous CuO nanowires arranged on a folded grid. The introduction of CuO at a volume fraction of 7 % led to a remarkable 6.7-fold increase in TC relative to pure LiNO3, indicating the composite's excellent thermal transport capabilities. Furthermore, the porous CuO nanowires provided significant corrosion resistance against LiNO3, enhancing the durability of the CPCMs. The heat storage characteristics of these CPCMs were also impressive, with thermal charging and discharging rates 1.3 times faster than those of pure LiNO3.

The specific heat capacity represents a critical property because it directly determines the amount of heat energy the substance could store for a given temperature change. While molten salts exhibit moderate specific heat capacities, they have proven particularly effective for TES systems, especially when employed as PCMs in CSP plants. Recent advancements in CPCMs have demonstrated that the thermosphysical properties of molten salts could be further enhanced through nanoparticle dispersion. Shin et al. [[88\]](#page-31-0) demonstrated that dispersing Al2O3 nanoparticles in eutectic carbonates (Li2CO3-K2CO3) notably improve the specific heat capacity of the CPCMs. In a comparable investigation, Hu et al. [\[89](#page-31-0)] explored how nanoparticles at different mass fractions influenced the specific heat capacity of CPCMs based on binary nitrates. The results demonstrated that the specific heat capacity of the nano-CPCMs rose by 1.9 % to 8.3 % as the nanoparticle mass fraction was elevated. Song et al. [[90\]](#page-31-0) synthesized CPCMs by incorporating SiO2 nanoparticles into a NaNO3-KNO3-LiNO3-Ca(NO3)2, noting that the

specific heat increase was contingent upon both stirring speed and duration. Zhang et al. [\[91](#page-31-0)] examined the effects of nanoparticle dispersion on quaternary nitrate PCMs, observing that variations in ultrasonication time influenced the degree of dispersion, which subsequently influenced the experimental outcomes of specific heat capacity. Furthermore, Shin et al. [[92](#page-31-0)] introduced SiO2 into the Li2CO3-K2CO3 system, which resulted in a 5–15 % increase in specific heat, thermal diffusivity enhancement of 25–28 %, and a corresponding rise in TC of 37–47 % across the measured temperature range. Seo et al. [\[93](#page-31-0)] investigated the influence of SiO2 size on the specific heat of ternary nitrate eutectic. Their results showed that the addition of 1.0 wt% SiO2 increased the specific heat capacity by 13–16 % while simultaneously enhancing both thermal diffusivity and TC. Interestingly, the study revealed that nanoparticles of different sizes had no significant impact on the specific heat capacity. In related studies, researchers [[94,95\]](#page-31-0) have suggested a potential mechanism to enhance specific heat capacity. However, this approach remains impractical for real-world applications and is subject to considerable debate. This is primarily due to the fact that, in most instances, the specific heat of oxides is lower than that of alkali salts. Consequently, the influence of oxides on molten salt-based nanofluids requires further investigation.

These comprehensive findings demonstrate that while molten salts possess sufficient thermal storage capacity for commercial CSP applications, their performance could be further optimized through nanoparticle engineering. However, it is important to note that such enhancements may involve potential trade-offs in material stability and processing complexity.

## *3.4. Other porous/molten salt CPCMs*

High-temperature stabilized CPCMs utilizing porous materials as a backbone have garnered significant interest due to their unique pore structures. These structures provide ample attachment sites for molten salts, effectively preventing leakage.

Diatomite is well recognized as a dependable porous support material. Soleimanpour et al. [\[96](#page-31-0)] developed a diatomite-supported material via a two-step process, resulting in novel SSPCMs ([Fig. 5](#page-10-0)a). The experimental results showed that SSPCMs showcased superior stability and remarkable physical properties, particularly with a NaNO3 content of 62.91 wt%. Li et al. [\[97](#page-31-0)] employed sodium hydroxide solution as a sintering additive to successfully prepare molten salt-diatomaceous earth composites by cold sintering process. This approach allowed for sintering at a significantly lower temperature of just 180 ◦C, which marked a notable reduction in the sintering temperature compared to the conventional thermal sintering method. This process not only contributed to excellent mechanical strength but also ensured superior thermal cycling performance, further emphasizing the advantages of using diatomite as a support material in CPCMs ([Fig. 5b](#page-10-0)).

However, the TC of high-temperature stabilized CPCMs still requires further enhancement. Xu et al. [\[98](#page-31-0)] prepared diatomite-based CPCMs by cold-pressing followed by sintering. Their findings demonstrated that diatomite exhibits strong chemical compatibility with NaNO3, allowing it to retain up to 70 % of NaNO3 in its structure, with a total energy density of 649 J/g and a TC of 0.5 W/m⋅K. The degree of deformation in the sintered products was observed to increase with higher salt content ([Fig. 5](#page-10-0)c). Meanwhile, the incorporation of graphite as a TC reinforcement significantly enhanced the TC of the salt-based CPCMs, with a sixfold increase achieved by adding 10 wt% graphite. However, it is important to note that when the support material is compressed and bonded by solidified molten salt, particularly when the load exceeds the melting point of the molten salt, the structural and mechanical properties of the CPCMs may be compromised. In extreme cases, leakage of the molten salt could cause structural damage, ultimately impacting the TES and release efficiency. Based on this, Jiang et al. [[99\]](#page-31-0) developed a novel porous ceramic skeleton (M-DC4) by modifying diatomite with CaCO3 ([Fig. 5d](#page-10-0)). Through modification, the molten salt loading capacity of diatomaceous earth-based ceramics was enhanced to 58.67 wt%, representing an increase of approximately 10 wt% compared to the unmodified diatomaceous earth-based ceramics (UM-DC). Additionally, NaNO3/M-DC4 composites demonstrated superior heat storage density and thermal efficiency across a broad temperature range of 130–330 ◦C, making them highly suitable for TES and TER applications. Wang et al. [[100](#page-31-0)] further investigated the performance of the M-DC4 ceramic in TES by utilizing amino montmorillonite (NH2-MT) to encapsulate eutectic nitrate ([Fig. 6](#page-11-0)a). The results showed that this approach not only strengthened the thermal efficiency of the composite but also notably enhanced the structural stability and leakage resistance of the supporting material. This suggests that the modified structure effectively accommodates higher molten salt loadings, leading to improved

performance in TES applications.

In addition, layered porous materials have emerged as a promising solution [[101](#page-31-0)]. Compared to single-pore size materials, these materials offer a wealth of reaction sites and capillary forces through their micropores and mesopores. Simultaneously, the interconnecting network formed by macropores enhances the material's transport capacity, yielding high specific surface area, low density, and excellent environmental adaptability. Qian et al. [[102](#page-31-0)] synthesized LiNO3/diatomite (fs-PCM2) using a straightforward mixing and sintering method. The diatomaceous earth disks were arranged in an orderly mesoporous array, with the central region predominantly consisting of macropores. Higher magnification microscopic observations further revealed a nested secondary mesoporous structure within the macropores. This multi-layered pore synergy enabled the material to achieve a high lithium nitrate loading rate of 60 %, while maintaining excellent leakage resistance in the molten salt state. After several thermal cycles, the material demonstrated stable chemical compatibility, significantly reduced subcooling, and improved thermal reliability. It is noteworthy that the current application of multi-scale porous materials is mostly concentrated in organic systems. Consequently, the use of multi-stage porous media to stabilize molten salt presents a promising research avenue, albeit one that faces a series of critical challenges.

Other porous materials, like expanded vermiculite (EV) have also attracted attention due to a range of properties such as extremely low density. Diaz Herrezuelo et al. [[105](#page-31-0)] demonstrated that molten NaNO3 could be successfully impregnated into 3D porous EV, achieving a maximum encapsulation capacity of 87 %. Li et al. [[106](#page-31-0)] discovered that EV, when used as a carrier for NaNO3, exhibited an adsorption capacity of 734.6 % and a latent heat of 157.2 J/g. These results suggest that EV could store a large amount of energy, making it a promising material for use in TES systems. To enhance the TC of the CPCMs, SiC was introduced, leading to a remarkable 4.5-fold increase in TC at a SiC content of 20 %. Additionally, expanded perlite (EP) exhibited excellent adsorption

properties, attributed to its foamy honeycomb internal structure, positioning it a promising candidate for TES applications. Li et al. [\[107](#page-31-0)] used mechanical mixing and direct melt techniques to fabricate CPCMs. NaNO3 was uniformly encapsulated within the three-dimensional network structure of EP. Following heat treatment at 500 ◦C for 2 h, the shape-stabilized EP exhibited an impressive adsorption capacity of up to 114.07 %. In a separate study, Li et al. [[103](#page-31-0)] employed halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) as a ceramic support material for CPCMs. By utilizing cold compression and hot sintering techniques, they fabricated molten salt CPCMs. HNTs, with their hollow tubular structure, provided increased surface area, allowing for better accommodation of molten salts. This structural characteristic facilitated the formation of dense composites and greatly improved the thermal stability of the nitrate salts. Notably, a 5:5 mass ratio of salt to HNTs yielded the optimal shape stability (Fig. 6b). The small pore sizes of these porous matrix CPCMs effectively utilized capillary forces and surface tension to prevent leakage, underscoring the potential of various porous materials in enhancing TES systems. When developing CPCMs for long-term energy storage, ensuring the chemical and physical compatibility of the components is crucial for achieving reliable and durable energy storage. In

this context, the researchers [[108](#page-31-0)] selected lightweight MgO as the matrix material and EG as the TCEM to create a CPCMs based on a NaNO2-NaNO3 eutectic mixture. This combination aims to improve heat recovery and transfer efficiency. Microstructural analysis of the CPCMs demonstrated strong thermal stability, good latent heat retention, and effective TC after undergoing thermal cycling in air up to 350 ◦C. Furthermore, Gr´egoire et al. highlighted the significant potential of solid-solid phase transitions in inorganic salt compounds for TES, suggesting that these materials could offer a reliable solution. Solid-solid PCMs present numerous advantages over traditional solid-liquid PCMs, such as eliminating leakage problems, negating the need for additional packaging, and experiencing minimal volume changes during phase transitions. Given these benefits, future research could focus on developing novel TES materials based on the solid-solid phase transitions of salt compounds. Regarding open-pore ceramic skeletons, Liu et al. [\[104\]](#page-31-0) developed multifunctional CPCMs using sol-gel and thermal sintering methods ([Fig. 6](#page-11-0)c), achieving a remarkable 92 % pore filling with PCMs. The enthalpies of phase transitions for these CPCMs ranged from 140 to 186.0 J/g, with TC spanning from 31.8 to 52.63 W/m⋅K. To further improve performance, the surfaces of porous aluminum nitride (AlN) were modified with titanium nitride (TiN) nanoparticles, resulting in a substantial increase in the average solar absorptivity from 70 % to 90 %. This innovative approach illustrates the effectiveness of combining diverse materials and techniques to enhance TES applications.

#### *3.5. Capsule/molten salt CPCMs*

In CSP systems, molten salt plays a crucial role as both a heat transfer and storage medium. However, it faces challenges, including low TC and corrosiveness. To overcome these challenges, researchers are actively exploring methods to enhance current phase change heat storage technologies. Among the various approaches, capsule encapsulation technology has emerged as a highly promising solution. This method involves creating a protective shell around the PCMs, thereby shielding them from adverse environmental effects. Additionally, it facilitates the controlled exchange of energy and materials between the PCMs and their surrounding environment, optimizing both the stability and efficiency of the TES system.

The KNO3@SiO2 microcapsules synthesized by Pethurajan et al. [[113](#page-31-0)] demonstrated a 50 % enhancement in thermal conductivity, achieving an encapsulation rate of 80.94 % for CPCMs. However, the significant solubility of molten salt in water poses challenges for encapsulation using traditional methods like the sol-gel technique. To address this research gap, Zhang et al. [\[114\]](#page-31-0) utilized a water-limited solgel technique to prepare KNO3@SiO2 microcapsules. This approach resulted in an encapsulation efficiency of 95.2 %, while also ensuring exceptional thermal stability over multiple cycles. Furthermore, Li et al. [[109](#page-31-0)] harnessed the significant water solubility of molten salts to synthesize stable binary molten salt nano-capsules through emulsification and sol-gel methods (Fig. 7a). The effectiveness of the nanoencapsulation technique was demonstrated through the successful encapsulation of individual nitrates as well as a range of binary salts within silica shells.

To enhance the homogeneity of the shell material around the core, surfactants are often utilized. However, if the decomposition temperature of these surfactants is lower than the melting temperature of typical molten salts, it can lead to the formation of pores in the capsule, compromising its structural integrity. Based on this, Park et al. [[110](#page-31-0)] introduced a surfactant-free microencapsulation technique [\(Fig. 7b](#page-12-0)), achieving a 9.9 % increase in TC at 25 ◦C compared to pure salt, with a maximum encapsulation rate of approximately 69.4 %. In an attempt to further improve the insufficient encapsulation rate, Zeng et al. [[111](#page-31-0)] developed high-temperature molten salt macro-capsules utilizing a double-shell coating strategy. This design incorporated graphite as the inner layer and a ceramic material as the outer layer ([Fig. 7](#page-12-0)c), which significantly improved thermal storage properties. In addition, by adjusting the core-shell ratio, such as increasing the size of the capsule and reducing the thickness of the shell, the heat storage density of the carbonate capsule was further improved. This strategy aids in optimizing the overall TES efficiency, showcasing its potential for effective thermal management in high-temperature CSP systems. To address the critical heat transfer challenges in CSP applications, Mao et al. [[115](#page-31-0)] explored a two-layer configuration by combining nitrate-encapsulated PCMs capsules with rock spheres, which improved operational efficiency and minimized heat transfer temperature differences. This approach directly addresses the operational needs of CSP plants, where minimizing temperature gradients during charging and discharging cycles is essential for improving overall system performance. In addition, LiNO3/NaCl was encapsulated within a SiO2 shell [\[112\]](#page-31-0), effectively isolating the PCMs from moisture in the environment. This configuration demonstrated excellent humidity resistance under varying conditions ([Fig. 7](#page-12-0)d), maintaining 99.7 % of its initial phase change performance after 50 thermal cycles. The hydrophobic nature and high-temperature stability (up to 480.6 ◦C) of the SiO2 shell ensured its compatibility with CSP systems operating in cyclic thermal and humid environments.

Arconeda et al. [\[116\]](#page-31-0) investigated the extensive encapsulation of NaCl as PCMs for high-temperature TES in CSP systems. The primary aim was to enhance thermal stability and mitigate salt evaporation during thermal cycling. The study employed a sol-gel method to form a protective coating around NaCl particles. After five thermal cycles between 25–850 ◦C, the SiO2-coated products failed to maintain their original cylindrical shape. Conversely, the dense TiO2 coating demonstrated superior thermal stability, exhibiting minimal reduction in latent heat across five cycles. This approach underscores the potential of largescale NaCl encapsulation for high-temperature heat storage applications. However, further optimization of the encapsulation structure is necessary to enhance the energy storage capacity over multiple cycles. This development marks a significant step toward enhancing the reliability and efficiency of CSP systems in diverse operational conditions.

In summary, these advances in capsule technology highlight the significant potential for the effective encapsulation of MSPCMs, improving their performance and stability for various applications.

# *3.6. Solid waste/molten salt CPCMs*

As the demand for resources escalates, the generation of solid waste continues to increase significantly. Despite various initiatives, less than 30 % of this waste is currently being repurposed. Consequently, the advancement of CPCMs utilizing solid waste as CSMs has garnered considerable interest.

Xiong et al. [[117](#page-31-0)] successfully synthesized CPCMs through a combination of cold compression and hot sintering, utilizing municipal sludge incineration ash as the backbone material. These CPCMs demonstrated remarkable overall performance, exhibiting a TES capacity of 330.34 J/g and a peak TC of 1.04 W/(m⋅K), alongside exceptional mechanical strength and outstanding thermal stability. Jiang et al. [[118](#page-31-0)] prepared SSPCMs by modifying steel slag with another solid waste (CFA) and then microencapsulating it. The results indicated that phase

reconstruction greatly improved the compatibility of CFA-modified steel slag with SS. By optimizing the preparation conditions, they established the ideal composition for the skeleton material, with 30 % CFA. When processed at a high temperature of 1100 ◦C, the resulting SSPCMs exhibited outstanding performance. It demonstrated a phase change temperature of 653 ◦C and a latent heat of 139 J/g, indicating high efficiency in TES. Additionally, the SSPCMs displayed excellent cyclic stability. Notably, the TC reached 1.46 W/(m⋅K), representing an impressive 180 % increase over that of pure NaCl-KCl. Additionally, the compressive strength of the composites achieved a remarkable 119 MPa, surpassing that of other salt-based CPCMs.

Anagnostopoulos et al. [\[119\]](#page-31-0) successfully utilized red mud (RM) as the skeletal material in their composite matrix for adsorbing SS, achieving a maximum loading of 60 %. The resulting composites (RM-SS CPCMs) demonstrated an optimal energy storage density of 1396 MJ/m<sup>3</sup> and a high thermal diffusivity of 56.6 % within the temperature range of 25–400 ◦C. Furthermore, the TC of RM-SS CPCMs was enhanced by an average of 47.2 % compared to the pure molten salts. Anagnostopoulos et al. [\[120\]](#page-31-0) subsequently developed a composite phase change material (WFS CPCMs) by blending waste foundry sand (WFS) with NaNO3 and a proprietary additive, X, composed exclusively of natural substances such as clay. The function of the proprietary additive X was to enhance the structural integrity of the WFS CPCMs. Experimental findings revealed that the WFS CPCMs maintains structural stability within a temperature range up to 400 ◦C, with an optimal composition ratio of NaNO3:WFS:X = 0.6:0.3:0.1. Over a temperature span from 25 ◦C to 400 ◦C, the material demonstrates an energy storage density of 628 ± 27 kJ/kg and an average TC of 1.38 W/(m⋅K). In comparison to pure NaNO3, the WFS CPCMs not only exhibited superior mechanical strength but also a reduced coefficient of thermal expansion. However, the recycling of WFS remains limited, with its predominant use being in constructionrelated applications. Wang et al. [\[121\]](#page-31-0) employed a hybrid sintering method using gold tailings (GT) as a substrate to encapsulate SS, achieving a uniform distribution of the salt within the GT structure, with an encapsulation rate of 45 wt%. The gold tailings-based composites (GT-CPCM) exhibited a peak compressive strength of 36.66 MPa and showed excellent cycling stability.

In conclusion, the combination of MSPCMs and solid waste to create CPCMs presents an effective solution for waste heat recovery. This approach not only reduces the cost of CPCMs production but also contributes to environmental protection by transforming waste materials into valuable resources. Moreover, when molten salt composite materials reach the end of their operational lifespan, improper disposal can result in significant environmental pollution and resource wastage. As such, it is imperative to undertake rigorous research into the recycling technologies and sustainable disposal strategies for these materials. However, the field of molten salt composite material recycling and disposal remains in its nascent stages. Several researchers have highlighted that the recyclability of PCMs is contingent upon both the composition of the materials and the packaging technologies employed. Certain PCMs, particularly those derived from bio-based or renewable resources, may offer greater recycling potential due to their ability to decompose and be reprocessed into new PCMs products [[122](#page-31-0)]. Nonetheless, it is crucial to note that a definitive solution to this issue is yet to be established. Consequently, future research efforts should prioritize the development of efficient recycling methodologies, including the exploration of innovative packaging solutions that facilitate the separation of PCMs from their packaging materials.

#### **4. Technical comparison and optimization of enhancement strategies for CPCMs**

Although significant progress has been made in enhancing the TC of MSPCMs through strategies such as carbon-based materials, oxides, and porous ceramics, differences in efficiency, cost, and scalability directly impact the feasibility of engineering applications. This section conducts a systematic comparison (Tables 3 and 4) to analyze the core advantages and bottlenecks of various enhancement strategies. Based on this analysis, multi-objective optimization pathways are proposed to provide decision-making support for TES system design.

Carbon-based materials demonstrate significant application potential in medium-to-high-temperature MSPCMs TES systems due to their exceptional TC and structural design flexibility. Table 3 compares the key performance parameters of three typical carbon-based materials, graphene, carbon nanotubes, and EG. Among these, EG stands out with distinct advantages. Its raw material costs are extremely low, and TC can be increased by 7.3 times through cold pressing or sintering processes [[69\]](#page-30-0). However, its mechanical strength heavily depends on pore structure, necessitating composite designs to optimize both thermal networks and structural stability. Graphene achieves an order-of-magnitude breakthrough in TC enhancement, but the decline in phase change enthalpy after doping limits its application in large-scale medium-tohigh-temperature TES [[51\]](#page-30-0). Future efforts should focus on reducing costs through technologies like electrochemical exfoliation. Carbon nanotubes, benefiting from scalable chemical vapor deposition (CVD) production, offer better cost control than graphene, yet their engineering applications face bottlenecks due to complex dispersion processes (ultrasonication and surface functionalization).

From the perspectives of cost-effectiveness and scalability, EG, with its abundant natural graphite reserves and mature processing techniques (cost-controllable oxidation-expansion processes), emerges as the preferred material for medium-to-high-temperature TES systems. Carbon nanotubes show promise in constructing directional thermal networks, making them suitable for localized high-temperature scenarios requiring rapid heat transfer (concentrated solar power receivers). In contrast, graphene, constrained by high costs and performance tradeoffs, is better suited for high-value applications (aerospace thermal management). Future research should prioritize multi-scale composite designs to balance thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, and hightemperature stability, while advancing the industrial adoption of lowcost preparation technologies.

Beyond carbon-based materials, strategies such as oxide doping, porous ceramic encapsulation, capsule technology, and solid wastebased composites also hold distinct value. [Table 4](#page-15-0) systematically compares five enhancement strategies across efficiency, cost, and technological maturity, revealing their techno-economic differences and proposing optimization pathways. According to [Table 4](#page-15-0), the choice of enhancement strategies must align with target scenarios. For costeffectiveness, solid waste-based materials [[117](#page-31-0)] are preferred for large-scale TES systems due to their low costs and mature processes, despite limited TC improvements. For high performance, carbon-based materials (EG) and oxide doping (CuO/LiNO3) excel in TC enhancement (570–900 %) and rapid heat transfer, though cost-process complexity trade-offs remain [[51,69\]](#page-30-0). For environmental adaptability, capsule technology [[116](#page-31-0)] and porous ceramics [[86\]](#page-31-0) enhance material durability through physical isolation, yet scalable production and longterm stability require further resolution.

In summary, optimizing enhancement strategies requires integrating multi-scale composite designs to leverage material synergies and improve utilization efficiency. Concurrently, process innovations, such as developing continuous capsule encapsulation production lines (microfluidics), are critical for cost reduction. Additionally, standardized protocols for raw material screening and performance evaluation must be established for solid waste-based CPCMs to transition from laboratory research to engineering applications.

#### **5. Performance trade-offs of encapsulation methods**

Despite the considerable advancements in the heat storage performance of molten salt-based CPCMs through the aforementioned porous framework materials and packaging strategies, their practical applications remain in need of comprehensive optimization in terms of durability, efficiency, and manufacturability. This section aims to systematically evaluate the critical trade-offs associated with encapsulation techniques and explore potential avenues for future research.

To comprehensively evaluate the advantages and limitations of various packaging strategies, [Table 5](#page-15-0) presents key performance indicators, including durability, efficiency, manufacturability, and typical use cases. It becomes apparent that no single method satisfies all criteria universally. For instance, the porous skeleton packaging technique demonstrates exceptional performance under high-load conditions due to its straightforward process [[69\]](#page-30-0). However, its durability is contingent upon the sinter-resistant design, which may complicate the design and elevate costs in practical applications. The capsule encapsulation method excels in resistance to environmental degradation [\[111\]](#page-31-0), yet its intricate process limits large-scale applicability, presenting a potential bottleneck in mass production. Although the solid waste-based encapsulation method is cost-effective and sustainable, its relatively low performance restricts its use to cost-sensitive [[117](#page-31-0)], large-scale heat storage applications. These trade-offs underscore the necessity of tailoring optimization strategies to specific scenarios. For instance, in medium and high-temperature heat storage applications, the porous backbone encapsulation method may be more appropriate. Consequently, future research should focus on the development of packaging technologies characterized by high durability and efficiency, while streamlining the process flow to reduce manufacturing costs. Furthermore, the customization of packaging strategies for distinct application scenarios represents a crucial avenue for future research, aiming to achieve an optimal balance between performance and cost.

# **6. Energy conversion of molten salt CPCMs**

Traditional TES in PCMs relies on passive heat absorption, requiring the temperature of the heat source to surpass the melting point of the PCMs. This inherent limitation restricts the practical applicability of such systems. In contrast, functionally engineered PCMs, by exploiting their advanced material properties, demonstrate a versatile thermal response that enables multi-energy conversion. This capability allows for efficient TES and release in environments tailored to specific needs. Recent studies have focused extensively on these materials for their potential in photothermal and magnetothermal energy conversion, particularly in solar energy applications.

Herrezuelo et al. [[123](#page-31-0)] developed a highly porous, self-supporting 3D structure (3DTES) composed of 86 % porous activated carbon/ alumina as a carrier material. This 3DTES offers several advantages, including remarkable robustness, chemical compatibility, and lightness, along with a TES efficiency of 70 %, outstanding thermal stability, and an energy storage density of up to 381 J/g, while effectively preventing liquid leakage from molten salts. Furthermore, the 3D activated carbon/ alumina carrier significantly enhanced solar absorption by 79 % and improved the TC of SS by 64 %. These findings pave the way for novel applications of PCMs in TES, highlighting the significant potential of 3D structural carriers in enhancing PCMs properties. Subsequently, Herrezuelo et al. [\[124\]](#page-31-0) further advanced the development of innovative TES systems for CSP applications. They employed highly porous 3D-printed patterned vermiculite (V) and Al2O3 as three-dimensional carriers (3DTES), which were then osmosed with molten sodium nitrate (NN) and SS. These 3DTES were evaluated in a real-world concentrated solar radiation environment using parabolic solar furnaces. The study revealed that different 3DTES exhibited distinct characteristics, with the V-nn-based 3DTES proving to be the most efficient in converting incoming solar radiation into heat. In contrast, the Al2O3-nn system demonstrated superior heat transfer capabilities, enabling faster charge/ discharge cycles. This research not only further broadens the scope of 3DTES in solar energy applications but also provides valuable insights for subsequent studies on the selection and design of suitable PCMs systems tailored to specific requirements.

Beyond the aforementioned 3DTES-related investigations, numerous other noteworthy explorations have been undertaken in the study of functionalized PCMs. Ji et al. [[125](#page-31-0)] utilized sol-gel interfacial polymerization to synthesize uniformly spherical GO/SiO2@Solar salt microcapsules [\(Fig. 8\)](#page-16-0). The resulting microencapsulated PCMs demonstrated superior thermal stability compared to conventional SS. The deliberate incorporation of two-dimensional graphene oxide (GO) notably enhanced the TC of the microcapsules. Specifically, 3 % GO microcapsules demonstrated a photothermal conversion efficiency that was 220.4 % greater than that of SS, while also achieving a hightemperature leakage resistance of over 96.4 %. Xu et al. [\[31](#page-30-0)] employed the distinctive natural three-dimensional fiber network structure of loofah as a biological template to fabricate porous SiC ceramic frameworks through the processes of flour paste impregnation, carbonization, and molten silicon reaction. The loofah's open honeycomb-like fiber network formed a continuous pore structure (with a porosity of 70 %) post‑carbonization. This structure, with its high specific surface area and hierarchical pore features, provided ample space for subsequent loading of NaCl-NaF molten salts. Simultaneously, the dense accumulation of SiC grains established an efficient heat conduction pathway. Experimental results revealed that the TC of the bioderived SiC framework reaches 20.7 W/m⋅K, while its threedimensional interconnected structure allows the composite material to maintain an impressive energy storage density of 424 kJ/kg,

augmenting the TC by over 100 times compared to pure molten salt. Furthermore, the optimization of the surface roughness and molten salt wettability of the SiC derived from the loofah network synergistically improved the material's light absorption performance, achieving an average absorption rate of 95.25 %. This resulted in a coupled enhancement of solar energy capture, heat conduction, and phasechange energy storage ([Fig. 9a](#page-17-0)). Wang et al. [[126](#page-31-0)] employed the solgel technique to encapsulate SS in microcapsules, with Fe3O4-functionalized carbon nanotubes (CNTs-Fe3O4) adhering tightly to the outer surface of the shell ([Fig. 9](#page-17-0)b). When compared to the SiO2@Solar salt without additives, the CNTs-Fe3O4/SiO2@Solar salt magnetic microcapsule demonstrated significantly enhanced absorption in both the visible and near-infrared regions. The absorption efficiency increased as the loading ratio of CNTs-Fe3O4 was elevated. Specifically, when the CNTs-Fe3O4 content reached 3 wt%, the photothermal conversion efficiency saw an improvement of 87.1 %. By preserving the superparamagnetic properties of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, the magnetic microcapsules effectively address the common issues of low thermal efficiency and limited functionality associated with traditional MSPCMs. This advancement opens up new possibilities for the development of high-performance, multifunctional phase change heat storage microcapsules. Additionally, the synergistic magnetic and photothermal effects within this system enhance its versatility, boosting the thermal energy storage capacity of the microcapsules. As a result, these microcapsules can be applied across a broader range of fields.

However, it is noted that conventional energy conversion materials are predominantly organic, and research on inorganic nonmetallic materials remains in the early stages, particularly concerning energy conversion using MSPCMs. Therefore, further investigation into functionalbased PCMs utilizing molten salts is necessary.

#### **7. Application of MSPCMs**

According to the method of heat storage, heat storage could be classified into three types: SHTES, LHTES and TCTES. Among these, SHTES is the most fundamental form of TES, but it offers the lowest heat storage capacity. TCTES, on the other hand, encompasses a wide range of applications, including low, medium, and high-temperature systems, many of which have achieved a high level of technological maturity. High-temperature TCTES systems, in particular, are known for their high heat storage density and wide temperature range. However, their complexity and the need for careful matching of reaction rates and heat transfer performance can limit their practicality in certain applications. LHTES, by contrast, can store 3–4 times more energy than SHTES and provides significant advantages in optimizing heat transfer and controlling hotspot temperatures. Although TCTES systems are versatile and mature in many contexts, the unique benefits of LHTES, including its higher energy storage density and simpler operational requirements, make it particularly well-suited for the focus of this study.

# *7.1. Shell and tube LHTES*

While molten salt-based stabilized composites have been extensively researched in LHTES systems, their practical applications in thermal storage remain limited. A common approach involves encapsulating or packing PCMs in a container to facilitate heat exchange with a heat transfer fluid (HTF) during the charging and discharging processes. This method allows for more efficient thermal energy management and improved performance in thermal storage systems. In commercial and industrial applications, shell-and-tube structures are the most widely used and studied devices for LHTES ([Fig. 10](#page-18-0)a). This heat exchanger features a unique design consisting of a vessel and a tube. The HTF circulates through the tube, while the PCMs fills the shell cavity. When the HTF is pressurized, this configuration significantly enhances the heat exchange efficiency, facilitating better thermal storage and release during the charging and discharging processes. Tehrani et al. [[130](#page-31-0)] conducted a comprehensive investigation into shell-and-tube LHTES systems, comparing the performance of various design alternatives, including cascaded PCMs and multilayer solid PCMs, while assessing their practical viability and efficiency in real-world power plant

applications. The simplified exothermic time prediction model [[131](#page-31-0)] and the theoretical model for melting time [[132](#page-32-0)], developed by Fornarelli et al., were experimentally and numerically validated, offering efficient design tools for shell-and-tube LHTES systems. Furthermore, the team systematically evaluated the influence of geometric parameters (such as tube count, diameter, and height-to-diameter ratio) on the energy storage capacity, heat release time, and overall cost through a multi-objective optimization approach. Their findings revealed that modules with 17, 19, or 37 tubes arranged in a honeycomb configuration could substantially enhance the heat transfer surface area to heat storage volume ratio, thereby offering greater adaptability across diverse applications [[133](#page-32-0)]. Prieto et al. [\[134](#page-32-0)] proposed an innovative cascaded PCMs storage system aimed at optimizing the performance of CSP plants and conducted an in-depth evaluation. Experimental results indicated that employing a shell-and-tube heat exchanger for PCMs storage, as a benchmark, could lead to significant reductions in both capital and operational costs.

Although shell-and-tube structures exhibit significant advantages, their performance is often limited by the low TC of PCMs. This limitation may impair the rate of heat transfer, which in turn may affect the overall thermal efficiency. In order to overcome this problem, researchers have explored and implemented a series of strategies to improve the overall manifestations of the device. These techniques include the incorporation of fins to increase surface area, the use of cascaded PCMs for better thermal management and incorporating highly conductive materials within the PCMs to facilitate heat transfer. These improvements aim to optimize thermal efficiency and enhance the effectiveness of LHTES systems [\[127\]](#page-31-0).

The SSPCMs exhibit considerable potential for industrial applications, especially in mitigating issues related to corrosion and thermal stress in high-temperature PCMs. To facilitate large-scale development, Ji et al. [\[128\]](#page-31-0) innovatively used wet extrusion and sintering techniques to prepare SSPCMs particles (SSPCM-P) to promote large-scale development (Fig. 10b). Their results indicated that SSPCM-P with a salt concentration below 60 wt% could be successfully produced using this technique, which was lower than the salt content achieved by traditional methods such as cold pressing and hot sintering. This improvement was primarily due to the migration of salts to the surface during wet mixing and evaporation, which promotes better binding during sintering.

The study demonstrates that SSPCM-P offers significantly superior

thermal performance when compared to conventional sensible heat materials. This improvement aids in maintaining stability throughout thermal cycling, ensuring consistent operation during both charging and discharging processes. However, the research also identified a key limitation of SSPCM-P: its unsuitability for direct use in steam environments, as the potential for salt precipitation poses a risk. This constraint restricts its application in scenarios where it would come into direct contact with steam. To address this issue, the researchers proposed an innovative solution: utilizing SSPCM-P within finned tube heat

exchangers. This configuration not only mitigates the chemical compatibility concerns between SSPCM-P and the HTF, but also leverages the material's compactness, ease of fabrication, and stacking capabilities. By employing this configuration, the system fully exploits the thermal performance benefits of SSPCM-P while cleverly circumventing its limitations, thus creating new opportunities for its widespread use in various applications.

To further investigate the impact of the shell-and-tube structure on the thermophysical properties of molten salts, Li et al. [\[135\]](#page-32-0) carried out a comparative analysis using two types of storage materials, pure nitrate and a nitrate-based composite consisting of nitrate, EV and graphite. They developed a two-dimensional mathematical model that incorporated the synergistic effects of natural convection and heat transfer, enabling accurate predictions of heat transfer within the molten salts. Furthermore, the study systematically evaluated how various design parameters including the number, layout, length, and thickness of fins along with different operating conditions, influenced the melting performance of the storage device. The findings demonstrated that incorporating fins into the system accelerated the melting rate of the molten salt, showing a clear advantage in terms of thermal performance. The shape of the fins greatly influences thermal performance during melting (charging). Round fins reduce charging time by up to 70 %, while longitudinal fins reduce it by up to 55 % [[136](#page-32-0)]. Additionally, the cumulative energy storage was enhanced by approximately 52 % using circular fins compared to systems without fins. However, while these studies explored various fin geometries, they did not address the critical issue of conserving the volume of PCMs. Elmaazouzi et al. [[137](#page-32-0)] addressed this gap by ensuring that the PCMs volume remained constant across different fin geometries. They investigated how fin thickness of fins impacted thermal performance by evaluating three different fin designs. The results indicated that fin thickness could significantly influence thermal performance, even when the total fin volume remains constant. Specifically, when the fin thickness ranged from 1 to 2 mm, both the complete melting time and charging time were reduced to varying degrees. Notably, when the fin thickness was 1 mm (for longitudinal fins), the complete melting time and charging time were reduced by 13.5 % and 11.4 %, respectively, compared to other LHTES.

While the introduction of fins in LHTES systems is expected to enhance heat transfer properties, these fins can also negatively affect the overall thermal efficiency by obstructing natural convection within the MSPCMs and reducing the usable filling volume of the PCMs. As a result, a complete redesign of the inner tube and housing was necessary to optimize the fin layout and fine-tune structural parameters, ensuring the optimal thermal capability of the LHTES system.

Yang et al. [[138](#page-32-0)] studied the heat storage characteristics of the horizontal LHTES system employing the sensible heat capacity technique and found that both the eccentricity and temperature gradient of the tube have a significant impact on the thermal performance. Their studies indicated that a proper eccentricity can accelerate the melting of molten salt, while an inappropriate eccentricity could reduce the internal uniformity of the molten salt. Through optimization, it was found that when the eccentricity reached 0.4, the melting time was reduced by 42.44 %, and the heat storage density increased by 70.8 %. Moreover, increasing the temperature difference not only improved the TC of the molten salt but also promoted natural convection phenomena. Under a temperature difference of 50 K, the melting time was significantly shortened by 62.8 %, and the heat storage density was significantly increased by 259.9 %. Sodhi et al. [[139](#page-32-0)] explored the effect of varying the volume distribution and heat transfer surface area changes on the heat transfer efficiency of PCMs and proposed an innovative design for the LHTES system. They reconfigured the casing of the system into a conical shape and adjusted the cross-section of the HTF tube along its length. In their study, Sodhi et al. designed seven different system configurations based on optimized shell geometries, including cylindrical, converging conical, and divergent conical shells, while incorporating both cylindrical and converging/diverging cross-sections for the

HTF tubes. Through a series of comparative experiments, they determined that the system featuring a converging shell and converging cross-section HTF tube exhibited superior performance compared to the traditional cylindrical shell-and-tube configuration, leading to a substantial reduction in both charge and discharge times, with decreases of 37 % and 47.3 %, respectively. These findings offer a promising new approach to enhancing heat transfer efficiency in LHTES systems.

Currently, major research efforts have focused on the temporal fluctuations of PCMs temperature during both charging and discharging processes [\[140\]](#page-32-0), along with the thermal properties and efficiency of systems [[141](#page-32-0)]. Additionally, efforts have been directed toward gaining a more thorough understanding of the underlying heat transfer mechanisms [[142](#page-32-0)]. For example, Sodhi et al. [[129](#page-31-0)] developed a shell-and-tube system that employs NaNO3 as the PCMs on the outer surface of the shell, with air serving as the HTF to create an efficient, cost-effective TES solution ([Fig. 10c](#page-18-0)). The temperature distribution characteristics of the system during both charging and discharging are noteworthy. During charging, both axial and radial temperature distributions undergo significant changes, primarily driven the dominance of natural convection. This suggests that the heat transfer process is intricate and dynamic. In contrast, during discharging, the heat transfer process becomes more symmetrical in both the axial and radial directions, with a significant temperature gradient along the length of the long-term heat storage module. At this point, the influence of natural convection diminishes, and conduction heat transfer becomes the predominant mechanism. Additionally, the study reveals that elevating the flow rate of the inlet HTF enhances the heat transfer rate during both charging and discharging phases, although this effect becomes limited beyond a certain threshold. Beyond this threshold, further increases in flow rate result in negligible improvements in heat transfer performance. The experiments showed that when the temperature gap between the HTF and the initial PCMs reached 40 ◦C, the charging duration decreased by 40 %, and the discharge time was shortened by 45 %, highlighting the significant impact of temperature differences on heat transfer efficiency. The findings of this research on the heat transfer characteristics of shell-andtube systems offer a crucial foundation and valuable insights for further investigation into the performance of industrial-scale and various types of LHTES.

In solar power plants, industrial-scale LHTES systems typically possess storage capacities in the gigajoule range, necessitating the use of large-scale shell-and-tube heat exchangers incorporating numerous HTF tubes. To facilitate the commercialization of this technology, it is essential to conduct comprehensive experimental investigations of shelland-tube LHTES systems constructed with multiple tubes and fins. In this regard, Niyas et al. [[143](#page-32-0)] developed a laboratory-scale shell-andtube LHTES prototype with a multi-tube configuration, featuring longitudinal fins on the exterior of the HTF tubes to enhance thermal performance. A schematic of the experimental setup employed to assess the thermal storage characteristics of the LHS prototype is presented in [Fig. 11a](#page-20-0), while a graphical representation of the setup is shown in [Fig. 11b](#page-20-0). During the experiment, measurements were taken at 5 s intervals across various locations, including HTF inlet and outlet temperatures, as well as flow rates. The findings revealed that natural convection significantly influences the charging process but is not a dominant factor during discharge, where conductive heat transfer primarily governs solidification. Owing to the enhanced natural convection after the phase change temperature, charging occurs at a faster rate than discharging. The LHS prototype exhibited a charging duration of approximately 124 min and a discharge duration of approximately 131 min. The energy stored during charging was 16.87 MJ, while the energy released during discharge was 15.41 MJ. The results indicated that, in comparison to HTF flow rate, variations in HTF inlet temperature exerted a more substantial effect on the charge-discharge time. Furthermore, HTF flow rate had a more pronounced impact when the HTF inlet temperature was either excessively low or high during both charging and discharging processes. To evaluate the long-term operation of solar thermal power plants, Raul et al. [[144](#page-32-0)] explored the influence of a finned multi-tube shell-and-tube LHTES system on the dynamic performance of Rankine cycle solar thermal power plants. The study examined the effects of PCMs fin count and fin thickness on the overall performance of solar power generation during periods after sunset under cloudy conditions in India. It was found that the inclusion of fins in the LHTES system reduced the duration of continuous power generation. When the number of fins exceeded 36 and the fin thickness surpassed 2

mm, the melting rate of the PCMs and the turbine output power exhibited minimal variation. The highest discharge efficiency was observed with a fin count of 48. While this research offers important perspectives on enhancing the efficiency of LHTES systems, it also underscores the need for further exploration into the melting and solidification behaviors of PCMs in multi-tube LHTES setups in these systems. Lv et al. [[145](#page-32-0)] developed a pilot-scale LHTES system (Fig. 12a) incorporating a quartz visualization window. This design enabled the observation of the melting and solidification processes of mesothermal PCMs within a multi-tube configuration. This innovative setup allowed for real-time observation of the temperature distributions and phase transitions within the PCMs, presenting a clearer understanding of the heat transfer dynamics involved. Observations from the quartz window showed a notable vertical temperature difference (up to 50 ◦C) between PCMs. This difference was caused by the varying temperatures of the conductive materials interacting with the upper and lower PCMs. Notably, the gradual variations in temperature ensured consistent power input and output across both phases. The analysis revealed that the heat transfer coefficients during charging (613.08 W/(m<sup>2</sup> ⋅K)) were notably higher than during discharging (295.28 W/(m2 ⋅K)), which contributed to lower average thermal resistances during charging (0.001984 (m<sup>2</sup> ⋅K)/ W) compared to discharging (0.005285 (m2 ⋅K)/W). This disparity was attributed to the differences in heat transfer mechanisms at play during the two phases, highlighting the importance of optimizing these conditions for enhanced efficiency in LHTES systems.

The development of LHTES technology is closely tied to the growth of CSP systems, which rely heavily on efficient thermal energy storage to address the intermittent nature of solar energy. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency [[148](#page-32-0)], global CSP capacity has experienced substantial growth over the past decade, expanding from 1.2 gigawatts (GW) in 2010 to 6.4 GW in 2020; however, growth has stagnated in recent years. To meet the targets set by the Paris Agreement, the installed CSP capacity must reach 196.7 GW by 2030 and 872.6 GW by 2050. Achieving these goals will require significant advancements in thermal energy storage technologies, including LHTES systems. Spain has implemented a fixed tariff system under Royal Decree No. 436/2004, offering CSP companies competitive tariffs for up to 25 years. This initiative has successfully facilitated the execution of landmark projects such as PS10, Andasol I, and Gemasolar, while the coordinated efforts of the European Commission have expedited their commercialization. These projects have demonstrated the potential of integrating LHTES systems with CSP plants to enhance overall efficiency and reliability. At the technological forefront, the advancement of LHTES is of paramount importance for CSP applications. Research indicated that PCMs could enhance the heat storage capacity by approximately 0.4 MJ (relative to a total capacity of 1 MJ) within the temperature range of 270–330 ◦C. Furthermore, optimizing the inlet temperature, resulting in a 39.6 % reduction in charging time to 440 ◦C and an 18.2 % decrease in discharge temperature to 170 ◦C, significantly boosts system efficiency [[149](#page-32-0)]. These improvements highlight the critical role of LHTES in addressing the challenges of solar energy intermittency and enhancing the economic viability of CSP plants. Consequently, LHTES holds promising potential for the future of concentrated solar power generation.

However, when LHTES was utilized for solar energy recirculating power (ORC) systems, its performance was hindered by the inherent fluctuations in solar energy, preventing it from operating effectively under the intended design conditions. It was observed that cascaded thermal storage (CLTES) systems were better suited for solar TES compared to single-stage thermal storage (LTES). To explore this further, Wang et al. [[146](#page-32-0)] simulated the thermal storage behaviors of both single-stage and cascaded thermal storage units, each containing various PCMs, using two-dimensional enthalpy-dynamics heat transfer model. They also assessed the system's thermal properties under typical seasonal solar fluctuations [\(Fig. 12b](#page-20-0)). The findings indicated that the optimized cyclic thermal storage method could increase the thermal efficiency by a minimum of 10 % and a maximum of 17.2 %. In addition to the enhancement techniques mentioned above, the use of SSCPCMs has proven to be an effective approach [[147](#page-32-0)]. This was because they have achieved excellent results in improving the thermophysical and mechanical properties, such as topology-optimized fins ([Fig. 12c](#page-20-0)). Among them, the inclusion of a porous skeleton can notably improve the TC of MSPCMs while having only a minimal effect on their thermal storage capacity. In a investigation by Zhang et al. [[150](#page-32-0)], the TES

properties of MSPCMs reinforced with ceramic foam was analyzed using three-dimensional numerical simulations. The findings indicated that as the ceramic foam's filling height corresponded to an elevated energy storage rate. However, the impact of ceramic foam reinforcement varied with different filling heights. Both the porosity of the foam and the filling height were found to similarly influence d the rate of energy storage. In terms of the foam's outer diameter, ceramic foams with smaller diameters showed a more pronounced enhancement in performance. Although metal foams offer greater performance improvements, ceramic foams continue to be a viable alternative for improving the thermal properties of molten salts, owing to their excellent corrosion resistance and low cost.

## *7.2. Packed bed LHTES*

The packed bed latent heat thermal energy storage system (PBLHS) has garnered significant interest on account of its cost-effectiveness and promising potential for various applications. The concept of the PBLHS is illustrated in [Fig. 13a](#page-22-0). In their work, Wang et al. [[151](#page-32-0)] developed and set up an innovative experimental system for medium and hightemperature PBLHS. They employed NaNO3-KNO3 as PCMs and designed a spherical encapsulated shell tailored for this purpose. Experimental investigations were conducted to analyze the heat transfer behavior during the charging, static holding, and discharging phases. The findings demonstrated that as the average air temperature within the thermal storage tank increased, the internal temperature gradient initially rose before decreasing and ultimately stabilized in the range of 225 to 272 ◦C, with a steady difference of around 84 ◦C (±1 ◦C). During the initial discharge phase, the temperature difference declined gradually. Overall, the total efficiency of the binary nitrate-based PBLHS system was found to be 79.3 %. While the study indicated that various parameters could enhance PBLHS performance, it also highlighted a need for more research into different system setups to further optimize performance. Mohammadnejad et al. [\[153\]](#page-32-0) conducted an analysis to assess how different arrangements, pore coefficients, and layer heights influenced the overall thermal characteristics of the system. Their findings indicated that an increase in porosity, increasing the inlet velocity of the HTF, and a reduced duration for the HTF to reach the desired temperature upon leaving the tank all contributed to improved thermal performance. Moreover, they discovered that minor adjustments in the sequence of PCMs within the packed bed, the porosity of individual layers, and the height of each layer could further optimize system performance. Notably, the research highlighted that it was unnecessary for the HTF to completely extract energy from the PCMs spheres; instead, it was sufficient to elevate the HTF to the desired temperature. This approach enabled the HTF to sustain the ideal temperature over an extended period, leading to a 29.2 % enhancement in the thermal characteristics of the packed bed.

Currently, the heat transfer properties of MSPCMs spheres in PBLHS were mainly studied by disorder accumulation. Research have explored the factors influencing charging and discharging efficiency, as well as heat transfer in sphere bed channels [[154](#page-32-0)], has shown that both charging and discharging efficiencies increase with higher inlet temperatures and flow velocities. While the random arrangement of spheres in heat storage tanks offers some practical benefits in terms of engineering efficiency [\[155\]](#page-32-0), its limitations, such as low energy storage density and uneven flow distribution, are becoming more apparent. As a result, researchers are increasingly favoring structured stacking methods. The two-dimensional arrangement of PCMs spheres, both radially and horizontally ordered, has been thoroughly studied, with detailed temperature contour data provided for these configurations [[156](#page-32-0)]. Additional studies on three-dimensional, ordered grid-supported stacking, including basic cubic structures, have demonstrated that this method effectively reduces pressure drop while preserving heat transfer coefficients comparable to those of traditional packed beds. This discovery presents considerable potential for improving the overall efficiency of heat transfer processes [[157](#page-32-0)]. In summary, the ordered stacking showed superior performance in both thermal storage and heat transfer capacity compared to the disordered arrangement of spheres in the thermal storage tank.

Furthermore, Li et al. [[152](#page-32-0)] carried out a comprehensive investigation into the thermal properties of the high-temperature PBTES system, revealing that thermal resistance, operating conditions, and the capsule shell configuration significantly impact system performance. The research specifically evaluated temperature changes and overall heat transfer during charging and discharging using encapsulated ternary carbonate, employing both experimental and numerical methods (Fig. 13b). It was found that the thermal resistance caused by the capsule shell is pivotal in controlling heat transfer within the system, underscoring the significance of the material and design of the enclosure in optimizing thermal efficiency. Additionally, operating conditions were shown to significantly influence system performance. For instance, increasing the HTF inlet temperature from 425 to 465 ◦C resulted in a 70 % increase in the charging heat transfer rate and a 45 % increase during discharge. Similarly, raising the mass flow of hot oil from 180 kg/h to 260 kg/h resulted in an approximate 30 % and 35 % improvement in the charge and discharge rates, respectively. These findings emphasize the critical role of operating parameters in enhancing thermal efficiency. The research also compared the performance of a packed bed configuration with a shell-and-tube setup, showing that the packed bed system

outperformed the shell-and-tube system in terms of thermal efficiency, achieving an overall efficiency increase of 1.9 to 2.4 times. This result offers valuable guidance for selecting system configurations. Finally, while encapsulation technology effectively reduces subcooling, leakage, and extends material lifespans in high-temperature LHTES systems, it still faces significant challenges. These drawbacks significantly limited the widespread industrial application of these systems. Li et al. [[158](#page-32-0)] conducted a numerical analysis of high-temperature PBTES systems utilizing carbonate-based phase change materials (CBPCMs). Their findings revealed that the CBPCM systems outperformed filled-bed systems containing iron oxide in terms of charge and discharge performance. This improvement was attributed to fact that CBPCM has a higher energy storage density and TC, which led to enhanced heat transfer efficiency and average power. Additionally, the phase transition of the CBPCMs played a crucial role in stabilizing the outlet temperature of HTF, which enhanced the system's efficiency and improved its suitability for thermal regulation. The research further highlighted that augmenting the mass fraction of thermally conductive enhanced materials (TCEM) led to reduced charging and discharging durations. This reduction is due to the enhanced thermal conductivity associated with higher TCEM loading, which improves the heat transfer rates.

With the purpose of further extending the practical application of MSPCMs in engineering, ELSihy et al. [\[159\]](#page-32-0) examined how the combination of graphene (Gr) and copper (Cu) nanoparticles, when integrated with PCMs, influenced the charge and discharge performance of PBLHS in a single cycle (Fig. 14a). When the volume fraction of Cu and Gr nanoparticles was increased from 0 % to 5 %, the melting and curing times for the cascade storage systems of Cu nanoparticle-enhanced PCMs (Cu-nepcm) and encapsulated with Gr nanoparticle-enhanced PCMs (Grnepcm) shortened to varying extents. When the inlet temperature was increased from 390 to 440 ◦C, the three optimal cascade packed-bed configurations showed 82.2 %, 85.6 %, 20.3 %, and 50.5 % increases in total bed storage energy, bed salt recovery energy, capacity ratio, and total utilization, respectively. Thus, this study provided insights into the design and operation of high-performance PBLHS systems. Taken together, the addition of nanoparticles, encapsulation and cascading could effectively solve the general problem of molten salt-based PBLHS. However, they overlooked the expansion and contraction influence of MSPCMs during melting and solidification. Based on this, Kumar et al. [[161](#page-32-0)] performed an in-depth experimental analysis on LHTES within cylindrical MSPCMs encapsulated in layer-filled beds (PBTESs), and they proposed a comprehensive numerical model that accounts for these expansion and contraction phenomena. Their investigation assessed the thermal properties of the system under varying conditions. The findings indicated that the air mass flow rate significantly impacts the efficiency of charging and discharging in PBTES systems, with the inlet air temperature being a key factor in this relationship. To examine the impact of expansion and contraction on the exit airflow, indoor PCMs cylindrical encapsulation experiments were conducted for comparison and validation. The findings demonstrated that the numerical model accounting for these effects yielded an outlet temperature that closely matched experimental values, achieving an average absolute deviation of 3.1 %, compared to 5.4 % for the model that did not consider these factors. This indicates that the refined model is effective in predicting the thermal properties of PCMs, particularly in PBTES systems, where solid and liquid density differences are significant. Although HTF exhibited efficient heat transfer performance, its operating temperature range and high cost have limited the economics of filled-bed TES systems utilizing these materials [\[162\]](#page-32-0). To enhance heat transfer efficiency while minimizing costs, the spray-filled bed TES technique was introduced ([Fig. 14b](#page-23-0)). At the core of this technology lies the utilization of a topspray device that directs high-temperature HTF to flow along the surface of stored particles as a liquid film, driven by gravity, rather than completely filling the interstitial spaces between particles. This innovative design concept stems from a comprehensive analysis and enhancement of the distribution and flow patterns of HTF in traditional PBLHS systems. In conventional systems, the flow of HTF within the packed bed often encounters issues such as uneven distribution and excessive flow resistance, which hinder the potential for improving heat transfer efficiency. The spray-assisted TES technology significantly enhances heat transfer performance by uniformly distributing the HTF as a fine spray across the surfaces of the heat storage particles, thereby forming a thin, continuous liquid film that maximizes the contact area between the HTF and the heat storage particles. This method reduces the reliance on costly liquid HTF by limiting their percentage in the filled bed to approximately 10 % based on the flow rate. Furthermore, only around 40 % of the bed volume is occupied by HTF, a marked reduction compared to conventional filled-bed TES systems. This innovative approach not only lowers the expenses associated with HTF but also promotes effective heat transfer by enabling direct contact between the liquid HTF and the storage material. This configuration optimizes thermal performance while addressing economic constraints, positioning it as a promising option for enhancing the performance of TES systems [[160\]](#page-32-0).

#### *7.3. Rotary drum heat exchanger*

Drum heat exchangers, alongside shell-and-tube designs, offer promising solutions to the challenge of low TC in storage materials. In these systems, a horizontal drum is partially immersed in liquid PCMs. As the liquid inside the drum evaporates, the PCMs solidifies on its outer surface, facilitating efficient heat transfer. Studies, such as those by Tombrink et al. [\[163\]](#page-32-0), have demonstrated that TES systems using drum heat exchangers can provide industrial process steam. Simulations of a multi-channel rotating drum in a drum heat exchanger indicate that the average thickness of the heat exchanger wall is the primary factor influencing heat transfer efficiency. Other design parameters, such as drum length and total drum diameter, also affect heat transfer, but to a lesser extent. Even when these parameters were adjusted by a factor of two, the resulting changes in heat transfer efficiency were generally less than 10 %. This finding indicates that the drum heat exchanger is highly scalable, making it suitable for industrial applications. When NaNO3 was used as the storage material, the volumetric energy density increased clearly to 332 kWh⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>3</sup> , representing a 65 % improvement over current technologies. The system was also able to achieve a surface heat transfer rate exceeding 300 kW⋅m<sup>−</sup> <sup>2</sup> , with up to 24 % of the power being used for generation during the discharge phase, demonstrating the substantial potential of the rotating drum heat exchanger. Additionally, TES systems could be charged using renewable energy sources, allowing for the separation of steam and electricity production from current cogeneration systems. If successfully scaled, this TES technology could contribute significantly to the decarbonization of industrial processes and enhance the flexibility of steam supply.

#### *7.4. Electric heaters*

The electric heater serves as the energy conversion component within the storage system. It heats the molten salt stored in the lowtemperature tank, which is subsequently transferred to the hightemperature tank for storage. When needed, the thermal energy stored in the high-temperature tank can be accessed and used directly as a source of heat, thereby enhancing both cost efficiency and operational flexibility.

Currently, most studies of MSPCMs have concentrated on nitrate flowing in macroscopic channels. To accommodate high flux densities, Frantz et al. [\[164\]](#page-32-0) chose to use a cylindrical induction heater as a test setup [\(Fig. 15a](#page-25-0)). Temperature measurements at the primary fluid inlet and outlet were taken using three N-type thermocouples, which were affixed to the outer surface of the tube by means of a micro-laser beam to ensure improved thermal contact between the thermocouples and the tube. Additionally, the cylindrical induction heater required water cooling to prevent overheating at elevated magnetic flux densities. To minimize uncertainty, paired thermocouples measuring temperature differences were calibrated simultaneously. Experimental findings showed that by varying the inlet temperature of the main fluid, the resulting Prandtl numbers ranged from 10 to 3.8, while the Reynolds numbers spanned from 14,000 to 222,000. Royo et al. [[167](#page-32-0)] introduced a modular adaptive energy storage system using PCMs to enhance the flexibility, regional integration, and overall operational efficiency of renewable energy systems. This innovative system combines a flat pillow plate with a heating rod, achieving an efficiency range of 65 % to 79 % during charging, depending on the configuration of the heating rods. Notably, the system's efficiency can be further boosted to 95 % by implementing active control regulation and dividing the system into distinct heating zones. The study underscores the potential for significant enhancements in the charging efficiency of PCMs-based storage systems by optimizing critical parameters, such as the set temperature, number of heating rods, and control strategies. Additionally, a thorough evaluation of various HTF temperature and volume flow rates was conducted to assess the thermal discharge process, resulting in efficiencies between 78 % and 92 %. These results are crucial for advancing the design and development of PCMs and electric heater energy storage systems, providing substantial support for improving the overall performance and adaptability of renewable energy systems.

To enhance the energy conversion efficiency of heat storage systems, researchers have suggested replacing nitrate salts with chloride salts as the heat storage medium. However, this shift places higher demands on the electric heater design. To tackle this challenge, Yang et al. [[165](#page-32-0)] introduced an innovative horizontally centered micro-annular channel electric heater [\(Fig. 15](#page-25-0)b). The performance of this heater was thoroughly validated through dielectric withstand voltage and thermal durability tests. The study also explored the impact of eccentricity and heat flux density on the heat transfer performance of the heater. The results indicated that the heat pipe could withstand up to 270 heating and cooling cycles, with operating temperatures ranging from 673.15 K to 1093.15 K, all while maintaining stable operation. Notably, in the horizontal eccentric micro-annular channel, dynamic turbulence was observed under low Reynolds number conditions. As the eccentricity increased, the friction coefficient and the average Nusselt number decreased. In contrast, an increase in heat flux led to a rise in the average Nusselt number but a decrease in the friction coefficient. Building on these findings, the researchers proposed an optimized heater design that focuses on minimizing entropy generation. This approach resulted in a significant increase in heat flux, achieving performance at least 5.1 times better than conventional heaters. This innovation not only addresses the heating challenges of chloride salt heat storage in TES systems but also offers a more efficient and reliable heating solution for high heat flow applications.

# *7.5. Others LHTES*

The CSP plants equipped with TES systems are gaining recognition as a highly potential solution for the development of advancing renewable energy infrastructure. This is due to their ability to deliver both dispatchable and cost-effective electricity, derived from the plentiful yet intermittent solar resource. In situations where electricity consumption patterns are irregular or when renewable energy-based power plants are being constructed, Yurin et al. [\[168\]](#page-32-0) suggested the implementation of low-power steam turbines at nuclear power plants (NPPs) to provide supplementary redundancy to meet fluctuating demand. In the event of communication failures within the power system, these turbines could utilize the reactor or existing thermal generation to maintain operations, ensuring power for the auxiliary needs of the NPP while adhering to the

safety standards set by the International Atomic Energy Agency. Furthermore, Abanades et al. [[166](#page-32-0)] have introduced a novel molten salt mixture (Sr(NO3)2/NaNO3/KNO3/LiNO3) with a melting point below 150 ◦C, which has been effectively implemented in a molten salt heat storage tank operating within a temperature range of 150 to 235 ◦C. When integrated with various types of concentrating solar collector technologies, this low-temperature molten salt heat storage system supplies adequate thermal energy for district heating and cooling (DHC) systems, particularly for applications such as dual-effect absorption chillers ([Fig. 15c](#page-25-0)). Notably, unlike the high-temperature molten salt heat storage systems (250–550 ◦C) typically utilized in conventional CSP plants, this research is specifically optimized to meet the distinct requirements of DHC systems. In traditional CSP systems, hightemperature heat storage primarily supports the power generation process. However, DHC systems generally operate at lower temperatures, especially in real-world settings, such as residential and commercial buildings. Presently, most existing DHC systems rely on water as the heat storage medium, yet due to water's boiling point limitation (100 ◦C), its operational temperature range is severely constrained, hindering its ability to meet certain applications demanding higher temperatures. Abanades et al. have innovatively broadened the applicability of concentrated solar technology by extending its operational range to the mid-temperature spectrum (150–235 ◦C), enabling its direct deployment in DHC systems. This expansion not only significantly enhances the versatility of concentrated solar technology but also provides increased adaptability for a wider array of applications, particularly in DHC systems that urgently require medium-temperature thermal energy.

To tackle the challenge of spatial mismatch between heat demand and supply, mobile thermal energy storage (M-TES) presents a promising approach. This system enables the collection and storage of thermal energy from industrial sites, which is then transported to various locations using mobile containers specifically designed for high energy density. For instance, Yang et al. [[170](#page-32-0)] used a molten salt (NaNO3/ NaNO2)-based CPCMs module as the M-TES medium, which was charged and discharged using air. The findings revealed that after 10 h of charging, the M-TES system successfully stored nearly 400 MJ of thermal energy, achieving a TES density of 560 kJ/kg, with an average temperature of the CPCMs reaching 662 K. During the following 10 h discharging period, around 97 % of the stored heat was released, with the average air temperature at the exit exceeding 468 K. This study preliminarily verified that M-TES would be feasibility of combining industrial heat treatment decarbonization with home heating. In addition to the above studies, several other works on TES devices containing saltbased composites have been reported. Guo et al. [[171](#page-32-0)] introduced a novel application of microencapsulated PCMs in high-temperature drilling fluids. These microcapsules exhibited a phase change temperature around 220 ◦C and a melting enthalpy of 68.48 J/g, allowing them to be reused effectively at this temperature. The compatibility of these microcapsules with the drilling fluid was assessed, showing that they slightly increased the fluid's viscosity while also reducing filtration. Hot rolling tests demonstrated that the microcapsules maintained their compatibility with the drilling fluid even under high temperatures, without adversely affecting its performance. Further testing was conducted to simulate the temperature control effect of the microcapsules in a laboratory setting. Results indicated that a concentration of 10 wt% of these microcapsules provided a significant temperature regulation effect. Specifically, they were able to slow the rate at which the drilling fluid warmed up during high-temperature formation, thereby reducing the degradation of fluid components. Similarly, Kang et al. [\[169](#page-32-0)] reported that molten-salt-based CPCMs were successful in stabilizing hightemperature drilling fluids, effectively slowing the rate at which the fluids warmed (Fig. 16). These findings suggest that microencapsulated PCMs could be crucial in creating more sustainable and efficient hightemperature drilling fluid systems for deep formations. Nevertheless, additional research is still necessary, particularly concerning the development of more optimized microcapsule shapes, higher enthalpy materials, better size control, and comprehensive field testing to assess performance in real-world drilling operations.

#### **8. Corrosion problems in the application of MSPCMs**

Corrosion poses a major threat to the safety and dependability of TES systems. Current research primarily focuses on the corrosive behavior of molten salts used in solar thermal storage, with particular emphasis on the common SS mixture. The application of protective coatings in molten nitrate environments is recognized as an effective mitigation strategy.

Kondaiah et al. [\[172,173\]](#page-32-0) investigated the thermal stability of iron and nickel alloys (with and without corrosion-resistant coatings) exposed to molten nitrate at elevated temperatures. The investigation specifically targeted the performance of SS, which is commonly used in solar thermal storage systems. The results show that the electrodeposited nickel coating significantly reduces the corrosion rate of the iron-based alloy, lowering it to about 20 μm/year due to its fractal surface design. This is a 68 % reduction compared to the uncoated surface. After 500 h of isothermal exposure at 600 ◦C, both the Ni and NiYSZ-coated alloys demonstrated minimal weight changes, around 2 %, in comparison to the uncoated alloys exposed to molten salts. At 620 ◦C, the mass loss of NiYSZ-coated alloys was reduced by 27–30 % compared to Ni-only coatings and by 40–70 % compared to the uncoated alloys. These experimental results highlighted the protective role of the NiYSZ coating, which not only improved the thermal stability of the molten salts but also provided robust protection against hightemperature corrosion. Despite these promising findings, the research noted that the cost of these corrosion-resistant coatings and their ability to maintain stability at high temperatures posed significant challenges. These factors have hindered the further development of this line of research, limiting its practical application in solar TES systems. Gonzalez et al. [[174](#page-32-0)] explored the effect of incorporating 2 wt% graphite into molten SS on the surface characteristics of carbon steel A516Gr70. Their findings revealed that the corrosion rate of the graphitized carbon steel decreased by nearly half at 390 ◦C. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that incorporating 2 wt% graphite to the molten salt led to a more than sixfold reduction in the corrosion rate when compared to carbon steel without graphite. This significant decrease was attributed to the formation of iron carbide crystals, which were evenly distributed across the steel surface. In conclusion, spray graphitization was identified as an effective technique for mitigating corrosion in carbon steel exposed to molten binary nitrates. Additionally, the affect of nanoparticles on the corrosion behavior of metallic materials has garnered increasing interest [\[175\]](#page-32-0). Nithiyanantham et al. [[176\]](#page-32-0) considered the influence of Al2O3 and SiO2 on the corrosion of carbon steel A516Gr70 dispersed in binary eutectic nitrate. Their study revealed that the introduction of nanoparticles produced a complex effect on the corrosion rate of the carbon steel. It was expected that the presence of nanoparticles within the corrosive layer would lead to a reduction in the rate of corrosion. However, when nanoparticles were incorporated into the salt, the corrosivity was higher due to the many air microbubbles trapped in the inter-particle pores, which increased the oxidation rate. Later, Gao et al. [[177](#page-32-0)] analyzed the corrosion mechanism of reticulated SiO2 and acicular TiO2 on 316 L stainless steel. They found that the corrosion processes were governed by ionic transport. Additionally, the inclusion of nanoparticles enhanced the impedance of 316 L stainless steel in molten salt, thereby slowing the corrosion rate. However, SS are conventionally employed at 550 ◦C due to their decomposition at higher temperatures, which restricts the attainable temperature differential and, consequently, the overall efficiency of the thermal energy storage system. To enhance the performance of molten salt for thermal storage, a novel molten chloride salt was proposed. Based on the potential corrosion resistance of silicon nitride (Si3N4), Wu et al. [[178](#page-32-0)] selected Si3N4 as the ceramic phase in the composite. Meanwhile, the tungsten (W) was

introduced to enhance the machinability of Si3N4 ceramics greatly. The results demonstrated that the Si3N4/W composites displayed significant resistance to chloride molten salt corrosion due to the complex grain boundaries within their network structure, which effectively hindered the diffusion of molten chloride salts. In addition, the Si3N4/W composites were able to withstand potential failures during the service period (less than 100 h) in an air environment, achieving smooth and uninterrupted operation. This demonstrates the composites' reliable performance against corrosive environments, highlighting their potential for various applications involving MSPCMs. Previous research has shown that techniques such as graphitization spraying could strengthen the corrosion resistance of carbon steel when exposed to molten nitrates. Nevertheless, the influence of nanoparticle doping remains multifaceted. On one hand, the incorporation of Al2O3 nanoparticles into SS induces the formation of a protective alumina layer, thereby mitigating the corrosion rate [\[179\]](#page-32-0). On the other hand, under specific conditions, the addition of molten salts to nanoparticles may lead to the formation of an irregular corrosion layer due to the entrapment of air bubbles and the decomposition of salts, consequently accelerating the oxidation process [[180](#page-32-0)]. This contradiction highlights the need for continued exploration of more effective strategies and materials to mitigate corrosion in these systems.

Although some progress has been made in the research on the corrosion challenges associated with MSPCMs packaging, the overall body of work remains limited. [Table 6](#page-28-0) consolidates key findings from multiple studies, encompassing molten salt types, service temperatures, material compositions, and innovative reinforcement strategies, thus offering a comprehensive overview of the current research progress. Regarding molten salt types, the studies examined systems such as nitrates, carbonates, chlorides, among others. The operational temperature range is vast, spanning from hundreds to thousands of degrees Celsius. The materials investigated include alloys, stainless steels, and ceramic-metal composites. Innovative reinforcement strategies include the development of advanced coatings, the incorporation of nanoparticles, and various surface treatments. These findings have provided numerous potential solutions to address the challenges of molten salt corrosion and underscore the growing depth and diversification of research in this area, which holds significant promise for advancing the widespread adoption of molten salt in solar TES systems and other related applications.

To offer a more structured understanding of molten salt corrosion evolution, [Fig. 17](#page-28-0) presents a timeline of significant research developments, emphasizing breakthroughs in coating technologies, material enhancements, and dynamic corrosion management. Since 2010, research in this domain has demonstrated a progressive shift from fundamental protection strategies to an in-depth exploration of underlying mechanisms. The Al2O3-Cr2O3 plasma spray coating developed by Cho et al. establishes a pivotal technological foundation for material protection in high-temperature molten salt environments [[181](#page-32-0)]. Ignatiev et al.'s compatibility study on HN80MTY nickel-based alloys highlighted their superior resistance to inter-granular corrosion in tellurium fluoride-containing molten salts, thereby providing a critical basis for selecting materials for molten salt reactors [[182](#page-32-0)]. Yang et al. [[183](#page-32-0)] further elucidated the exacerbating effect of oxygen impurities on the corrosion of SiC in LiF-NaF-KF molten salts, underscoring the necessity of stringent molten salt purity control. The 8YSZ/alumina/CNT composite coating developed by Thakare et al. marked a significant enhancement in corrosion resistance in Na2SO4-V2O5 molten salts by reducing the monoclinic phase ratio [[184](#page-32-0)], thereby advancing the application of nanocomposite coatings.

The study by Yang et al. on Pt + Hf co-modified NiAl coatings revealed a complex failure mechanism in Na2SO4/NaCl mixed salts [[185](#page-32-0)]. The rapid dissolution of HfO2 facilitates the diffusion of Ni, Ti, and other elements into the oxide scale, which results in the formation of loose Al2S3 and chloride bubbles, ultimately accelerating the degradation of the coating. This finding serves as a crucial alert for the future optimization of coating compositions. As research progresses, technological advancements have progressively focused on the design of multiscale protection systems. For instance, the fractal-textured Ni coating [[186](#page-32-0)] reduces corrosion rates by controlling surface morphology, while laser treatment technology [\[187\]](#page-32-0) mitigates dynamic corrosion through microstructure optimization. By 2024–2025, breakthroughs in cermet composite coatings and Si3N4/W composites [[178\]](#page-32-0) are anticipated to enhance protection efficiency by over 70 % through microstructural design and compositional control. Additionally, the environmental barrier coating developed by Dong et al. is expected to further expand the applicability of materials in extreme environments [\[188\]](#page-32-0). This progression not only reflects the iterative refinement of coating technology, from single-protection approaches to multi-mechanism collaboration, but also offers a comprehensive solution for the long-term, stable operation of high-temperature molten salt systems, achieved through the deep integration of material compatibility assessments, failure mechanism analysis, and innovations in surface engineering.

#### **9. Conclusions and outlook**

Due to their unique properties, molten salts play a significant role as thermal storage materials for medium and high temperatures. This paper provides a comprehensive summary of the preparation methods, properties, and recent developments in the application of molten salt CPCMs. It specifically highlights the use of solid waste as a backbone structure and examines the corrosion impacts of molten salts on commercial thermal energy storage encapsulation materials. The following key conclusions can be drawn from the review:

- 1. In the production of molten salt SSPCMs, key factors like the melting temperature range, latent heat, and cost play a critical role in determining the choice of molten salts. In addition, to successfully fabricate composites, the skeletal material should be physically and chemically compatible with the molten salt at elevated temperatures to better accommodate the liquid molten salt during the fabrication process.
- 2. Researchers have made significant strides in enhancing the performance of MSPCMs by integrating various support materials. These

advancements include improvements in thermal conductivity, antileakage properties, and thermal stability. However, challenges remain. For instance, while support materials can enhance thermal conductivity, they may reduce latent enthalpy. It is suggested that for carbon porous materials, the mass loading within the composite should be kept between 3 % and 20 % to optimize the balance between latent enthalpy and thermal conductivity. Although porous carbon materials hold potential as support structures, they are susceptible to oxidation at elevated temperatures. Additionally, the chemical compatibility of these materials with MSPCMs is not yet fully established, highlighting the need for further research into effective surface modification techniques to enhance compatibility and performance.

- 3. The typical encapsulation techniques for MSPCMs include core-shell encapsulation and shape stabilization approaches. These methods are effective in creating stable structures and enhancing the thermophysical properties of the materials. The sol-gel technique, a promising approach for microencapsulation, can produce stable capsules using inorganic substances like SiO2. However, the shortcoming is that the current preparation process regarding microencapsulated MSPCMs is complicated and cannot be mass-produced in practical commercial applications. As a result, there is a critical need to investigate alternative microencapsulation methods for MSPCMs.
- 4. Due to their superior shape stability and modular construction, molten salt-based CPCMs can be seamlessly incorporated into various TES systems, leading to substantial improvements in the overall system efficiency. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the performance-boosting capabilities of the molten salt CPCMs composite module. However, so far, the application cases using molten salt CPCMs in such systems are relatively rare. Therefore, it is important to fully utilize such CPCMs and integrate these thermal energy storage devices based on CPCMs into energy storage systems for practical applications.
- 5. Owing to the excellent thermal storage capability of molten salt CPCMs, related researchers have explored their potential application areas. However, there are still considerable shortcomings in the structural design of TES systems utilizing these CPCMs. To address these issues, further exploratory mechanical tests, particularly those focusing on corrosion effects, are needed to deepen our understanding of stress corrosion cracking phenomena. Comprehensive measurements of thermophysical properties should be prioritized, especially for widely used chloride, carbonate, and fluoride salt systems, and the development of corresponding novel corrosion protection hardening strategies. Moreover, optimization modeling efforts should take a more integrated approach, exploring various complex geometries to enhance system design and performance. Tackling these challenges will enhance the performance and efficiency of TES systems that incorporate molten salt CPCMs, thereby facilitating their wider adoption in energy storage applications.
