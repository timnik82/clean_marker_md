# Recent advancements in latent heat phase change materials and their applications for thermal energy storage and buildings: A state of the art review



#### *Keywords:* Phase change materials (PCMs) Advanced PCMs Thermal Energy Storage PCM Applications

# ABSTRACT

Phase change materials (PCMs) have received substantial interest for their ability to store and release latent heat for energy conservation and thermal control purposes. PCMs are available in a variety of latent heat and melting points but their performance is low due to low value of thermal conductivity which limits its usage. The addition of highly thermal conductivity nanoparticles, porous metal foams and encapsulation methods have been used to address this issue and try to fix the low thermal conductivity of PCMs which is broadly discussed in this manuscript. The ability of PCM to store and release the thermal energy prompted the researchers to use it in potential applications. The energy retained and emitted by PCMs may be used for a variety of purposes, such as in photovoltaic (PV) panels, thermoelectric generators, building air-conditioning, air and water heating systems, heat exchangers, desalination solar stills, textiles, thermal management of electronic equipment and batteries and food packaging. Therefore, in this review, first the advancements in thermal properties of PCMs are thoroughly discussed in terms of enhancement in melting and solidification rates. After that, the use of PCMs in various applications is then explored, and conclusions are drawn accordingly. Based on analysis of recent literature, it was discovered that the phase transition temperature, phase transition enthalpy and thermal conductivity are three important parameters for the selection of an appropriate PCM for use in various applications. The current status of these advanced energy storage materials is also presented in this review. Lastly, some challenges and future recommendation are also proposed for future researchers which will bring a revolution in thermal management field.

Analysis was done by ANN analytically, gave authentic results with less computation time and cost. Akbari et al. [\[37\]](#page-29-0) used two-phase mixture model to analyze the heat transfer and fluid flow characteristics of laminar Cu/H2O nanofluid in a 3-D curved microtube. The results were illustrated for coefficient of friction, Nusselt number patterns, dimensionless axial velocity and dimensionless temperature profiles. Results showed that the due to centrifugal and buoyancy forces, the axial velocity profiles showed no symmetry. As the number of nanoparticles enhanced, isotherms attained the desired temperature quickly.

Goshayeshi et al. [\[38\]](#page-29-0) dispersed various types of Fe2O3 NPs into the Kerosene to investigate the closed-loop pulsating copper heat pipe thermal behavior. Results showed that the addition of NPs enhanced the thermal performance of pulsated heat pipe, magnetic field reduced the thermal resistance by 12% packed with gamma Fe2O3 NPs. Gamma Fe2O3 NPs of 20 nm size were observed to be best candidate for attaining better thermal performance.

The use of nanofiber technologies for stability of PCMs is also one of the most recent approaches to the study of these materials. Researchers have proposed and studied the use of PCMs for cooling of electronic equipment and batteries. Their findings revealed that the heat sink performs well before the PCMs fully melts. After PCM has fully melted, the heart sink's productivity begins to deteriorate rapidly. The latent heat capacity of PCMs with suitable transition temperature can be used to provide thermal comfort and minimize building loads. In recent years PCMs use in building heating–cooling systems and hot water supply has received a lot of attention due to diverse variety of melting temperatures of PCMs. Accumulating solar thermal energy during daytime and supply it during night for heating application may be a practical option for lowering the building's energy costs.

# **Various advancements in PCMs**

PCMs have drawbacks like low thermal conductivity, leakage, volume change during phase transition, flammability, super cooling, corrosiveness, and poor stability. Thermal conductivity is a critical property of PCM that influences heat transfer capacity and its low value reduces thermal response rate [\[75\]](#page-30-0). A variety of techniques have been investigated in literature to improve the performance of PCMs that include addition of NPs, adding expanded graphite [\[76,77\],](#page-30-0) and preparing eutectic PCMs [\[20\]](#page-29-0), porous metal foams [\[78,79\],](#page-30-0) heat pipes [\[80,81\]](#page-30-0) and capsulation of PCM as represented in Fig. 3. Encapsulation, or microencapsulation, is the process of completely covering and isolating the core from its surroundings. The thermal stability and leakage resistance of PCMs increased significantly after encapsulation, greatly expanding the application domains of PCMs. Microencapsulation is described as a physical or chemical reaction that produces a homogenous and stable shell material enveloping the core material. On the basis of the formation mechanism and shell state, microencapsulation methods may be divided into three categories: physical, chemical, and physicochemical [\[82\]](#page-30-0).

The initial stage in producing microencapsulated PCM and nano encapsulated PCMs is often the production of an emulsion (oil/water). The dispersed phase is primarily comprised of one or more PCM. The composition and synthesis technique of the coating around the PCM droplets vary depending on the synthesis methodology. The emulsion polymerization, suspension polymerization, and coacervation encapsulation techniques are primarily used to synthesize PCM encapsulated in organic polymers. Interfacial polymerization and in situ polymerization techniques are used to synthesize PCM encapsulated in organic and inorganic coatings. PCM encapsulated in inorganic coatings is usually produced by the sol–gel technique [\[83\]](#page-30-0). The various capsulation methods of PCM are summarized in Table 1.

Therefore, the above discussed are some ways to increase PCM thermal performance that recently gained significant attention.

#### *Addition of nanoparticles in base PCM*

The addition of high thermal conductivity NPs improves the heat transfer within PCMs. NPs function as a link for thermal energy transfer since they have a low thermal resistance. Nano enhanced PCMs (NEPCMs) are being studied due to high thermal conductivity of NPs and their effect of enhancement of heat storage and retrieval rate of PCMs. There are a lot of studies which explore the influence of NPs parameters like size, shape, materials and concentration on the PCM thermal properties. It is reported that the heat transfer rate and thermal reliability of PCMs have improved with the addition of NPs. The nonmetallic NPs have better influence on thermal characteristics of PCMs than metallic NPs [\[86\].](#page-30-0) Thermal conductivity of NEPCMs is reported to enhance by 20% to 100% as compared pristine PCM. The NPs addition in salt hydrates PCM improve their sub cooling, phase segregation and thermal conductivity properties [\[87\].](#page-30-0)

Nizetic et al. [\[88\]](#page-30-0) critically reviewed studies of NPs enhanced fluid in terms of preparation, applications and thermal properties. Findings showed that the use of NPs in PCMs increased the thermal conductivity but marginally lowered latent heat. In TES and cooling systems, NEPCMs are mainly used. Authors concluded that literature does not provide the optimal preparation process for nanofluids, economic and environmental assessments, safety concerns and handling guidance.

Wu et al. [\[89\]](#page-30-0) explored the thermal conductivity of paraffin by dispersing graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) NPs. The dispersant WinSperse3050 was added for stable dispersion of NPs in PCM. Dispersion of 5% GNPs in PCM enhanced the thermal conductivity by 37.1% than pristine PCM. Melting/freezing rates and thermal stability of PCM were improved by the addition of CNTs and GNPs. Kalidasan et al. [\[90\]](#page-30-0) prepared nanocomposite of polyaniline and cobalt NPs and mixed it with paraffin wax in various proportions (FESEM image shown in Fig. 4). According to the findings, enriching the PCM with polymer nanocomposite increased the latent heat of fusion and thermal conductivity by 15.9% and 20.4%, respectively and improved thermal stability for 200 thermal cycles.

Daneshazarian et al. [\[91\]](#page-30-0) numerically inspected the thermal performance of halogen graphene nanoplatelets (xGNP) enriched PCM packed in vertical cylindrical cavity at different xGNP concentrations. Results showed that with 0.5 wt% of xGNP in the PCM, melting rate decreased by 9.7% and heat storage rate improved by 12.6%. Authors further noted that the inclusion of NPs in PCM by more than 0.5 wt% decreased melting rate and heat storage rate, as well as increased the dynamic viscosity. Liu et al. [\[92\]](#page-30-0) prepared hybrid NPs through coating carbon layer on aluminium NPs and dispersing them in sodium sulphate decahydrate for thermal efficiency enhancement. According to the findings, the use of 3 wt% hybrid NPs increased thermal conductivity by 26.41%, decreased the freezing latent heat enthalpy by 5.13%, and strengthened the stability of PCM.

Raj et al. [\[93\]](#page-30-0) studied thermal characteristics of shape-stabilized polyethylene glycol dispersed with 1 wt% MWCNTs and GNPs. Various thermal, structural, and chemical analysis demonstrated that the NEPCM possessed better thermal response, good chemical and thermal stability. Results concluded that addition of MWCNTs and GNPs in PCM enhanced the thermal conductivity of PCM by 61.73% and 84.48% respectively and lowered the heat sink base temperature by maximum of 9.77%. Benbrika et al. [\[94\]](#page-31-0) numerically analyzed the melting/solidification characteristics of a latent heat TES unit of horizontal cylinders by diffusing GNPs in PCM. According to the findings, with the addition of 3 wt% GNPs in PCM, solidification time was reduced by 50%, melting time slightly reduced and energy storage capacity was reduced by 14%.

Sun et al. [\[95\]](#page-31-0) investigated melting process of TES system utilizing paraffin PCM and graphite and nano coconut shell charcoal. The concentration of NPs was 0.02, 0.06, and 0.10 wt%. It was found that the melting time shortened by 21% with 0.06% graphite nano particles in PCM with 2 wt% oleic acid dispersant. Qu et al. [\[96\]](#page-31-0) studied the face stabilized paraffin with the hybrid nano additives of expanded graphite (EG)/MWCNTs and EG/carbon nano fillers (CNF). The shaping material for paraffin was polyethylene and the concentration of NPs and EG was varied. According to findings, PCM thermal conductivity enhanced by 60% and 21.2% for MWCNTs/EG and CNF/EG for mass ratio of 4:1 respectively compared to case of5% EG.

Shahsavar et al. [\[97\]](#page-31-0) performed experimental and numerical study to evaluate the effect of temperature and concentration of NPs on viscosity and thermal conductivity of paraffin/Fe2O3 PCM. For improving NPs dispersion, oleic acid was utilized. According to findings the increase in NPs concentration augmented both "k" and viscosity of nanofluid while the increase of temperature enhanced the "k" and decreased the viscosity. Furthermore, the artificial neural network (ANN) model was utilized to simulate the "k" and viscosity of nanofluid using experimental data. It was observed that the developed ANN model provided accurate results.

Arici et al. [\[98\]](#page-31-0) explored the melting of PCM with various arrangement of fins, various wall orientations, and CuO NPs. As compared to the no fin case, the integration of fins in the cavity shortened the melting time by maximum value of 52% and 68% based on fins length and fins position, respectively, with short fins performing better with NPs. While it was observed that the combine use of fins and NPs augmented the melting rate by 54%. Sarani et al. [\[99\]](#page-31-0) examined the effect of discontinuous fins configuration and CuO NPs on PCM solidification rate enhancement. Findings showed that the inclusion of discontinuous fins increased the discharging rate by 89%. Maximum enhancement in discharge rate of 77.8% and 59.5% was observed for the combination of CuO NPs with copper and aluminium discontinuous fins respectively. Sheikholeslami et al. [\[100\]](#page-31-0)explored the melting/freezing of RT35 PCM with triangular fins and CuO NPs in triple tube heat exchanger. The use of triangular fins increased freezing rate by about 40.75%, and the addition of CuO NPs lowered freezing time by 44.88%. The combine use of fins and NPs in the system lowered the solidification time from 20.2 min to 11.13 min. Alizadeh and colleagues [\[101\]](#page-31-0) investigated numerically the solidification process of latent heat TES device with curved fins and numerous NPs. The results revealed that with 4% single walled carbon nanotubes, increased solidification rate of PCM by 40.9% and optimized fin shortened the freezing time of PCM by 61.54%. The combine use of optimized fins and NPs increased the rate of solidification by 68.81 %.

## *Use of fins along with base PCM*

PCMs have good energy density, and constant phase change temperature but the major drawback of PCMs is weak heat transfer during melting/freezing cycles especially in case of organic PCMs. So for accelerating the PCM responsiveness for heat transfer, increasing contact area by utilization of fins is widely researched technique due to favorable outcomes compared to no fin case[\[102,103\].](#page-31-0) Different types of fins i.e. rectangular [\[104\]](#page-31-0), longitudinal [\[105,106\],](#page-31-0)radial [\[107,108\]](#page-31-0), annular [\[109\]](#page-31-0), tree [\[110,111\]](#page-31-0), plate [\[112\]](#page-31-0), spiral [\[113,114\]](#page-31-0), triangular [\[115\],](#page-31-0) trapezoidal [\[116\],](#page-31-0) corrugated [\[117\],](#page-31-0) perforated [\[118,119\],](#page-31-0) helical [\[120\],](#page-31-0) and some others shapes [121–[124\]](#page-31-0) have been studied for optimizing the geometric shapes, size, orientation and materials of fins. In recent studies, various types of fins along with NEPCMs and metal foams are utilizing for thermal performance enhancement of latent heat thermal energy storage systems [\[125,126](#page-31-0)–131]. By using various type of fins with base PCM, the effect of heat transfer is increased due to large area availability.

Ren et al. [\[132\]](#page-31-0) studied the improvement in melting of PCM by incorporating triangular fins and copper nanoparticles in latent heat enclosure. The results showed that the melting rate of PCM was improved with triangular fins as compared to rectangular fins and the addition of NPs. The long and slender triangular fins were recommended for attaining high energy storage efficiency. Hasnain et al. [\[133\]](#page-31-0)  investigated numerically the melting of PCM by utilizing branched fins and Al2O3 nanoparticles in horizontal latent heat TES system. It was revealed that the double branched fins lowered melting time by 45.9% and inclusion of 10% Al2O3 nanoparticles in PCM reduced melting time by 26.8% as compared to simple PCM case.

Zhang et al. [\[111\]](#page-31-0) designed and investigated the PCM based TES system with novel shape fins. Topology optimization technique was used to optimize the PCM and fins layout in TES system. Results showed that the optimal volume fraction of fins to be 20%, while charging and discharging rate of PCM enhanced by 46.8% and 47.1% respectively compared to longitudinal fins case. Huang et al. [\[134\]](#page-31-0) investigated the charging and discharging behavior of fractal tree shaped fins based latent heat TES system. Results showed that inclination angle influence melting process more than freezing process. The vertical fractal latent heat system gave 52.5% reduction in melting time while horizontal fractal latent heat system gave 94.7% and 101.5% improvement in melting and freezing rate respectively.

Yang et al. [\[135\]](#page-31-0) studied the thermal performance of irregularly spaced annular fins based shell and tube latent heat TES system. Results indicated a 62.8% reduction in total melting duration, 34.4% temperature difference reduction in PCM region and 84.7% improvement in melting rate uniformity.

Liu et al. [\[136\]](#page-31-0) investigated the solidification ability of latent TES system using novel triangular shaped longitudinal fins depicted in Fig. 5. Aside from fin geometry, the impact of initial temperature and fin material on solidification rate was also investigated. The results showed that the novel fin shortened solidification time by 38.30% as compared to the simple longitudinal fin case. The optimal temperature gradient between the inner wall and the PCM was over 20 K for a quick solidification rate.

Kothari et al. [\[137\]](#page-31-0) studied the effect of varying the inclination angles of paraffin base heat sinks with plate fins. According to the findings, for critical temperature of 75 C, three finned heat sink at a 0◦ inclination angle increased operating time by 74%, while a decrease in inclination angle from 90◦ to 0◦ enhanced operating time by 66%, 46%, and 43% for no fin, one fin, and two fin PCM based heat sinks, respectively. With reduction in inclination angle from 900 to 0◦, the PCM melting time for unfinned and finned heat sinks was reduced by 44 % and 30 %, respectively.

# *PCMs saturated with porous materials*

Metallic and non-metallic porous foams saturated with organic paraffin type PCMs are being explored for their thermal performance enhancement of PCMs and their possible usage in diverse applications. The integration of porous materials and PCMs results in shape-stabilized PCMs which have better thermal conductivity and no leakage issues

[\[40\]](#page-29-0).

Yao and Wu [\[138\]](#page-31-0) explored the thermal characteristics of composite PCM of copper metal foam and paraffin by varying the foam porosity and pore density. The low porosity and high pores density were observed as ideal for the uniform distribution of temperature and low temperature maintenance. Copper foam with porosity of 0.935 and pore density of 7 pores per inch (PPI) augmented the melting rate by 2.1 times and maximum temperature drop of 30 ◦C was achieved. Zhang et al. [\[139\]](#page-31-0)  explored the charging behavior of paraffin saturated with copper foam under varying centrifugal accelerations. By the centrifugal acceleration, the heat transfer coefficient and the PCM convection have been improved. The melting rate of PCM increased asymmetrically in the direction of centrifugal acceleration, resulting in phase interface with a curve pattern. Hu and Gong [\[140\]i](#page-31-0)nvestigated the thermal regulation of RT 62HC PCM infiltrated with 3D printed porous aluminium material. The research used a variety of porosities and heat fluxes with various thermocouples (TC) positions. The results revealed that for 80% porosity of porous material, the highest increase in PCM melting time and temperature reduction of 78% relative to the case without porous material was achieved (charging/discharging figure shown in Fig. 6).

Ferfera et al. [\[141\]](#page-31-0) used numerical simulations to analyze the microcellular foam impregnated in paraffin PCM. The effect of porosity on PCM based heat sink thermal conductivity and thermal management was examined. The thermal conductivity of copper and nickel foams with porosities of 96.5%, saturated in PCM, was 28 and 8 times higher than pure PCM, respectively. Porosity reduction improved phase transition rate and increased thermal diffusivity and effusivity. Dinesh and Bhattacharya [\[142\]](#page-32-0) numerically explored the effect of metal foam pore size on PCM thermal behavior. According to the findings, the pore size had a significant impact on the melting rate of PCM with the same porosity, with a smaller pore radius resulting in quicker melting and energy storage. Although the melting rate for large pore sizes was initially high, it gradually decreased. Lower porosity resulted in a higher rate of energy storage for pore sizes of equal scale.

LoIasiello et al. [\[78\]](#page-30-0) explored the melting process of PCM saturated with aluminium metal foam under various heating loads, porosity, number of PPI and orientations. Results revealed that the lower porosity decreased the melting time, while PPI had no effect and orientation had marginal effect on melting time. Cumulative energy analysis showed that higher energy stored by PPIs under vertical direction and porosity had little impact on the energy storage as low porosity meant less PCM. Hasan et al. [\[143\]](#page-32-0) performed thermal investigation on paraffin metal foam composite, prepared by prepared vacuum impregnation method. Nickel and aluminium metal foams were used and maximum impregnation ratio of 98% was achieved. The results showed that paraffin/ nickel and paraffin/aluminum composite thermal conductivity was 6 times and18 times higher than PCM respectively. An empirical model for thermal conductivity prediction has also been developed by the author.

## *Shape-stabilized PCMs*

Shape-stabilized PCM is another process in which molten PCM is held in place by capillary action in porous material. Shape-stabilized materials include mineral materials like diatomite, vermiculite and perlite, as well as polymeric materials such as polyethylene, polyurethane, or acyclic resins. Mineral porous materials are mostly used to hold organic PCMS, while polymeric porous materials are used to contain both organic (mainly paraffins) and inorganic PCMs. The methods adopted to prepare the shape-stabilized PCMs include physical adsorption, electrospinning, centrifugal spinning, and intercalation. The selection of suitable porous material is influenced by porosity, thermal conductivity, availability, chemical consistency with the PCM, and preparation techniques [\[144,145\]](#page-32-0).

Atinafu et al. [\[146\]](#page-32-0) developed a composite PCM of n-dodecane PCM that used biochar-CNTs as a supporting material. According to the findings, biochar-CNTs and CNT assisted PCM had PCM loading capacities of 70.2% and 83.2%, as well as latent heat of 127.4 J/g and 152.3 J/ g, respectively. Besides composite PCM showed better chemical uniformity, form stability, and thermal stability up to 600 C with considerable weight loss. Also, Atinafu and colleagues [\[147\]](#page-32-0) investigated the shape-stabilized PCM of eicosane and biochar harvested from wheat straw and softwood. According to the results, the composite PCM of eicosane-wheat straw biochar had the highest TES capacity of 75 J/g and 37.1% encapsulation capacity, while the composite PCM of eicosane-softwood biochar had the lowest TES capacity of 52 J/g and 26.4% encapsulation capacity.

Li et al. [\[148\]](#page-32-0) used silicon carbide powder, silicon carbide whisker, and silicon carbide nanofiber supports to prepare composite stearic acid PCMs by vacuum impregnation and ethanol techniques. According to findings, the thermal conductivities of composite PCMs were 61.80% to 169.29% moreas compared to pure PCM. Besides, the composite PCM demonstrated improved charging/discharging behavior and TES capacity. Chriaa et al. [\[149\]](#page-32-0) prepared the shape-stabilized PCM by using hexadecane and various styrene-b-(ethylene-co-butylene)-b-styrene (SEBS) copolymer concentrations with polyethylene coating. The composite PCM was reported to be melted at 25 ◦C with latent heat of 162.3 J/g. With good form stability, PCM loading approached 80% and thermal deterioration of the composite PCM commenced above 200 C.

Shen et al. [\[150\]](#page-32-0) produced shape-stabilized PCM by impregnating cellulose sponge with an emulsified hydrated salt/paraffin composite. According to the findings, shape stabilized PCM increased thermal stability, provided high storage density, decreased super cooling, and had negligible leakage. The phase shift enthalpy of shape stabilized PCM was found to be 227.3 J/g. Xie et al. [\[151\]](#page-32-0) prepared shape-stabilized composite PCM by physically mixing the eutectic mixture (Na2HPO4-12H2O and Na2CO3-10H2O) with diatomite and coating it with polyurethane acrylate. Due to polymer coating on the composite PCM, leakage was repressed, and salt hydrate supercooling was precluded. High latent heat of fusion of 102.6 J/g, as well as increased thermal reliability and stability was attained. Shape-stabilized composite PCM also minimized high peak temperatures and temperature variations.

# *Performance enhancement using capsulation of PCM*

Addition of NPs increases the viscosity and causes agglomeration in the base fluid due to a stabilization problem [\[69,86\].](#page-30-0) One approach to increase PCM performance include encapsulation of PCM [\[152\].](#page-32-0) PCM encapsulation is a recent advancement in the field of TES that addresses problems in PCMs such as phase change leakage, low thermal conductivity, low thermal stability, corrosion, super cooling, and environmental deterioration. There are several kinds of encapsulation based on capsule size, such as macroencapsulation, microencapsulation, and nanoencapsulation, although the microencapsulation is the most researched size.

The shell material selection is influenced by several factors, including the operating temperature range, thermal conductivity, toxicity, the encapsulation method, permeability, and compliance with the base PCM. Organic shell materials include melamine–formaldehyde, urea–formaldehyde, polystyrene, polyurethane, and polymethyl methacrylate, among many others. Inorganic shell materials include silica, calcium carbonate, and titania. Some of the methods to prepare encapsulated PCMs are polymerization, coacervation, sol–gel, ionic gelation, spray drying, electrostatic, and solvent evaporation. Each these PCM encapsulation methods has its own combination of benefits and drawbacks, the encapsulation approach should be chosen based on the type of PCM, cost, desired encapsulation efficiency, thermal stability, and reagent accessibility [\[152,153\].](#page-32-0)

Nikpourian et al. [\[154\]](#page-32-0) using interfacial polymerization process prepared nano encapsulated paraffin wax with polyurethane. The aim of the study was to examine nano capsules' chemical composition, thermal properties, and morphology. The nano capsules had an energy storage efficiency and capacity of 80.2% and 97.5%, respectively, as well as melting/freezing phase transition enthalpies of 153.9/142.3 J/g. After 100 heating/cooling cycles, the prepared core shell structure demonstrated high thermal stability. Zhang et al. [\[155\]](#page-32-0) performed thermal analysis of nano encapsulated PCM of octadecane core with pentafluorostyrene shell. According to results nano encapsulated PCM presented encapsulation efficiency of 76%, melting enthalpy of 171.8 J/g, and the best thermal performance at shell/core mass ratio of 1:2. Pandey et al. [\[156\]](#page-32-0) prepared encapsulated PCM of n-eicosane core and shell of amphiphilic copolymer of poly methyl methacrylate and poly 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate by double emulsion technique (SEM image shown in [Fig. 7](#page-8-0)). According to the findings, the encapsulated PCM had a 95% loading efficiency, 160 J/g latent heat, uniform thermal behavior after 500 heating/cooling cycles, and no leakage issue.

Abdeali et al. [\[157\]](#page-32-0) prepared nano encapsulated PCM by using a mini emulsion polymerization process, with paraffin wax as the core and polyurethane as the shell. The small particle size distribution was observed when the sonication time was increased from 15 to 20 min and the reaction temperature was raised from 60 to 80 C. It was observed that the narrow particle size distribution of core/shell resulted in uniform melting and a lower temperature rise. Tian et al. [\[158\]](#page-32-0) used coacervation method to prepare MEPCM paraffin with gelatin-sodium alginate as shell material. According to the findings, encapsulation ratio of 82.12% and encapsulation efficiency of 72.5% was attained with melting latent heat and temperature of 126.8 J/g and 60.4 C and freezing latent heat and temperature of 51.3 C and 133.8 J/g, respectively. As compared to pure PCM, MEPCM gave high thermal stability, no leakage, and less decomposition. Yu et al. [\[159\]](#page-32-0) prepared MEPCM containing paraffin core and double-layer shell with first layer tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) hydrolyzed Silica and second layer of modified spherical silica. Results demonstrated the MEPCM improved thermal conductivity by 133.33% and sustained sealing at 65 C. The PCM loss was postponed by 47 ◦C and latent thermal energy approached 166.61 J/g.

Suresh and Pethurajan [\[160\]](#page-32-0) investigated the thermal characterization of MEPCMs fused with GNPs in a finned heat sink. The PCM shell was made of polymer, with NPs concentrations of 0.5, 1, and 3 wt%. Results revealed that the thermal response rate of MEPCM with GNPs increased by 29.6 % during heating at the set point temperature of 60 ◦C. At 10 W, the time delay to reach 80 ◦C was 22.5 % for microencapsulated PCM and 18 % for PCM with 3 wt% GNPs.

Rakkappan et al. [\[161\]](#page-32-0) studied PCM encapsulated in urea–formaldehyde shells modified with GNP and CuO NPs at concentrations of 2, 6, and 10 wt%. Results revealed that as opposed to the unencapsulated PCM scenario, the encapsulated PCM with 6 wt% GNPs and 10 wt% CuO NPs resulted in charging, discharging time reductions of 25.22 %, 31.01 % and 31.91 %, 37.02 %, respectively.

Bergamo et al. [\[162\]](#page-32-0) prepared nano encapsulated PCM by coating TiO2 shell on soybean oil derivative, which was then tested for its potential use in TES system. Because of the TiO2shell, the thermal resistance of encapsulated PCM was reduced by 70% compared to unencapsulated PCM. While the cooling time of water increased by 18%, the thermal energy retained in TES system was 10.6% higher than blank TES.

Wang et al. [\[163\]](#page-32-0) explored the thermal performance of various PCM encapsulating shapes for free and forced convection. The studied shapes were spherical, cylindrical, tetrahedral, pyramidal and biconcave disc red blood cell. The tetra-n-butylammonium bromide based PCM with phase transition range of 0–12 C was employed as PCM. Results showed that red blood cell shape encapsulated PCM outperformed other shapes due to rapid melting/freezing processes which were further improved by free stream flow of heat transfer fluid.

Mohaghegh et al. [\[164\]](#page-32-0) investigated the thermal properties of noctadecane PCM encapsulated in a pear-shaped geometry. To establish the restriction in geometry, a thin insulated cylindrical rod was positioned in the centre of pear-shaped cavity. According to findings, the PCM encapsulated in pear-shaped geometry took 178 min to melt in constrained scenario and 97 min in unconstrained case. Furthermore, the pear-shaped design took less time to melt (97 min) than a standard cylindrical cavity (108 min).

Ishak et al. [\[165\]](#page-32-0) synthesized MEPCM using the sol gel technique, utilizing stearic acid PCM as the core and SiO2 as the shell material. Tetraethyl orthosilicate was employed as precursor to create the SiO<sup>2</sup> layer on the stearic acid. Thermal investigation revealed that MEPCM possessed melting/solidification temperatures and latent heats of 70.37 C, 64.27 C and 182.53 J/g, 160.12 J/g, respectively. It was reported that the maximum encapsulation efficiency was 86.68%.

Chen et al. [\[166\]](#page-32-0) prepared composite eutectic salt PCM by infusing

the sodium thiosulfate pentahydrate and sodium acetate trihydrate eutectic salt with marco-porous carbonized melamine sponge. The composite eutectic salt PCM was then encapsulated in silicon carbide modified polyurethane shell. The macro encapsulated PCM was subjected to comprehensive thermal and mechanical evaluation. The results indicated that macro encapsulated PCM displayed improved mechanical strength and thermal conductivity, as well as outstanding thermal stability and thermal reliability.

Shi et al. [\[167\]](#page-32-0) utilized acetylated cellulose nanofibrils as the shell and paraffin PCM as the core to prepare MEPCM. PCM and acetylated cellulose nanofibrils content was altered in various proportions. The leakage proof MEPCM demonstrated high energy storage capacity of 173 J/g, high PCM loading of 80%, outstanding thermal stability after 100 heating/cooling cycles, decreased super cooling, and effective shape stability.

Xing et al. [\[168\]](#page-32-0) prepared MEPCM by employing eutectic of capric and palmitic fatty acids as the core and polyvinyl chloride as the shell. For encapsulating, the author utilized the solvent evaporation technique. Using a core shell ratio of 1:2, the MEPCM displayed melting/ solidification temperatures of 17.1 C 18.8 C, latent heat values of 92.1 J/ g, 91.3 J/g respectively, and the encapsulation ratio of 57%. MEPCM demonstrated outstanding thermal reliability after 500 thermal cycles, and improved thermal stability.

Cai et al. [\[169\]](#page-32-0) used dodecanol dodecanoate PCM as core material to prepare MEPCM via interfacial polymerization of Toluene 2, 4-diisocyanate, diethylene triamine. Solvent-free technique was used in the polymerization of MEPCM. According to the results, the MEPCM had spherical shape, latent heat range of 103.4–140.3 J/g, strong thermal resistance with commencing evaporation temperature of 234 C, and enhanced thermal conductivity.

Sari et al. [\[170\]](#page-32-0) studied the heptadecane MEPCM with calcium carbonate shell for leakage prevention and thermal conductivity enhancement. Results showed that the prepared MEPCM had outstanding thermal durability, enhanced thermal conductivity, long term stability, high latent heat capacity. # *PCMs usage in solar stills/desalination systems*

Energy and water scarcity are two of the world's most pressing challenges, all of which influence a country's overall economic situation. Around a third of the world's countries face water scarcity, and about 97% of the earth's water is seawater, which is unfit for consumption or crop cultivation. 2% of available freshwater is frozen, and 1% is underground, canals, rivers, and reservoirs [\[187\]](#page-32-0). However,

microbes, trace elements, industrial waste have polluted this water [188–[190\]](#page-32-0). Solar distillation appears to be a feasible choice, and solar stills are a research priority due to the geographical position of many countries with significant solar energy capacity [\[191\]](#page-33-0). The usable heat from the sun can be used in the solar still in the same way as rain does. After evaporation, saline water in a solar still can be drained and used as freshwater. By varying different parameters, a lot of research has been done to advance the effectiveness of traditional solar stills. Because of its high energy storage capacity, PCM has been considered in improving solar still efficiency [\[192\]](#page-33-0). Recently, plenty of experimental and theoretical research has been done in the literature which is discussed below.

For fresh water generation, Kumar et al. [\[193\]](#page-33-0) compared the performance of single slope solar still using PCM and NEPCM to the standard passive solar still. A solar still holding 7.5 L of water was filled with 5 kg of PCM, and 0.5 percent mass silica NPs were introduced to the paraffin PCM. The incorporation of PCM and nanoPCM increased freshwater production by 51.22% and 67.07%, respectively, compared to simple solar still according to the results. In the Tehran climatic conditions, Jahanpanah et al[.\[194\]](#page-33-0) examined the inclusion of lowtemperature PCM of 3 kg and 6 kg amounts in the desalination solar still shown in the [Fig. 9](#page-10-0). The insertion of 6 kg PCM in the solar still increased daily productivity by 30.3% and improved efficiency by 8.29%, suggesting that increasing PCM quantity improved solar still performance.

The solar still with external condenser enclosing paraffin wax PCM and evacuated tube collector was tested experimentally by Faegh and Shafii [\[195\].](#page-33-0) During the daytime, PCM extracted waste heat from condensed vapor from saline water, and in the evening, it released heat, assisting the desalination operation. The results showed that using solar still with an external condenser enclosing PCM improved distillate output by 86%, with a gross yield of 6.55 kg/m2 /day, a 50% efficiency, and a cost of 0.015 USD/L/m<sup>2</sup> . Harahsheh et al. [\[196\]](#page-33-0) explored the effectiveness of a solar still system by employing inorganic salt PCM and solar collector. Different parameters such as hot and cold-water circulation rate and basin water levels for distillation productivity were evaluated. The findings indicated that the higher ambient temperature and high hot water flow rate were closely connected with improved solar still efficiency, the optimum cooling water flow rate of being 10 m/ s, while an increase in water level in the basin decreased the productivity. The maximum daily yield of 4.3 L/m2 /day was obtained, and 40% yield obtained after sunset. Arabi et al. [\[197\]](#page-33-0) theoretically investigated a solar still with a solar collector and various PCMs enclosed in tubes (as presented in Fig. 10) to improve the solar still efficiency at various parameters. The findings showed that increasing solar irradiation, installing an additional collector, increasing coolant flow rate, and increasing PCM mass improved the solar still system's efficiency substantially. Solar still productivity for simple solar still, with external collector, and with external collector and PCM tubes was 2.8, 5.1, and 6.31 L/m2 respectively, after 24 h of operation.

Kateshia and Lakhera [\[198\]](#page-33-0) examined the efficiency improvement of single slope solar still with PCM and aluminium pin fins. The output, energy efficiency and exergy efficiency of the solar system still with PCM/pin fins augmented by 30%, 47% and 63% relative to a conventional scenario; and costs per liter was 0.017 USD/L/m2 .

Vigneswaran et al. [\[199\]](#page-33-0) inspected the thermal efficiency of a conventional solar still combined with single PCM and a double PCMs to optimize the distillate yield. The latent heat of PCM was used to provide contingency yield during periods of low irradiation time. Finding showed that the solar still with two PCMs enhanced the yield discharge time by 25% as compared to the single PCM case. Multiple PCM solar stills had a 46.29% efficiency and yield of 4.40 L/m2 /day, which was higher than a single PCM and conventional solar still case.

Kabeel et al. [\[200\]](#page-33-0) examined the enhancement of distillate output of

tubular solar still by using copper tubes packed with PCM, during the day and night time. According to results, the output and daily efficiency of traditional solar stills were approximately 4.3 L/m<sup>2</sup> /day and 33%, respectively, while solar stills with copper tubes enclosing PCM generated output of about 9 L/m2 /day and daily efficiency of around 72%. Mousa et al. [\[201\]](#page-33-0) investigated the candle wax (tricosane) filled in copper tubes of solar desalination system. The experiment used 3 kg of tap water and variable mass of PCM. Presence of PCM resulted in charging and discharging zones, in which temperature was highly influenced by PCM, with the first zone resulting from PCM melting and the second arising from PCM solidification. According to the findings, PCM had a negative effect on water output during the day but a positive impact at nighttime.

Elashmawy and colleagues [\[202\]](#page-33-0) designed novel paraffin filled aluminium tubes for performance enhancement of tubular solar still. To boost radiation absorption, a solar tracking device was mounted on the parabolic solar concentrator, and copper rods were inserted into paraffin wax to improve thermal behavior. Solar still with PCM tubes improved output and efficiency by 40.51% and 38.25%, respectively as shown in the Fig. 11. PCM tubes solar still resulted in output, efficiency, and cost of 5.55 L/m2 /day, 44%, and 0.00782 USD/liter, respectively. As opposed to current solar desalination methods, a 21.8% cost reduction was achieved.

Sarhaddi et al. [\[203\]](#page-33-0) numerically studied the exergy and energy efficiency of the PCM based weir type cascade solar still. The influence of solar radiation and ambient temperature for sunny and cloudy days were studied. Results showed that the total energy and exergy efficiency of solar still without PCM on a sunny day and with PCM on a cloudy day were 76.69%, 6.5% and 74.35%, 8.59%, respectively. For cloudy days, PCM-based solar still was recommended, while for sunny days, solar still without PCM was preferred. The largest irreversibility rate of solar still with and without PCM was for absorber plate; according to the author, the absorber plate should be the subject of research for design optimization. *PCMs as thermal energy storage in building air conditioning/HVAC applications*

Building air conditioning is a high energy consumption practice worldwide; with heavy environment impacts i.e. global warming and climate change [\[213,214\].](#page-33-0) Since temperature and demand fluctuate on a regular and seasonal basis, conserving energy in buildings is very challenging [\[215\].](#page-33-0) Nearly 40% of global energy demand is accounted for by the building sector [\[216\]](#page-33-0). Heating and cooling loads account for a substantial portion of overall energy consumption in buildings. To meet peak electricity demand and lower costs, utility companies usually give customers discounts based on time-of-use pricing. During off-peak hours, the time of use market system provides lower power costs, while during peak hours, energy is more costly [\[217\]](#page-33-0).

Various researches have been done research using TES materials, to increase building thermal comfort, decrease peak load, and minimize electricity demand [\[218\].](#page-33-0) Thermal energy storage materials can reduce the peak energy demand by storing extra energy and providing excess energy at peak hours. Because of the high heat storage capability and feasible melting and freezing temperatures, PCM is an efficient TES medium in this regard [\[219\]](#page-33-0). Studies have shown that enclosing PCM in homes [\[41\]](#page-29-0), such as HVAC modules [\[220,221\]](#page-33-0), solar water heaters [\[222,223\],](#page-33-0) and other building systems like envelopes [\[218,224,225\]](#page-33-0)  improves energy storage and utilization significantly. PCM undergoes phase transition due to daytime temperature variations in PCMintegrated building envelopes, reducing the drastic impact of ambient temperature variations of indoor temperatures, lowering energy demand for space conditioning, and improving occupant thermal comfort [\[226\].](#page-33-0)

Various innovations such as inclusion of NPs [ ,](#page-33-0) metal foams[231–[233\],](#page-33-0) expanded graphite in PCM [234–[238\]](#page-33-0), PCM encapsulation [\[16,239](#page-29-0)–242], and eutectic PCMs techniques [\[243](#page-34-0)–247] have been used in research to improve PCM efficiency and thermal characteristics for building thermal comfort.

Sarri et al. [\[248\]](#page-34-0) numerically investigated the impact of macroencapsulated PCM in aluminum rigid slabs using shaded or unshaded lightweight buildings in various Algerian cities. Simulations were carried out using EnergyPlus and GenOpt tool to determine to optimum melting temperature of PCM enclosed in building envelope. Results concluded that the use PCM improved the overall thermal comfort and optimized the annual energy consumption during summer and winter season. The use of shading devices along with HVAC system was favorable during summer season with optimal melting temperature of 28 C of PCM, while for winter season shaded device was not recommended and ideal melting temperature of PCM was between 19 C and 21 C.

Kishore et al. [\[249\]](#page-34-0) explored the thermal load regulation by embedding PCM in building envelope (walls shown in [Fig. 12](#page-12-0)). Various parameters of PCM were studied including location in the wall, melting temperature and layer thickness etc. The results showed that the inclusion of PCM in the wall reduced heat gain during peak hours by 70%. Maximum thermal load regulation occurred when PCM was adjacent to interior of building and transition temperature was 24 C to 25 C with the transition temperature being the most important determining factor.

Li et al. [\[250\]](#page-34-0) explored the thermal characteristics of hybrid PCM wallboards and compared with the gypsum wallboard for the climate of Shanghai. Three types of PCMs were microencapsulated in the wallboards in different configurations. Results showed that the PCM inclusion in wallboard reduced power consumption by 30% while maintaining optimal thermal comfort. Boussaba et al. [\[251\]](#page-34-0) performed thermal analysis on non-cocoa vegetable fat bio PCM latent heat TES material for building thermal management. The bio PCM was inserted in 56 wt% in the board and recycled graphite introduced in board to improve thermal conductivity. Results concluded that the novel bio PCM could be potential candidate in buildings application for hot and cold climates due to suitable performance. Li et al. [\[252\]](#page-34-0) prepared composite sodium sulfate decahydrate (SSD) PCM for building floor heating application. Expanded graphite (EG) was inserted in composite PCM in various proportions by vacuum impregnation to enhance the thermal conductivity stability. Results revealed that wit 7% expanded graphite in composite PCM, heating and cooling time reduced by 36.2% and 44.5% compared to pure sodium sulfate decahydrate as shown in [Fig. 13](#page-12-0)  and thermal conductivity enhanced by 3.6 times compared to pure sodium sulfate decahydrate.

Wi et al. [\[253\]](#page-34-0) prepared shape-stabilized PCM by inserting expanded perlite and expanded vermiculite into n-octadecane by vacuum impregnation method and inserted this composite PCM into red clay in various concentrations to perform thermal analysis. The peak temperature dropped by 1.6 ◦C during phase change of PCM, and the time lag in phase transition was around 1.33 h. Hekimoglu et al. [\[254\]](#page-34-0) prepared novel shape stable composite PCM using silica fume and capric acid/ stearic acid eutectic mixture to investigate the thermal storing and releasing properties in building applications. The composite PCM was introduced in ordinary cement mortar in weight fraction of 10%, 15% and 20%. Results revealed that latent heat-based cement mortar had temperature difference of 2.48 C and 1.71 C for heating and cooling stage respectively compared to ordinary cement mortar and presented suitable properties for indoor thermal regulation.

Yu et al. [\[255\]](#page-34-0) prepared two novel shape-stabilized composite PCMs by physical blending of organic alcohols 1-tetradecanol and 1-hexadecanol into diatomite. PCM based wallboards were constructed and attached to outside surface of test room to compare its thermal performance against polystyrene plastic wallboard. Results concluded that polystyrene wallboard was efficient during daytime energy savings and composite PCM based wallboard was effective for nighttime energy savings. Kong et al. [\[256\]](#page-34-0) coupled passive composite PCM wallboard and active solar thermal energy storage system to form a hybrid heat supply system for winter. #### **Major challenges and future recommendations**

The use of phase change materials (PCM) in energy storage applications has received a lot of attention. Following are some challenges recommendations for careful consideration based on this study. These suggestions can be summed up as follows:

- Even though a great deal of research is being conducted on the thermophysical and chemical properties of PCMs for their use in different applications, there is still no international standard for PCM testing and analysis. Furthermore, the thermophysical properties of PCMs are the subject of much debate in the literature.
- The materials with the best thermal properties, such as paraffin wax or fatty acids, are highly flammable.
- Organic PCMs have major drawback of low thermal conductivity which negatively effects its utilization in applications.
- The cost of nanomaterials, instability, and agglomeration are all significant obstacles to the widespread use of nanomaterials based PCM.
- In recent studies, nanoparticles have demonstrated to have promising as a PCM additive. However, there is a shortage of studies into hybrid nanomaterials' implementations. As a result, further research into the thermal properties and performance analysis of hybrid NPs based PCM is needed.
- NEPCM preparation is time-consuming and expensive processes. Although studies have shown that NEPCMs have higher thermal conductivity than single PCMs, it has also been found that the phase enthalpy of PCMs decreases when nanoparticles are added. - Encapsulation of PCMs is costly and time consuming, give poor thermal characteristics. Further research needs to be done to optimize the PCM encapsulation process.
- Although most PCMs and NEPCMs have defined melting points, but thermophysical properties of liquids and solids vary amongst studies. A standard test procedure and database should be established to yield correct results and records.
- For several years, researchers have tested and analyzed the thermal conductivity of PCMs using a variety of techniques. The usage of various values of PCM thermophysical properties revealed a major problem with research criteria.
- To maintain stable performance of the storage systems, future work must avoid phase separation and supercooling of PCMs.
- It is suggested that the use of PCMs in various sectors should be assessed in terms of their effect on the atmosphere and human health.
- The fundamental feature of an energy storage system is phase transition enthalpy. So, detailed examination of the existence of interactions between PCM and nanostructures, as well as their potential impact on the performance of these systems is needed.

PCMs are classified as a category of special materials with excellent potential in latent heat storage technologies, as they can retain and release a significant amount of energy when experiencing phase transition process. The major concluded points are:

- PCMs have a high phase transition enthalpy but have a poor thermal conductivity. Various methods have been used to solve the problem of poor thermal conductivity, and studies have shown that dispersing different high thermal conductivity nanoparticles to PCMs accelerate the phase transition rate and improve the energy storage/release capacity of PCMs.
- In most studies, adding nanoparticles lowers the melting temperature and raises the freezing temperature.
- Composite PCMs are developed by impregnating PCMs with porous materials such as metallic foams, carbon foams, and expanded

- graphite. Prepared composite PCMs are important for improving the PCM's thermophysical properties and preventing leakage issue.

However, further research is needed to improve current encapsulation techniques and make them commercially available.
