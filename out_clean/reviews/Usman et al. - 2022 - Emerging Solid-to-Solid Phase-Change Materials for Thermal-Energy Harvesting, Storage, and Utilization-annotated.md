# **Emerging Solid-to-Solid Phase-Change Materials for Thermal-Energy Harvesting, Storage, and Utilization**

*Ali Usman, Feng Xiong, Waseem Aftab, Mulin Qin, and Ruqiang Zou\**

**Phase-change materials (PCMs) offer tremendous potential to store thermal energy during reversible phase transitions for state-of-the-art applications. The practicality of these materials is adversely restricted by volume expansion, phase segregation, and leakage problems associated with conventional solid-liquid PCMs. Solid–solid PCMs, as promising alternatives to solid–liquid PCMs, are gaining much attention toward practical thermal-energy storage (TES) owing to their inimitable advantages such as solid-state processing, negligible volume change during phase transition, no contamination, and long cyclic life. Herein, the aim is to provide a holistic analysis of solid– solid PCMs suitable for thermal-energy harvesting, storage, and utilization. The developing strategies of solid–solid PCMs are presented and then the structure–property relationship is discussed, followed by potential applications. Finally, an outlook discussion with momentous challenges and future directions is presented. Hopefully, this review will provide a guideline to the scientific community to develop high-performance solid–solid PCMs for advanced TES applications.**

PCMs are an extensive class of materials, in which thermal energy is stored in the form of heat of fusion during the phase-change process and remains stored till the materials endure a reverse transition process.[9,10] Phase transition temperature and heat of fusion are the requisite parameters of PCMs. The phase transition temperature is a temperature at which

material starts changing its states from one phase to another. The heat of fusion also termed as latent heat of fusion, refers to the amount of thermal energy absorbed or released during the phase transition of PCMs. The conceptual diagram of phasechange energy storage and release is presented in (**Figure 1**a). The working mechanism of PCMs involved storing and release of thermal energy at constant temperature followed by intermolecular interactions. The reversible breaking and formation of hydrogen bonding are accountable for the storage and release of thermal energy during melting and solidifying process. Under thermal charging, PCMs first perform like a sensible heat-storage material during which thermal energy is stored resulting in a temperature rise, but, when the melting temperature is achieved, then the materials are stored energy as latent heat from the melting process at a constant temperature. When melting is completed, further thermal action results in the rise of temperature analogous to sensible heat storage. Upon cooling of melted PCMs, the PCM starts to freeze and release an amount of latent heat about equal to melting enthalpy, when freezing temperature is attained. The thermo-physical properties of PCMs are governed by thermodynamics equilibrium,[11] and can be represented by the Gibbs free energy equation;

∆ = *G H*∆ −*T S*∆ (1)

For a first-order phase transition, the Gibbs free energy of the solid phase equals to liquid phase and therefore Δ*G*S→<sup>L</sup> = 0, giving the relation of Δ*H*fusion = *T*<sup>m</sup> Δ*S*fusion. Where *T*m indicates the melting temperature, Δ*H*fusion is the measure of latent heat of fusion, and (Δ*S*), is the change in entropy after phase transition.

Generally, PCMs are classified into three main categories organic (paraffin, fatty acid, and polymers), inorganic PCMs (salt hydrate, metal, and alloys), and their eutectic mixtures based on their composition.[9] They typically undergo four kinds of phase transition processes solid-to-liquid (S–L), solidto-solid (S–S), solid-to-gas (S–G), and liquid-to-gas (L–G) phase transition, or vice versa. Among them, S–L and S–S phase transitions are the most suitable for TES-related applications.[12–14] The S–L PCMs change their states between solid and liquid on account of the reversible breaking and formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonding or supramolecular bonding upon thermal action, which are accountable for the storage or release of thermal energy correspondingly. These PCMs have been broadly covered by various researchers in the past for the applications of TES including solar-TES, off-peak electricity, and infrared stealth technologies.[9,15–24] Nevertheless, S–L PCMs suffer from high volume expansion and leakage of the liquid PCM during multiple phase-change cycles, which hindered their utilization at advanced levels.

In this regard, tremendous attention has been paid to encapsulate S–L PCMs to prevent leakage and make them

reliable for practical TES. However, this technology is still limited to producing high-performance encapsulated PCMs on large scale. For instance, nano-encapsulated PCMs exhibit better performance and shape-stability but mass production of these PCMs is challenging. On the other hand, macro-encapsulated PCMs suffer from poor thermal properties and large volume expansion which ultimately results in leakage over repeated cycles. Moreover, the inclusion of inactive shell materials or supporting materials causes a significant decline in energy storage capacity. In contrast to S–L PCMs, S–S PCMs go through less change in volume and entropy ensuring their effectiveness in practical TES. These PCMs offer an elegant solution to overcome these issues toward the potential of TES applications due to solid-state switching.[25–28] S–S phase transitions are usually observed between the different crystalline forms of materials or polymorphs in a single component system. The working attitude of these materials involves molecular re-orientations, from one ordered crystalline phase to another disordered semi-crystalline solid phase without shifting into isotropic liquid phase to store or release thermal energy by changing temperature or pressure (Figure 1b). They generally exhibit first-order phase transitions that undergo discontinuous changes in phase-change enthalpy, changes in volume, and entropy owing to a crystal-packed structure. The extent of these changes is often observed much less as compared to conventional S–L PCMs, which makes them preferable and optimum choice in the field of TES applications. Further, these PCMs also present desirable characteristics such as smaller phase separation and no degradation upon thermal cycling.[29,30] Even though the mechanical strength slightly shrinks after phase transformation in some cases of S–S PCMs, they normally maintain their bulk solid properties in a certain range of temperature, and their core role regarding leakage remains intact, thus they are also known as solid-state PCMs. The inherent thermal characteristics of S–S PCMs over S–L PCMs are presented in (Figure 1c).

Despite the growing importance of S–S PCMs, in-depth analysis of these PCMs is not sensed about the fundamentals of structural–thermal relationship with mechanical and physical properties toward TES. Therefore, there is an urgency to talk about the latest scientific literature progress on S–S PCMs in a thought-provoking way. Significant advancements have been made in the past on S–S phase transition materials for thermalenergy applications by means of different materials polyalcohols, polymeric, inorganic, and hybrid which are briefly summarized in (Figure 1d). To the best of our knowledge, only one single report has been made on S–S PCMs. However, they only focus on one type of molecular study at a smaller scale and did not study thermal-energy systems toward the adoption of these materials at a broader scale.[28]

Here, we provide an inside view of various types of S–S PCMs, from the point of molecular engineering, solid-state phase transition, structure–plasticity property correlation, and topological arrangement identifying the execution of these materials into an unexplored sector of the field. We hope that this review will support the scientific fraternity to view a comprehensive understanding of materials design developments and their innovative results in the field of S–S PCMs for hightech TES applications.

# **2. Overview of S–S PCMs**

S–S PCMs are divided into three major families of materials to study their molecular and structural rearrangement occurring in solid-state phase transitions for the potential of TES has been illustrated in **Figure 2**. Organic molecular crystals exhibit energetic S–S phase transitions from one crystalline structure to another crystalline lattice configuration with the ease of polymorphic behavior. The family of organic S–S PCMs mainly includes polyalcohols/plastic crystals (PCs), polymeric, and organic-salt-based materials. Generally, organic S–S PCMs lie between 25 and 185 °C with their phase-change enthalpies from 15 to 270 kJ kg<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> . Polymeric S–S PCMs involve reversible phase transitions from a crystalline solid phase to another semi-crystalline or amorphous phase. Poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) and poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) are primarily employed as phasechange units incorporated into polymer skeleton through different chemical cross-linking polymerizations methods for the assembly of polymeric S–S PCMs. These materials are effectively corresponding to the low- and mid-range of temperatures that lie between 15 and 68 °C with phase-change enthalpies of 15–183 J g−<sup>1</sup> . Polyalcohols is a sub-class of organic PCMs that undergo solid-state transformations from layered or chain structures to partially disordered ones with suspected rupture of hydrogen bonding over the function of temperature or pressure from low to high. These materials are typically applied in thermal-energy applications, that is, solar water heating and domestic heating owing to their reversible crystallographic arrangement. PCs also exhibit solid-state phase transformations analogous to polyalcohol. Recently these materials have gotten substantial attention as an alternative eco-friendly solution for refrigeration systems due to their magnificent behavior toward stimuli-controlled phase transition within solid-state processing.

Organic salt/protic ionic liquids are also another sub-class of organic PCMs that usually undergo S–L and S–S phase transition. These PCMs normally operate in the temperature range of 90–208 °C during S–L transitions for solar TES and wind applications. But, herein we only focus on such salts that exhibit S–S phase transitions. For instance, pyrazolium sulfonate [Pzy] [CF3SO3], guanidinium triflate [Gdm][CF3SO3], and guanidinium ethane sulfonate [Gdm][C2H5SO3] are the only salts that are presenting S–S phase transition in the temperature range of 59–115 °C for domestic water-heating applications. They mainly consist of organic cation and organic/inorganic anions or the substituent group and hydrogen bonding is the main driving force within the crystal packing structure during transitions. Inorganic S–S PCMs are such kinds of PCMs that can reversibly store and release thermal energy through order–disorder transition with the help of crystallographic structure transformation and magnetic transformation. The inorganic PCMs family mainly includes metallic and ceramics-based materials. The phase transition temperatures of these materials range from 30 to 120 °C with phase-change enthalpies falling between 25 and 70 J g−<sup>1</sup> , which is relatively less than organic S–S PCMs. Further, inorganic-salt-based S–S PCMs are more suitable for high-temperature applications having an operating temperature range of 590–800 °C. Hybrid PCMs are another kind of S–S PCMs that is a combination of organic and inorganic materials mainly including perovskites and organometallic-based materials. Hence, this article is categorized into three major categories of S–S PCMs including organic, inorganic, and hybrid materials to discuss their molecular structure and topological arrangement for advanced TES applications. The widespread adoption and commercialization of S–S PCMs are directly associated to a notable extent with their abundant availability and cost. Most of the organic S–S PCMs have been prepared with extensively available, low-cost starting materials which makes a straightforward pathway toward practicality. In contrast, inorganic S–S PCMs are typically obtained by metallic alloys, and optimization is needed to tune the intrinsic properties relationship when integrated for advanced utilization. The hybrid S–S PCMs have been prepared by relatively complex synthetic schemes that ultimately increase mass production costs. So, in the present situation, emerging categories of organic S–S PCMs including polymeric and PCs are being considered an optimal choice for innovative applications likely wearable thermal management applications and solid-state refrigeration technology applications, respectively. The phase transitions temperature of these materials is near ambient temperature, which is a necessary parameter when considering these applications. In addition, the flexibility and thermal storage capacity of these materials can be tuned by controlling the molecular weight for wearable applications.[31,32]

On a concluding note, certain parameters are required regarding materials consideration and commercial-engineering aspects toward potential thermal-energy applications. For instance, thermal management of the human body by using S–S PCMs has been extensively studied for the fabrication of smart textiles to solve the leakage issue of liquid PCMs during phase transition, which is the main hindrance in PCM-integrated textiles. The key problem is significantly addressed by using polymeric-based S–S PCMs by providing a relatively stable and comfortable temperature range for human. # **3. Strategies for Developing S–S PCMs**

Until now, several developing strategies have been proposed for the functioning of S–S PCMs. The developing strategies mainly include molecular rearrangement likely face center lattice to cubic crystal lattice or crystalline region to gel formation stateamorphous region for polyalcohol and polymeric S–S PCMs, respectively, suggesting that the conversion of these materials purely occurs in solid state. These PCMs are typically developed by two kinds of strategies that include certain phase transition mechanisms for different materials. In the first strategy of phase transitions, a specified type of S–S PCMs undergoes an S–S phase transition from one crystalline phase to another crystalline phase with the associated energy fluctuation to absorb or release heat in terms of molecular arrangement. For instance, polyalcohols exhibit an S–S phase transition from sheet-like crystalline structures to face-centered-cubic (FCC) or bodycentered-cubic (BCC) crystal structures. The basic phenomena of these materials involve reversible phase transition from an ordered and layered structure like tetragonal, monoclinic, or orthorhombic crystal geometry to orientationally disordered phases such as FCC or BCC crystal with great symmetry orientation (**Figure 3**a). In short, these molecules involve phase transformation from homogeneous phase to heterogeneous phase upon external effect, that is, temperature or pressure difference while enduring a first-order transition. Therefore, these kinds of materials are also known as PCs representing the solid-state operation in their crystal structure.[33–35]

While, in the case of polymeric S–S PCMs, phase-change motifs are typically integrated into a secondary structure via chemical covalent bonding. This phenomenon helps to prevent leaking the crystallizable constituents when they are in a liquid state (non-crystalline state). Because, the phase-changing constituents are structurally integrated into the macromolecular backbone of polymers through block polymerization[36] or crosslinking polymerization with non-crystallizable moieties,[37–42] side-chain grafting,[43–45] or hyper branching.[46,47] For instance, PEG or PEO are mostly used as the soft segment to attach with polymer skeleton as a hard segment for making linear chains of polymeric S–S PCMs due to the significant advantages of PEG: good biocompatibility, excellent biodegradability, high latent heat, and operational simplicity.[48,49] The PCMs absorb heat through latent heat mechanisms when the soft segment approaches phase transition temperature. The free movement of these soft segments is hindered, as they develop cross-linking network by the backbone of the polymer skeleton that allows the system to keep it in a solid state. The transition temperature of these S–S PCMs could be tuned by varying the chain length of the polymer backbone, adjusting the ratio of soft and hard segments, or changing the soft segments[50] (Figure 3b). A detailed discussion about thermo-physical properties of polyalcohols and polymeric PCMs is provided in latter Section 3.1.

Inorganic S–S PCMs usually involves the absorption and release process of thermal energy upon magnetically induced transition, order–disorder, or crystalline phase transformation, similar to polyalcohols phase transition methodology (Figure 3c). For instance, the development of phase change within ferrite (Fe) and austenite (As), as well as inorganic salt like lithium sulfate, involves a solid-state phase transition.[51,52] Examples of inorganic S–S PCMs include vanadium dioxide (VO2), which typically undergo monoclinic to insulator phase transition over-temperature difference. By tuning the phase transition temperature or by developing the binary system with two different materials, such transformation can also be tailored for TES applications. A comprehensive explanation of inorganic S–S PCMs about thermo-physical properties and potential applications is provided in latter Section 3.2. Hybrid S–S PCMs are another class that includes perovskites and organometallic materials, in which inorganic 2D crystalline sheets as the supporting network are attached with crystallizing segments represented as "R" as alkyl chains through covalent bonding in the second methodology.[53–56] Generally, hybrid S–S PCMs can be defined with the chemical formula (*n*C*n*H2*<sup>n</sup>* + 1NH3)2MX4, where *n*C*n*H2*n* represents long alkyl chains with inorganic counterparts (MX4 −2 ), M indicating a metal atom and X representing halogen group (Figure 3d). It generally exhibits analogous behavior with polymeric S–S PCMs in terms of their transitions. When these segments start absorbing heat energy, crystallizing segments start melting and storing thermal energy. In the reverse process, cooling from its melted state leads to heat release in a reversible process. Nonetheless, the lateral movements of molecules are hindered due to the attachment of inorganic materials (sheet-like scaffolds) with the soft segments. An inclusive discussion regarding thermal analysis and other properties of the hybrid PCMs is given in the Section 3.3.

The differences in thermal properties (phase transition temperature *T*t and latent heat Δ*H*) of S–S PCMs mainly depend on their material types, the strength of the bonds, and structures. Thus, different kinds of S–S PCMs cover wide transition temperature ranges with different phase-change enthalpy for certain TES applications. Accordingly, suitable phase transition temperature and high latent heat storage capacity are the core requirements for each applications scenario to satisfy their adaptability. Based on the applications, some additional parameters also need to be optimized. For instance, the thermal management of the human body for wearable applications pays more attention to multifunction and good mechanical properties such as flexibility and elasticity. Similarly, good washability is required for smart textiles. On the other hand, the reuse of waste heat from an industrial power plant requires stimulicontrolled heat-storage materials, which stored latent heat for longer and are later released on demand irrespective of temperature barrier. Further, the large-scale heat storage represented by solar heat storage needs a high heat-storage density for solar thermal-energy applications. Thermal management of electronic equipment and vehicles requires more focus on efficient heat dissipation and capacity.

#### **3.1. Organic-Based S–S PCMs**

Organic S–S PCMs mainly comprise a specific group of hydrocarbons-based materials that include polyalcohols, or PCs and polymers. Polyalcohols or small polyols are hydrocarbons that have BCC tetrahedral molecular structures near ambient temperature. These polyalcohols and some of their amine derivatives materials typically store and release thermal energy through reversible solid-state phase transformation when reasonably small molecules are engaged in the reversible breaking of supramolecular interactions, that is, hydrogen bonds in the molecular vibrations and lattice vibrations as a function of temperature.[57] Thus, these materials are suitable for cold storage containers and packaging applications in the pharmaceutical and food industry, respectively. In the following sections, we will highlight recent developments regarding polyalcohols and polymeric S–S PCMs for thermal management utilization, space heating in detail, and so on.

#### *3.1.1. Polymeric S–S PCMs*

Among organic S–S PCMs, polymeric S–S PCMs are extensively studied owing to their exceptional properties including operational simplicity, reasonable latent heat storage capacity, and widely selectable phase transition temperature with excellent shape stability and adaptability.[36,40,58,59] These PCMs are usually prepared by two methods including physical and chemical methods. The physical approach mainly involves the direct dispersion of PCMs molecules into a polymer framework as supporting skeletons with higher melting temperatures. In this case, PCMs can be developed by physical bonding of soft segment or S–L PCMs into polymer skeleton through melt blending, soaking, and physical adsorbing action technique to develop a stable polymeric matrix that helps to prevent leakage problems during phase transition. The obtained polymer can maintain its solid state during the S–L phase transition of the PCMs compound until the temperature of the supporting polymer is below its melting point. Thus, these polymeric PCMs are generally termed form-stable PCMs.[14] However, the dispersion of PCMs into polymer supporting matrix may cause large phase segregation problems during multiple melting and freezing cycles, which ultimately limit their practical application.[36,60,61] The chemically cross-linking approach is another method to develop polymeric S–S PCMs including blocking copolymerization, chemical grafting, and chemically cross-linked polymerization to overcome this issue. The cross-linked polymeric S–S PCMs mainly entailed two units; crystallizable identities/soft segments are chemically grafted to a backbone of a polymeric skeleton as explained earlier.

The working phenomena of cross-linking polymeric S–S PCMs generally involved the phase change of the soft segment into another semi-crystalline or amorphous phase rather than transforming into a liquid phase during the heating process. And the crystal structure exhibits reversed behavior when they are cooled below phase-changing temperature. Therefore, polymeric S–S PCMs have been enormously applied in various fashions for effective utilization of TES systems due to their favorable characteristics; negligible volume change, cost-effective, no liquid or gas generation during phase transition, and longer life span. Nonetheless, a slight reduction in latent heat was typically observed in these PCMs, because the mobility of crystallizing molecules is restricted through cross-linking rigid segments. The extent of the decrease in phase-change enthalpy is based on two things; a choice of polymer backbone and phase changing group.[60] Therefore, several modifications in materials design approaches have been reported to improve the latent heat of PCMs. Considerable efforts have been made in the past via integrating PEG chains into several kinds of polymer skeleton materials through different techniques including polystyrene-*graft*-PEG copolymers, grafting 1-octadecanol (ODT) onto an amine cured epoxy cross-linked network, polyesters, and cross-linked polyurethanes (PUs) for the fabrication of polymeric S–S PCMs.[39,45,61–68] Further, various kinds of cross-linking segments including trihydroxy surfactants, that is, Tween 80 and span 80, hexahydroxy compounds, that is, dipentaerythritol, pentaerythritol (PE), sorbitol, and inositol as well as *β*-cyclodextrin were also being used for the preparation of PUs-based S–S PCMs.[68–72] Among them, PUs-based S–S PCMs systems have earned remarkable attention for effective utilization of TES systems in real-life applications owing to their fascinating thermophysical characteristics; excellent thermal stability and good mechanical properties. The prominent preparation method of PUs-based S–S PCMs is chemically cross-linking polymerization by which PEG and its derivatives are chemically covalently bonded with supporting skeletons like hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI) and diphenylmethane diisocyanate (MDI) and then cross-linked with various chain extender, that is, 1,4-butane diol,[36] PE,[37] and PVA[73] through the formulation of urethane linkages (**Figure 4**a). The TES performance of PUs-based S–S PCMs is primarily influenced by several factors; 1) varying the structure of a molecule, 2) free movement of the soft segment or crystallizing group, and 3) cross-linking density present in the network. In view of the TES performance and their applicability, significant advancements have been made by our group in the development of PUs-based S–S PCMs by varying PEG molecular weight to adapt its intrinsic properties toward state-of-the-art TES applications.[31,32,74,75] For instance, a kind of flexible PCMs is particularly favorable for human body thermal management to thermo-regulated textiles or practical wearable applications. In this context, our group has made notable progress in the development of solar-driven thermal-storage fabric with the impregnation of phosphorene encapsulated into PU-based S–S PCMs system to improve the washability, which was also coated on commercial cotton fiber to check its compatibility with the skin for practical applications. In designed assembly, phosphorene transforms solar energy into heat which is stored inside the PCMs following solid-state processing (Figure 4b).[74] The recognized fabric stored latent heat at about 51.3 J g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> , which is mainly depending on how much PCM content was used in the fabric. The exceptional solar energy storage performance of the integrated PCM displays remarkable potential in solar-driven thermal therapy of the human body as well as other heating devices. Further, ultra-flexible and reliable S–S PCMs based on PUs were also developed by our group for TES applications by varying the molecular weight of PEG segments and the degree of crosslinking density within the polymeric network (Figure 4c).[32] It was determined that fabricated PUs-based S–S PCMs with a molecular weight (PEG-3000) exhibited favorable flexibility and reasonable latent heat storage capacity that making them an optimal choice for applications of flexible PCMs. The resultant PCMs are potentially applied for wearable smart textiles and thermal temperature control system in electronic devices owing to their favorable flexibility and mechanical properties over a wide range of temperature.[32,76] The fabricated PCMs exhibited adaptable phase-change enthalpy and mechanical properties by tuning the molecular weight of PEG, endowed better thermal management for the human body and wearable devices. Moreover, recently explored flexible PCMs thin film can be considered for wearable devices. The authors developed a stable and super tough type of S–S PCMs with *π*–*π* stacking through a block copolymerization.[77] In the presented design, PEG, as PCM units were primarily attached to the MDI, served as polymer skeleton and biphenol through a two-step cross-linking reaction. The rigid groups containing benzene rings within PCMs afforded strong intermolecular forces, that is, hydrogen bonding and *π*–*π* stacking, resulting in PCMs displaying robust mechanical properties and shape stability through the solid-state operation (Figure 4d).[77]

Another recent and important development was made to design intrinsically flexible phase-change film through a chemically cross-linked network reaction with the combination of PEG, melamine, and toluene diisocyanate as a cross-linker reagent.[78] The as-prepared PCMs film displayed ultra-flexible characteristics and exhibits a suitable phase-change enthalpy by tuning the phase transition temperature from 5 to 50 °C to tailor its functionality toward potential applications. This is mainly attributed due to the chemically cross-linked PEG chains into the entire polymer network, which implies the presence of S–S phase transitions characteristics. In addition, these films exhibited excellent energy storage efficiency of ≈93.5% which can be achieved with applied current 1.5 A, corresponding to stable energy conversion efficiency during the heating and cooling process (Figure 4e,f).[78] Further, the temperature control performance test revealed a controlled temperature for stitched lab coats and bending items over the phase transitions temperature range of 39.5–49.5 °C (Figure 4g) demonstrating their wearability and temperature control stability as these factors are highly desirable for the human body and portable electronics, respectively, in an extreme environment. The IR images of the lab coat presented excellent shape stability and flexible thermal control devices during the heating and cooling process in both conditions (sewed and bending) from 21 to 89 °C.

On the other hand, several thermoset polymeric S–S PCMs were prepared through solvent-free radical polymerization in which PEG as a phase-change unit was permanently bonded to the polymer skeleton via the chemical cross-linking network.[42,79,80] These S–S PCMs also endowed great potential for TES applications, that is, buildings and the textile industry due to the vigorous physical properties with good thermal stability and mechanical properties. Nevertheless, the majority of polymeric S–S PCMs were prepared by conventional chemical cross-linking methods to keep their shape stability above the phase switching temperature. Even though they have better physical and thermal properties with decent LHS capacity, they still have a big concern regarding workability and recyclability, as they cannot be reprocessed due to permanent or irreversible chemical bonds similar to thermosetting poly-

mers. Further, these PCMs generally do not show enough ductility due to the absence of solid-state plasticity during fabrication.[39]

In this connection, influential strategies have been put forth through dynamic covalent chemistry including the formation of hydroxyl-ester bonds,[81] hindered amine bonds,[82] reversible dynamic urea bond,[83] oxime carbamate bond,[84] aliphatic disulfide bond,[85] aromatic imine bond,[86] and Diels–Alder reaction for the fabrication of recyclable polymers in the last few decades. These dynamic cross-link networks provide the possibility of obtaining a more reliable and thermally stable nature of covalent bonds than the conventional cross-linking networks. The dynamic covalent bond present in urethane,

ester, Diels–Alder, and oxime carbamate bond can be triggered by light, heat, and chemical reagents. Thus, polymeric S–S PCMs developed by the dynamic cross-linking network are being more realistic strategies and gaining significant interest for TES owing to their good solid-state plasticity, good mechanical property, and excellent recyclability, which ultimately help to limit the waste pollution.[81,87–89] Wu et al. designed a novel type of S–S PCMs (DC-PCMs) by using a two-step dynamic cross-linking polymerization. In which the PEG segments serving as the PCM unit was first attached to the backbone of polymer scaffolds like HDI trimer and furfuryl alcohol and then the intermediate product was formed. In the second step, the intermediate product was further reacted with bis(4-maleinmidophenyl)methane through a thermally reversible Diels– Alder reaction connecting furan and maleimide (**Figure 5**a).[90] PEG chain is mainly attributed to storing and releasing thermal energy. The authors' finding recognized that the chemical cross-linking existing inside the PCMs is responsible for the formation of the S–S phase transition. The developed DC-PCMs structure presents good shape stability and keeps its original shape without any leakage behavior, as the PEG segments are chemically connected to the polymer backbone to retain their original shape. The polymeric framework in PCMs imparted shape stability and vigorous mechanical properties that can be modified via adjusting the PEG molecular weight. Further, thermally reversible Diels–Alder reaction with a relatively larger temperature (89.8–121 °C) than the phase switching temperature of PCMs varying between 39 and 71 °C exhibited the admirable thermal recyclability and solid-state plasticity of the designed PCMs. The S–S PCMs samples could be plastically redesigned into varied eternal forms at 120 °C because of the structural manipulations instead of displaying liquid variability (Figure 5b).[90] Conclusively, the as-prepared DC-PCMs endowed myriad unique properties toward the potential of TES applications as compared to conventional cross-linked PCMs owing to the reversible energy storage ability excellent thermal reliability, and solid-state plasticity. Following similar routes, another report on PEG-containing S–S PCMs was successfully prepared through dynamic ferric ion cross-linking polymerization by Wang et al.[91] The overall fabrication of developed S–S PCMs was a complex cross-linking network that involves four stages including preparation of pre-polymer, dissolved citric acid in DMF, formation of a carboxylate group (COO<sup>−</sup>) ended group pre-polymer by adding triethylamine owing to the interactions of COO<sup>−</sup> with ferric ion (Fe<sup>3</sup><sup>+</sup>) leading to the formation of highly cross-linked network of S–S PCMs, followed by the cross-linking structure. The authors pointed out that, the developed polymeric S–S PCMs exhibit solid-state transition even at 95 °C, and no leaking of liquid was noticed during phase transition because of the dynamic ion crosslinking network structure. #### *3.1.2. Polyalcohols*

Polyalcohols, especially small polyols, are potential candidates for TES applications that undergo a solid-state phase transition. These molecules usually undergo reversible phase transitions from a low temperature ordered layered structure, that is, tetragonal, monoclinic to a high temperature orientationally disordered FCC or BCC phase, referred to as plastic or orientationally dispersed crystal. Thus, these materials are also known as PCs. Fundamentally, PCs are a sub-class of polyalcohols, however, all the polyalcohols and amine derivatives do not give the phenomena of PCs. These molecules usually undergo firstorder solid-state transitions from low temperature ordered layered structure to an orientationally disordered phase known as the PCs phase.[33,113]

Polyalcohols-based PCMs have been investigated first as solid-state phase transition since the late 1961s, by J. Timmermanns.[33] In this direction, several papers regarding polyalcohol and its amine derivatives have been reported in terms of their thermodynamic and crystallographic properties for advanced TES utilization.[35,114–121] More importantly, these highly symmetric polyalcohols mainly included PE, neopentylglycol (NPG), tris(hydroxymethyl)amino methane (TRIS), pentaglycerine (PG), trimethylol propane, and 2-amino-2-methyl-1,3-propanediol (AMPL) materials. These molecules absorb a great amount of energy through reversible breaking and the formation of nearest neighbor bonds (H-bond) in the molecular crystals. The raise in phase-change enthalpy is mainly attributed to the breakage of square bridge-type molecules present in an H-bonding interaction when the temperature climbs to its transition temperature. Phase-change phenomena of polyalcohols

are usually experiencing a first-order transition and their Gibbs energy changes are approaching zero.[115,122,123] Thus, these materials have significant potential for passive heating in buildings, that is, domestic water heating systems.

Regarding this, the energy storage performance of pristine polyalcohols like PE, NPG, TAM, and aminoglycol (AMPD) and their multicomponent or binary blends as S–S PCMs for thermal energy was experimentally studied by Gao et al.[124] with the support of calorimetric analysis. They revealed that pristine AMPD is not practically suitable for emerging TES systems because of its hygroscopic identity and wide-ranging charging and discharging temperatures. However, pristine PE has potential for low-temperature solar TES applications. In addition, PE/AMPD mixtures with a low or high weight ratio of AMP can also be considered promising mixtures for functional applications. In another study, the TES performance of several monobasic, binary, and ternary systems which mainly comprised NPG, PE, and TRIS arranged in varied fractions of masses were expressively studied by Yan et al.[117] It was reported that the latent heat of reported mixtures of different polyalcohols like NPG/PE and NPG/TAM/PE, had reasonable thermal storage capacity, but relatively lower than pure polyols. For this, various inorganic salts, that is, magnesium chloride or sodium sulfate are being employed to enhance the enthalpy of these materials.[125] But, the problem is that, such composite PCMs exhibit S–L phase transition instead of S–S phase transition with the practice of these kinds of salts. #### *3.1.3. Organic Salts*

Organic salts are relatively a new kind of material in the family of S–S PCMs for renewable energy storage applications due to their intrinsic advantages including chemical thermal stability, and economic viability with non-flammable and environmentfriendly nature. #### **3.2. Inorganic-Based S–S PCMs**

Inorganic-based S–S PCMs are a kind of PCMs that are capable to store or release thermal energy through various switching mechanisms including order–disorder transformation, crystallographic structure transformation, and magnetic transformation from crystal structure to an amorphous structure. Broadly speaking, the extent of phase-change enthalpy of inorganicbased S–S PCMs is usually lower than organic and polymeric S–S PCMs with high phase transition temperatures. Regarding this, a variety of PCMs with S–S phase transformations was studied for achieving a reasonable amount of latent heat or discharge heat at a specific temperature.[51,52,145–148] It was found that magnetic transformations and crystallographic structure are the most widely studied transition among other transitions owing to their intrinsic switching of crystal structure. Nevertheless, such kinds of materials are quite scarcely reported in the literature survey, which can undergo multifold and reversible S–S transformation at narrow temperatures. Besides this, the most adaptable application of inorganic S–S PCMs is in industrial power plants to recuperate heat from the irregularly discharged heat or exhaust gases. The basic phenomena of inorganic S–S PCMs in an industrial power plant involved stimuli controlled phase change at high temperatures to store latent heat energy significantly through thermal charging of waste and later on release it during solid-state operation. Generally, inorganic S–S PCMs can be classified into ceramics, metallics, and metal alloys based on their materials composition.

#### *3.2.1. Ceramics*

Ceramics are those materials that are neither organic nor metallic. It may be crystalline, glassy, or exhibits both crystalline and glassy states of matter. In addition, they are typically stiff and chemically non-reactive materials that can be designed for use under high temperature and high heat flux without too much consideration of the serviceability. Recently, ceramics-based heat-storage materials have been gaining substantial attention in terms of energy-saving applications, as they can conserve latent heat energy for a longer time and release accumulated heat on demand by the application of minute pressure. #### **3.3. Hybrid S–S PCMs**

Hybrid S–S PCMs, a relatively new class of PCMs that are mainly composed of organic/inorganic, coordination of metal cations and anions, have earned significant attention for various thermal-energy applications due to their fascinating properties, that is, high thermal stability and non-flammability. Therefore, these materials can be extensively applied for both solar thermal and electric energy storage because of the existence of diverse functional properties like dielectric, optical, and caloric. Although, these materials generally present relatively lower latent heat than most of the S–S PCMs. Besides this, some of the hybrid materials including spin-crossover (SCO) complex and perovskite-oxide-based compounds have gained significant potential for solid-state barocaloric refrigerants systems due to a suitable phase transition temperature in the range of 44–63 °C with reasonable latent heat. The phase transition of these compounds can be tuned by varying the mole fraction of its chemical substitution. Further, these compounds can also be suitable for various solid-state devices with a range of operating temperatures. Herein, we will briefly cover organometallic and perovskites-based complex compounds following reversible S–S phase transitions toward potential thermal-energy applications.

#### *3.3.1. Organometallic S–S PCMs*

Organometallic S–S PCM at present mainly refers to a layered perovskite-organometallics, mainly constituted through alternative layers between organic/inorganic constituents resulting in a sandwich-like crystalline structure. These materials are usually designated as numerical formula (*n*C*n*H2*<sup>n</sup>* + 1NH3)2MX4, where M presents a metal atom, X shows a halogen group, and *n* is the number that typically ranges from 9 to 19 as explained earlier, in which, the organic segments comprised of alkyl chains of *n*-alkyl groups are chemically bonded with the inorganic layer via ionic bonds. The phase transition of these materials mainly includes crystallizing motif chains, whereas, the inorganic components have remained structurally unaffected because the supporting framework is thermally inactive. The crystallizing group is ordinarily melted and undergoes phase transformation phenomena between an ordered state or crystalline structure to a partly crystalline or disordered one over absorption of heat energy. This means that the phase-change enthalpy and melting temperature of these materials are mainly based on the chain length of crystallizing motif and partly based on specific metal and halogen identities within an inorganic group. Nonetheless, inorganic constituents mainly involve the improvement in weight aptitude of organometallic S–S PCMs rather than latent heat capacity. Therefore, the latent heat storage capacity of these compounds per unit mass is relatively lower as compared to the melting enthalpy of normal S–L PCMs. Generally, the S–S phase transition of the hybrid materials exhibits reversible behavior even after multiple cycles with negligible change in volume roughly 5–10% at solid-state transition.[28,56] In this context, several organometallic S–S PCMs have been prepared and categorized employing X = Cl, with a melting enthalpy range from 63.5 to 152.7 J g−<sup>1</sup> following the S–S phase transformation temperature between 20.1 and 95.2 °C.[164–167] Among them, thermal characteristics of Co- and Zinc-based binary combinations were perceived up to mark. The binary combinations of these materials usually presented similar performance to priorly studied polyalcohols blends for carbon atom (C9-C15) at transition temperatures of 75 and 100 °C with latent heat enthalpy between 73 and 110 J g−<sup>1</sup> . [166]

Recently, hybrid halometallates are growing as a favorable candidate means of combining TES with multifunctional designs for new high-tech applications as they have shown better thermal stability with non-flammability behavior over traditional S–L PCMs. In addition, the family of hybrid materials also offers different options for new TES systems based on hybrid porous materials. For instance, evaporative TES systems are designed through metal–organic frameworks.[168,169] Herein, Salgado-Beceiro et al. explored a novel hybrid halometallate [(CH3)3S][FeCl4] as an innovative material for multidimensional-energy storage system about TES and electric energy storage with PCs transition behavior.[170] This material experiences a first-order solid-state plastic transition at ambient temperature with decent enthalpy ≈40 kJ kg−<sup>1</sup> with a transition temperature range between 42 and 29 °C, which is generally suitable for solar TES. It has been revealed that such kinds of material also exhibit multidimensional properties with temperature-induced reversible transformations in its dielectric, magnetization, and electron spin resonance manners associated with phase transition. Therefore, this compound is extremely stimulating for achieving both solar thermal and electric energy storage owing to its coexistence and multifunctional properties. In short, solar radiation can be transformed and conserved as thermal energy by the designed material which brings a significant rise in the magnitude of electric energy that can be stored through capacitors built by this material because of their dielectric permittivity property.

Lately, molecular SCO complex compounds have been recognized as an outstanding contenders in solid-state barocaloric refrigerants.[169,171–173] The examples of SCO complexes include octahedrally coordinated 3d transition metal ion materials that can undergo a reversible transition between two electronic arrangements, low-spin (LS) and high-spin (HS) states on account of their minimum and a maximum number of unpaired d-electron, respectively. The interconversion phenomena between these two states can be activated with the help of external stimuli (change in pressure or temperature and light irradiation, etc.). The antibonding identity of eg orbitals signifies definite flagging and broadening of the metal–ligand interaction in the high spin phase. The higher-spin diversity (2S + 1) ultimately lowers the latent energy of the high spin state improving magnetic as well as vibrational entropy that led to the low spin→high spin switching over relatively increasing temperature. However, a slight upsurge in pressure helps to reduce the volume at a low spin state over a sudden transition that led to a substantial decrease in entropy, which can be harvested as a caloric effect (**Figure 9**a).[173] Further, the potential value of SCO complexes concerning thermal characteristics has been moderately explored for barocaloric refrigeration technology to resolve thermal management issues in a functional device. #### *3.3.2. Perovskites-Based S–S PCMs*

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites compounds are typically cost-effective and easy to obtain with an eco-friendly attitude. These materials are usually represented with the molecular formula; A2MX4, where A refers to pliable organic cation, M is metal ion and X may be Cl, Br, I, COO−, and CN−. The phase transition study of these compounds is usually escorted by symmetry breaking under the influence of temperature. Hence, hybrid-perovskites PCMs can form crystal structures between 0D and 3D concerning symmetry. Broadly speaking, perovskites or layered perovskites are usually blended with organic/inorganic compounds constituents, to yield a new material. These materials generally exhibited reasonable phasechange enthalpy through a completely reversible phase transitions pathway.[174,175] They have resembled behavior similar to organometallic or hybrid halometallates, whose functionality is also linked with the structural transition. Due to interesting features like antiferroelectricity, switchable dielectric, and nonlinear optical properties, perovskite compounds are becoming an excellent candidate for environmentally friendly solid-state cooling device applications.[176–179] Therefore, recently perovskites-based S–S PCMs are attracting significant attention in the field of TES, which can be favorable for solid-state devices with several working temperatures.[54,165,178,180]

In this connection, Uchimura et al. studied the quadruple perovskite oxides RCu3Fe4O12 family (R; rare earth metals and it may be La, Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd) that display variable transition temperatures between (−19 and 95 °C) for dynamic TES.[181] These oxides are normally crystallized in the two divergent transition metals referring to copper Cu and Fe, depicting discrete crystallographic sites of pseudo square *A*- and octahedral *B*-sites correspondingly from low temperature to high temperature through intersite charge-transfer phase transition (**Figure 10**a).[181] In addition to their crystallographic structure, these oxide experiences two divergent sorts of electronic phase transitions including intersite charge transfer (ICT), and charge disproportionation transitions (CD) that rely on the size of the R ion. The definite latent heat of complex perovskites compounds (RCu3Fe4O12) was measured over a function of temperature. Their finding indicated that transition enthalpy of established perovskites-based PCMs regularly drops from 26 J g−<sup>1</sup> with substitution of R→La to 16 J g−<sup>1</sup> , R; Gd with the lowered transition temperature suggests a decrease in TES performances due to its elemental replacement, that is, V1−*x*W*x*O2 (Figure 10b).[181] In short, the replacements of R ion in RCu3Fe4O12 attains the control of working temperature for TES material, as it is decisive for colossal phase-change enthalpy to entropy changes. Nevertheless, a significant reduction was observed in transition entropies over doping of tungsten (W) and chromium (Cr) in V1−*x*(Cr, W)*x*O2. Overall, the author's results endorsed that the replacements of the atoms are implicitly connected to the electronic state of the crystal structure, which helps to keep the TES property and control the transition temperature.

Similarly, a-site-ordered quadruple perovskite-structure oxide NdCu3Fe4O12 presenting favorable latent heat at ambient temperature owing to the ICT transitions, were explained by Kosugi et al.[182] The as-prepared perovskite compound usually crystallizes through a cubic ordered quadruple structure following Nd and copper (Cu) are ordered in 1:3 ratio at the A site, while the iron is placed on the center of the BO6 octahedra. It was also depicted that the inherent changes are constant with an electronic structure changes between the high-temperature state (Nd3+Cu2<sup>+</sup> 3Fe3.75<sup>+</sup> 4O2–12) to low temperature one (Nd3+Cu3<sup>+</sup> 3Fe3<sup>+</sup> 4O2–12) (Figure 10c).[182] The latent enthalpy and entropy change obtained through the study of first-order transitions was found to be 26.4 kJ kg−<sup>1</sup> (158 J cc−<sup>1</sup> ) and 85.3 J K−<sup>1</sup> kg−<sup>1</sup> , respectively. The ICT transition temperature decreases linearly by 301.5 K at 0.1 kbar to 280.5 K at 5.2 kbar corresponding to the pressure coefficient of the intersite transition. The author's results highlighted that experienced entropy changes in the developed structure through BCE is analogous to previously reported inorganic solid materials (Figure 10d,e).[182] This fact is mainly attributed to the presence of unusual magnetic ordering and large volume changes in the crystal structure. Conclusively, ICT oxides like perovskites-based PCMs are promising for TES and efficient refrigeration system as the transition temperature of these compounds can be adjusted by varying the mole fraction of its chemical substitution. Further, these compounds can also be suitable for various solid-state devices with a range of operating temperatures.

#### **4. Challenges Associated with S–S PCMs**

The implementation of S–S PCMs in thermal-energy applications has increased significantly in recent years, owing to their inimitable advantages: solid-state processing, negligible volume change during phase transition, and long cyclic life. Despite these outstanding features, the adoption of these materials in specific engineering applications faces some issues such as lower phase-change enthalpy, and lower thermal conductivity typically in the case of polymeric- and polyalcohols-based PCMs, phase-change kinetics, ease of production, and cost in case of hybrid and inorganic S–S PCMs. Thermal conductivity is a very basic parametric means of thermo-physical properties of PCMs and it is directly linked to the heat transfer rate of the materials.[183] Thus, thermal conductivity is one of the key parameters that must be highlighted in the selection of S–S PCMs for TES applications.

The thermal conductivity of inorganic PCMs such as metals, ceramics, and alloys is relatively higher than organic ones, therefore, less attention has been paid to increasing the thermal conductivity of inorganic-based PCMs. On the other hand, most of the polymeric- and polyalcohols-based PCMs exhibit relatively low thermal conductivity, similar to S–L PCMs. For instance, the thermal conductivity of various polymeric S–S PCMs was found in the range of 0.10–0.15 W m−<sup>1</sup> K−<sup>11</sup> [43,184] which is comparable to S–L PCMs such as organic S–L PCMs, paraffin, and fatty acid. This is mainly attributed due to resemblances in counterparts or molecular structures of polymeric S–S PCMs and organic S–L PCMs. So, it is highly desirable to tune the thermal conductivity of PCMs by adding some a)*K*PCM represents thermal conductivity of PCMs; b)*K*PCM represents thermal conductivity of PCMs composites.

#### **4.1. Thermal Conductivity Enhancement Strategies**

Thermal conductivity enhancement of S–S PCMs has been typically governed by using the S–L PCMs technique and mainly two kinds of additives are being used in this regard: carbon- and metals-based geometries. Regarding this, considerable efforts have been devoted to increasing the thermal conductivity of PCMs.[183,186,187]

#### **4.2. Carbon-Based Additives**

Carbon-based additives include various demission structures. For instance, 0D refers to nanoparticles, 1D indicates nanotubes and nanowires, 2D included nanosheets, and porous 3D. These additives are usually considered an optimum choice to develop the composite with PCMs due to the advantages of high thermal conductivity, and extensive availability. #### **4.3. Metal-Based Additives**

The thermal conductivity of PCMs can also be tuned by using metals-based additives including metal foam, metal nanoparticles, and metal oxide nanoparticles. In this regard, extensive effort has been made by using metal foams and metal nanoparticles for improving the thermal conductivity of PCMs with reasonable change in enthalpy and transition temperature.[196,197]

To conclude, carbon-based materials are relatively more significantly used for promising the composites rather than metalbased-additives, as they earned more favorable attitudes toward potential TES applications due to sealant characteristics: diversity of allotropies, good thermal stability, and high thermal conductivity. Although, metallic materials generally present higher thermal conductivity, while bearing some issues as well like high density and chemical activity that ultimately limits their practicability in various scenarios. Apart from the type of additives, some other factors may also influence the enhancement of thermal conductivity such as dispersity, functionalization, synthesis methods, and degree of extent of alignment.

# **5. Summary and Outlook**

TES technologies play an important role in the sustainable development of modern societies. Among various methods of energy storage, phase-change energy storage is one of the actively followed technologies in terms of storage density, economic viability, and industrial maturity. Despite these outstanding features, the widespread adoption of this technology is adversely limited by volume expansion, phase segregation, and leakage problems associated with conventional S–L PCMs. Being a promising alternative to S–L PCMs, tremendous attention has been paid to developing S–S PCMs owing to their inimitable advantages: solid-state processing, negligible volume change during phase transition, and long cyclic life. Further, these PCMs also offer inimitable opportunities to develop novel and innovative materials that can syndicate energy storage applications with other multifunctional attributes for practical realizations.
