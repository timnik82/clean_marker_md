#### ABSTRACT

The construction industry has seen significant growth in energy consumption over the past few years, primarily due to the demand for heating and cooling in buildings. This increase in energy use presents considerable environmental and economic challenges, making reducing energy consumption in homes a critical issue for many countries. A promising approach to improving energy performance in homes while reducing CO2 emissions is integrating phase change material (PCM)-based thermal energy storage (TES) systems into building designs. This review focuses on using bio-based phase change materials (BPCMs) in TES applications, which could contribute to lower energy consumption in the construction sector. Recent advancements in BPCM technology indicate that these substances, derived from renewable resources such as paraffin and fatty acids, can achieve thermal storage capacities similar to traditional PCMs. BPCMs function like thermal batteries, absorbing, storing, and releasing thermal energy through phase transitions, typically between 20 ◦C and 30 ◦C. This process helps stabilize indoor climates and decreases reliance on mechanical heating and cooling systems. This article comprehensively assesses the properties, thermal performance, and applications of BPCMs in building materials. It also provides an essential evaluation of life cycle assessment (LCA) and financial feasibility. Its purpose is to guide the research, engineering, and policy communities in adopting practical applications for sustainable construction practices. Integrating BPCMs in TES structures is crucial for improving sustainability and energy conservation in the construction industry. This approach offers a viable pathway toward creating more energy-efficient buildings. Furthermore, a comparative analysis is provided, evaluating BPCMs along different varieties of section trade substances, inclusive of natural, inorganic, and eutectic alternatives. This analysis specializes in numerous crucial factors: price, energy performance, and environmental impact. The review addresses challenges such as heat transfer costs, compatibility with conventional building materials, and policy implications.

# *2.2. Thermal energy storage technologies*

Thermal energy storage is a very effective and assuring technology, and it is anticipated to significantly impact the optimization and regulation of thermal energy usage. It permits transient thermal energy storage due to a material's internal energy changing when its temperature rises [[29\]](#page-16-0). Using thermal energy storage technology in building construction can significantly improve overall energy efficiency. These technologies significantly lower total energy consumption because they may narrow the energy gap between provision and requirement [\[32](#page-16-0)]. The three primary methods for efficiently storing thermal energy are sensible heat, latent heat, and thermochemical processes. The applications for which these techniques are employed are determined by the benefits and drawbacks each offers [[33\]](#page-16-0). Thermochemical techniques contain storing energy through reversible chemical reactions. In those procedures, energy is absorbed or released as the chemical bonds are broken and reformed, taking into account high energy density storage. This approach is mainly tremendous due to its capacity to store energy for prolonged periods without sizable losses and its ability to offer lengthy-time period storage solutions. Thermal energy storage for homes offers both qualitative and quantitative advantages. Qualitatively, it enhances indoor comfort via preserving steady temperatures, reducing dependency on outside energy resources, and lowering emissions. Quantitatively, it could cause extensive energy financial savings, with research showing discounts in energy consumption through up to 30 % in homes prepared with efficient TES systems. One instance of a TES system in buildings is the usage of PCMs in partitions or ceilings, which imbibe and release heat to maintain stable indoor temperatures. Another example is the setup of water tanks for storing hot water generated for the duration of off-height hours for later use, lowering the need for continuous heating. Additionally, ice storage systems may be used in commercial homes to provide and store ice at night, which is then used for cooling purposes all through the day. Future advancements in thermal energy storage technology are likely to focus on improving the efficiency and costeffectiveness of existing methods. Innovations in materials science could lead to the evolution of new PCMs with higher energy storage capacities and faster response times. Advancements in thermochemical storage could result in more sustainable and scalable solutions, potentially integrating renewable energy sources for even greater environmental benefits. In residential buildings, TES systems can be integrated into heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems to optimize energy use. For instance, homeowners can utilize solar thermal collectors to store energy during sunny periods, which can then be used to heat water or provide space heating during cooler times ([Fig. 1](#page-3-0)). Furthermore, incorporating advanced PCMs into residential construction materials can greatly enhance energy efficiency by steadying indoor temperatures, repressing reliance on traditional heating and cooling systems, and lowering utility bills.

Sensible heat storage: Thermal energy can be saved for later release by reducing the temperature of a solid or liquid after it has been raised without resulting in a phase change [\[34](#page-16-0)]. The quantity of thermal energy stored may be calculated based on the mass, temperature differential, and specific heat capacity of the storage medium [\(Fig. 2](#page-3-0)(a)). When a material changes from a solid to a liquid or vice versa, latent heat storage is the release or preservation of heat during that process [[35\]](#page-16-0). Construction applications frequently employ solid-liquid phase transition because it provides almost isothermal heat storage and recovery and a high energy storage density. As a substantial amount of sensible and latent heat is melted, the PCM retains it [\(Fig. 2\(](#page-3-0)b)). The thermochemical storage of heat is an additional technique for storing thermal energy. This mechanism stores and releases thermal energy through a completely reversible chemical reaction resulting from the breaking and re-forming of molecular bonds. An endothermic reaction occurs when the thermochemical substance (AB) is heated to the point where it separates into components A and B. A and B, the two separate components, retain heat during this operation. The thermal energy is released when A and B recombine due to an exothermic process ([Fig. 2](#page-3-0) (c)). Scientists and engineers are turning to thermochemical heat storage more frequently to find new uses for thermal energy that can be stored, such as waste heat recovery, the integration of renewable energy sources, and industrial processes [\[36](#page-16-0),[37\]](#page-16-0).

#### *2.3. Latent heat storage and PCMs*

Latent heat storage differs from the other thermal energy storage techniques previously addressed in that it can store heat at a temperature that is almost constant and consistent with the phase change material's phase transition temperature. Moreover, it offers a high density of energy storage. PCMs are substances that, depending on their physical condition, have the capacity to either emit or absorb large amounts of latent heat [\[38](#page-16-0)]. Because PCMs have a high heat capacity within a narrow temperature range, they may be used in nearly isothermal heat storage. These substances have the ability to change into liquid-gas, solid-liquid, and solid-solid [[39](#page-16-0),[40\]](#page-16-0). PCMs are primarily classified as inorganic, eutectic, and organic compounds [[41\]](#page-16-0). It is categorized further based on the chemical constituents of the PCM. A thorough schematic illustration of these grouped PCMs is shown in [Fig. 3.](#page-3-0) Organic PCMs come into two categories: paraffinic and non-paraffinic materials, the latter of which includes subcategories such alcohols, fatty acids, esters, and glycols. Organic PCMs are mostly composed of carbon‑hydrogen links. Applications for heat storage, particularly at lower to medium temperatures, frequently make use of these organic PCMs [\[42](#page-16-0)]. High storage density, a suitable phase transition temperature range of 0–150 ◦C, and a stable chemical and physical composition are some advantages [[43\]](#page-16-0).

In contrast, materials such as metals and salts make up inorganic PCMs, which lack carbon or hydrogen atoms. Their usefulness for solar applications stems from their ability to melt at temperatures between 100 ◦C and 1000 ◦C, which are about ten times greater than organic PCMs. When two or more PCM types are combined, a crystalline mixture is formed during the crystallization phases. This is made possible by eutectic PCMs, which enable each component's congruent melting and solidification. Compounds that are organic-inorganic, organic-inorganic, or inorganic-inorganic can form eutectic combinations. Different materials can be mixed to adjust latent heat, melting points, and thermal conductivity to meet specific requirements.

Although little is known about the characteristics of eutectic PCM, some notable benefits are as follows: an acceptable volumetric heat storage density and well-defined melting temperatures. Similarly, biobased PCMs can be embedded in wall panels or ceiling tiles where they absorb heat during the day from sunlight and indoor activities to prevent overheating and then release stored heat at night to maintain a comfortable indoor climate without the need for additional heating, which is especially beneficial in buildings with large glass facades [[69,70](#page-17-0)]. Furthermore, heating terminals can be designed to include tanks filled with bio-based PCMs, storing thermal energy generated from renewable sources like solar thermal collectors or biomass boilers and distributing it through heating terminals when needed, effectively decoupling energy generation from consumption and optimizing energy use. By storing excess thermal energy produced during off-peak hours and releasing it during peak demand periods, bio-based PCMs can increase energy efficiency and reduce operating costs in hybrid heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems. Furthermore, biobased PCMs can be added to free cooling systems to release heat at night and absorb it during the day, therefore minimizing the need for mechanical cooling in areas with considerable daytime and nighttime temperature swings.

Lauryl Alcohol/Expanded Graphite Composites and their integration into building materials significantly enhance thermal regulation within buildings. Using lauryl alcohol as a PCM and expanded graphite to improve thermal conductivity, these composites can be effectively integrated into ceramic tiles or wall panels. This integration facilitates efficient thermal storage and release, enhancing indoor comfort and minimizing energy consumption [\[71,72](#page-17-0)]. Additionally, PCM-Enhanced Radiant Heating Systems leverage bio-based PCMs to store thermal energy within floor structures. During the heating season, these systems absorb heat from the hydronic system and release it gradually, ensuring the floor temperature remains consistent and comfortable, significantly reducing the energy demand for heating. Buildings become more energy efficient when bio-based PCMs, such as insulation materials, are included in the building envelope. These PCMs retain a suitable interior temperature while using less HVAC equipment by absorbing heat throughout the day and releasing it at night. Along with improving comfort, this method helps buildings be designed with more energy efficiency [[73\]](#page-17-0).

#### *3.2. Various PCMs used in a solar heating system*

More and more materials are included in solar heating systems to improve thermal energy storage and efficiency. When combined with conventional solar thermal systems, PCMs significantly boost performance. Hybrid systems work alongside traditional water storage tanks to extend heat availability, storing heat during peak sunlight hours for later use, such as preheating water in a conventional tank, thereby improving efficiency. As we look at the parts of the sun-assisted heating system, thermal energy storage technology is also essential to solar heating [\[77](#page-17-0)]. #### **4. PCM incorporation and thermal efficiency of system**

PCM must be appropriately integrated into the building material to improve the building envelope's ability to store heat. The integration process must guarantee minor or nonexistent leakage during phase transition and optimize the PCM's latent heat storage capability. Researchers have devised various techniques for integrating PCM into the building envelope, as shown in Fig. 5. Direct or indirect methods can be used to incorporate the PCM into the building envelope. The most straightforward and least expensive approach is direct inclusion, in which PCM is combined directly during manufacture with building supplies, including wallboards, cement, and concrete [[82\]](#page-17-0). Immersion and wet mixing were the two techniques used to add PCM directly. The PCM is immediately combined with building materials, including cement, mortar, and concrete, at the manufacturing site when using the wet mixing method. The thermal energy storage capacity of the building material will be improved by directly mixing PCM. Both the fact that the PCM does not impede the hydration process and that it does not react with any of the mix's ingredients are necessary conditions for the effective use of this approach. The immersion method involves dipping porous building materials (such as concrete blocks, gypsum wallboard, porous aggregate, etc.) in a container of liquid PCM. By capillary action, the building component absorbs the PCM. Temperature, PCM type, and concrete's absorption ability all affect how well and long it takes for the porous building element to soak in [[83\]](#page-17-0). Several significant drawbacks were present in the construction material's two direct PCM inclusion procedures [\[84](#page-17-0)]. These include but are not limited to (a) PCM leakage, which may impede the hydration process; (b) PCM loss, which may result from leakage during several heating-cooling thermal cycles. (c) The immersion procedure requires a longer time—nearly 4–8 h. (d) Wet mixing might have an impact on the building element's mechanical

strength. Indirect inclusion is another way to include PCM into the building envelope. This method was first used to encapsulate the PCM before being included in the construction component. These benefits have led to the widespread usage of PCMs, enclosed in an appropriate shell material, to incorporate PCM in building components [[85\]](#page-17-0). PCM is included by microencapsulation into a shell material with one mm to one-micrometer diameter. More so than 1 mm, PCM is incorporated into the shell material by microencapsulation [[86\]](#page-17-0). Enhancing the PCM's thermal performance is primarily dependent on the shell material. The shell's composition must harmonize with the surrounding environment and the PCM. Various appropriate shell materials, including natural and synthetic polymers, have been proposed by Giro-Paloma et al. (2016) [[87\]](#page-17-0) for the fabrication of microencapsulation. Some authors have therefore investigated non-polymers, such as a shell material to encapsulate PCM, which is environmentally friendly and has high thermal conductivity, such as silica, calcium carbonate, and melamineformaldehyde resin. Polymers are typically flammable, toxic, and have poor thermal conductivity. Due to its favorable heat transfer properties, aluminum is the most widely utilized shell material for microencapsulation [[88\]](#page-17-0). Furthermore, PCM is encapsulated using copper and stainless steel [[89\]](#page-17-0). Aluminum foil sheets, polymer bags, pouches, and HDPE panels are a few more shell materials that may be utilized to produce macro encapsulation [\[90](#page-17-0)]. PCM that has been face- or shape-stabilized is not encased in a shell material. They receive assistance from a highly porous and thermally conductive substance to lessen leakage and improve the PCM's thermal conductivity. To increase the application of PCM in the building envelope, porous materials such as clay, graphite scaffolds, polyurethane foam, and porous carbon are frequently utilized [[91\]](#page-17-0).

Martinopoulos et al.'s [[92\]](#page-17-0) analysis indicates that solar thermal systems account for at least 45 % of the overall heating demand. Its energy efficiency may be increased by improving the thermal performance, which has much promise. The two main methods that the PCM and solar thermal system may combine are thermal enhancement of the PCM. Phase segregation, heat transfer ratio, latent heat, leakage, phase change temperature, supercooling, and other parameters are PCM's main categories of dominating impact factors. There are three main types of solar thermal systems: solar collectors, thermal storage units, and solar heating terminals. The related studies will be covered in this part, along with a quick evaluation.

#### *4.1. Thermal enhancement approaches for PCM*

Enhancing the efficacy of solar heating requires the phase change material and associated components to be capable of self-behaving. Choosing PCM requires thoughtful evaluation of many variables, such as the proper phase change temperature, a large energy storage capacity, an effective heat exchanger, stable physiochemical assets, non-leakage, harmless, not corrosive inert, non-explosive, sustainable, limited phase segregation, permitted overcooling, etc. A PCM's thermal enhancement may be categorized in several ways, such as lowering super cooling/ phase separation, increasing thermal conductivity, changing the phase change temperature, minimizing latent heat expansion, and many more. Initially, an illustration of PCM's improved thermal properties is shown (Table 2). In contrast to metallic PCMs, almost all pure PCMs exhibit poor thermal conductivity [[93\]](#page-17-0). Because of this, the enhancement of thermal conductivity is significant for PCMs. Materials and a matrix with high thermal conductivity are two ways to increase the PCMs' thermal conductivity. Additionally, among the materials are carbon-based or metallic, such as expanded graphite, copper, silver, aluminum, and their derivatives, as well as metal oxide and nitride [[94\]](#page-17-0). The mentioned thermal enhancers can improve PCM's thermal conducting performance. Furthermore, many other methods, including nanoscale encapsulation, are being explored to improve PCM thermal conductivity [\[95](#page-17-0)]. Using this technique, the material particles' contact area is greatly expanded, bridging them considerably closer together and producing a stronger heat channel, quicker heat transfer rate, and superior thermal regulating properties. Two options for enhancing thermal conducting performance in a fixed temperature differential include modifying the material's thermal conductivity and the thermal media material's contact area. Regarding the PCM's thermal conductivity, thermal augmentation techniques primarily take into account, though are not restricted to, the following factors: Expanding the region of interaction between PCM particles using techniques like nanofluid encapsulation. 2. Constructing thermal bridges between individual PCM cells; this can be done, for instance, by combining PCM with materials with high heat conductivity, such as highly conductive foam or particles. Appropriate phase change points of the PCMs are also critical to their deployment and ability to transfer heat. Different application circumstances necessitate different temperatures for the beginning phase transition. The two primary phase change point adjustment techniques are binary/ternary eutectic synthesis and selecting appropriate phase change material with a qualifying temperature. The eutectic has been shown to have the lowest phase change point of any component material, with no supercooling or phase segregation and sharp melting/freezing [[96\]](#page-17-0). Its objective laws have led to the development of an equation to compute the eutectic phase change temperature. Te = 1 1 Ti Rlnxi Hi (16), where component i, xi, R, and the gas constant (8.315 J/(Kmol)) are the component i mole fractions. Te and Ti are the eutectic phase change points. Eq. (16) may be applied to ascertain a eutectic chemical's optimal composition mass ratio despite its binary, ternary, or even higher configuration. Both organic and inorganic eutectic PCMs are included, along with details on their thermal properties and defect suppression methods. Among the primary issues with PCMs is supercooling, separation of phases, leaks, rust, etc. The suppressive techniques will also be covered in this part, as follows. For really cooling: Overcooling is a recognized PCM downside that may be explained as phase change set off in an exothermic operation. Due to this weakness, the material's ability to completely exploit latent heat is hindered, which results in undesired thermal energy storage and efficiency. Consequently, control of undercooling is significant for PCM applications. Many materials and techniques exist for supercooling inhibition. The subcooling defect can be reduced or eliminated using a porous matrix, salt hydrates, and other modifying materials. It may be inferred from recent research that additional treatments, such as nucleating chemicals and even appropriate system design in some situations, are acceptable in the controlled partial melting process. Regarding phase separation: Phase separation, often called phase segregation or incongruent melting, restricts the PCM's deployment ability. The phenomena mostly manifest as sediments resulting from varying compositions within the eutectic, primarily occurring during phase transition events. The PCM's lifetime is significantly reduced when an incompatible phase transition prevents it from completing another heat cycle. Regarding leakage: Because form-stable PCM is malleable and can be molded into various forms, it can be used for numerous solar heating system components. Leakage, one of its deadly disadvantages, must be removed, however. Consequently, various porous matrix, encapsulation, and material modification approaches have been developed to address this problem. Similarly, other nonmetallic materials, such as plastics and Perspex [[97\]](#page-17-0), have been previously explored. However, the main drawback of plastic materials for PCM-related applications is their poor heat transmission capability. The selection of materials for PCM corrosion is contingent upon the specific conditions, meaning that the choice of materials is subject to change based on various factors (Fig. 6).

# **5. Bio-based phase change materials**

Bio-based phase change materials (BPCMs) are a broad class of materials created and widely employed in several TES systems industries to address these problems. #### *5.1. Organic BPCMs*

Sustainable biosources, such as animal fat and tropical oils like coconut, palm, and palm kernel oil, are the primary sources of biobased, non-paraffin organic PCMs. Their wide range of melting points makes them perfect for many applications. The following studies deal with organic and BPCMs that can potentially store thermal energy. Three distinct organic solvents were employed by Ong et al. [[103](#page-18-0)] in 2023 to extract coffee oil from SCGs. These oils' chemistry, composition, and thermophysical characteristics were examined to assess their potential as BPCMs. According to DSC data, coffee oil extracts have a latent heat of 51.15 1.46 J/g and a phase transition temperature of around 4.5 0.72 ◦C. Research using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) indicates that 60–80 % of coffee oil comprises fatty acids. FTIR and DSC analyses revealed that coffee oil extracts exhibited remarkable chemical and thermal stability during thermal cycling. Up to 46 min are spent keeping body temperatures below average, according to practical research assessing coffee oil extract as a possible cold therapy strategy. In their 2023 work, Ismail et al. [[104](#page-18-0)] concentrated on a low-cost BPCM derived from refined vegetable oil consumables. To construct a PCM microcapsule, molten PCM was poured into holes created by chemical etching of the cenospheres. To prevent PCM from leaving the microcapsule, a silica coating was then applied to the holes. On the basis of its microstructural, thermal, chemical compatibility, and leakage characteristics, we assessed the silica-coated PCM microcapsule (SCPCM). This novel microcapsule had improved conductivity, was more thermostable, and was leak-proof. At a slower rate of around 16 ◦C, the PCM underwent thermal degradation due to the silica layer in SCPCM. Compressive strength efficiency increased when 10 % of the sand was replaced with SCPCM. By replacing sand with 20–30 vol% cenosphere microcapsules, an acceptable compressive strength seems to be attained. As the replacement level increased to more than 30 vol%, the ductility of the SCPCM mortar decreased due to the hydrophilic nature of the silica deposit.

Molecular dynamics simulated annealing and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were utilized by Phiriyawirut et al. [[105](#page-18-0)] in 2023 to investigate the thermal efficiency of PCMs produced by hydroprocessing crude palm oil and using monoacylglycerols (MAGs) as an emulsifier. The start melting temperature is unaffected by emulsification, however the peak and end-set melting temperatures are raised by the addition of MAG. It's possible that the MAGs' emulsification of PCMs caused the absorption peak to expand and shift positively, increasing the operating temperature of PCMs made from palm oil. The emulsified PCMs crystallized faster, and the better MAGs interactions than alkanes (ALKs)- MAGs interactions may have contributed to the following drop in melting temperature during the second heating. This affected further emulsification processes to control the PCM properties and operating temperature. The feasibility of using biochar-based catalysts for microwave-induced catalytic biomass pyrolysis to produce gaseous fuel and enhance bio-oil in 2023 was examined by Guo et al. [\[106\]](#page-18-0), who found that the structural characteristics and Fe-series alteration were important. The results demonstrated that the microstructure of pure biochar had an impact on its catalytic activity. During biomass pyrolysis, KOH-activated biochar with many microporous structures may greatly boost gas yields when heated with microwaves. When Fe crystal nanoparticles were added to biochar, the production of flammable gasses (CO, H2, and CH4) increased by 50 %, and the bio-oil became significantly simpler than when biochar was used alone. This resulted from the biochar's enhanced catalytic activity.

#### *5.2. Inorganic BPCMs*

BPCMs are made from natural resources and, like traditional paraffin-based PCMs, can absorb, store, and release large quantities of latent heat. A quantitative study of the circular latent heat thermal energy storage (LHTES) partial charging and discharging procedures was conducted in 2022 by Zadeh et al. [[107](#page-18-0)]. To increase the storage's sensitivity to temperature, three aluminum foam sheets were added, separated by 120 ◦F. The study found that the angle (θ) and thickness (wmf) of the right-most metal foam sheet have a substantial impact on the charging and discharging processes in 16 different storage configurations. The configuration with the greatest full charging energy (case no. 7, θ = π/6 and wmf = 7 mm) was found to not fundamentally exhibit ideal efficiency in the partial charging mode when charging and discharging according to the recommended capacity- and time-based charging/discharging energys. Rather, case number seven has up to 6 % less charging energy than the other cases when it is half charged. A Yshaped design was found to work best when charged vertically and then rotated 60 degrees to reach maximum discharge (to a position resembling a ⅄). Fire-resistant phase change materials (PCMs) were developed in 2022 by Yin et al. [\[108\]](#page-18-0) by combining ammonium polyphosphate (APP) with polyethylene glycol (PEG) loaded with poly (glycerolitaconic acid) in an approachable and ecologically acceptable manner. Thorough research was done on heat response behavior, flame retardancy, and phase change performance. The results demonstrated a significant rise in the limiting oxygen index (LOI) with the concentration of APP. The vertical testing yielded a UL-94 V0 grade, the LOI value increased from 21.6 % to 28.7 %, and the pHRR decreased by 36.15 % when the amount of APP filled reached 15 wt%. Its solvent-free method, profiles of all biobased supporting matrices, mild curing temperature, fire safety, and high preparation effectiveness for PCM production are expected to make it a useful temperature regulator in buildings.

In 2022, Gbekou et al. [\[109\]](#page-18-0) conducted research on microencapsulated phase change materials (mPCMs), which are small coreshell particles dispersed in a biobased aqueous solution. Their mechanical and thermophysical properties in cement mortars were investigated. The mortar samples were produced using a predetermined process that ensures a constant particle size and removes the potential for microcapsule leakage. Adding mPCMs significantly causes the mortar to lose its density, porosity, and mechanical strength. On the other hand, hot-disk characterization demonstrated a notable improvement in thermal performance. The system with 8 wt% mPCMs has well-balanced mechanical and thermal properties.

#### *5.3. Composite BPCMs*

Composite BPCMs reduce the negative impacts of distinct PCM classes by combining inorganic and organic components. These composites may combine the benefits of enhanced thermal conductivity from inorganic BPCMs and high latent heat from organic BPCMs. Alipour et al. [\[110\]](#page-18-0) evaluated the thermal properties of methyl palmitate (MP) and decanoic acid (DA) binary combinations in 2023 and presented a new and renewable BPCM. Both PCMs come from biomass, the majority of which being vegetable oil. The results showed that the binary eutectic combination of 70 % DA and 30 % MP is a PCM with the necessary properties to be used as TES in construction materials. These characteristics include high values of the respective latent heats (Hf = 202.3 J/g, Hm = 201.43 J/g) and realistic melting and freezing temperatures (Tm = 20.19 ◦C, Tf = 15.08 ◦C). Analysis of its other thermophysical properties provided additional evidence that this combination met the PCM function. As the model compound, Fan et al. [[111](#page-18-0)] first encased beeswax using an epoxy resin containing crystalline alkyl side chains (D18) and a number of amine curing agents. They then investigated the connection between the cross-linking network architecture and the phase change. It was demonstrated that the polarity effect and the steric hindrance impact had an affect on the phase change performances of the polymeric FSPCMs. With M-xylylenediamine as the curing agent, the EPM-60 sample exhibited the greatest enthalpy value (146.2 J/g). Sheng et al.'s 2023 study [[112\]](#page-18-0) focused on cutting-edge shape-stable phase change composites (SSPCMs) for thermoelectric sensing. The matrix material was polylactic acid (PLA), and the latent heat storage media was selected to be a mixture of wood flour (WF) and PEG. The PEG/WF composites (WP) investigation showed that capillary force allowed WF to easily encapsulate PEG. When the PEG/WF/PLA ternary composites (WPCs) were combined with PLA, their high latent heat (87.2 J/g) was maintained.

#### **6. An analysis of the use of various BPCM types in TES systems and their relative improvements**

Bio-based phase change materials are gaining attention due to their potential to increase energy efficiency while having a lower environmental effect than traditional PCMs made from fossil fuels. All BPCMs are made from sustainable resources, such as biopolymers and agricultural waste. This reduces dependency on non-renewable resources and encourages sustainable energy solutions. Undoubtedly, one of the key advantages of utilizing BPCMs is this. The carbon footprint of PCMs generated from bio-based materials is less than that of their nonrenewable counterparts. They assist in reducing greenhouse gas emissions and decreasing environmental deterioration. After their useful life is up, PCMs with organic bio-based foundations often biodegrade, which is good for the environment. On the other hand, some common PCMs could be challenging to get rid of. In a range of TES systems, BPCMs can improve energy efficiency by lowering temperature variations and peak energy consumption. Finally, but just as importantly, BPCMs often do not include the hazardous or poisonous ingredients that can be present in some standard PCMs. This can improve workplace security and make it simpler to follow rules. This explains the various uses of BPCMs in various industries, including waste heat recovery, construction materials, textiles, refrigeration, and heating and cooling of buildings. Despite the previously mentioned advantages, BPCMs are limited by design, as demonstrated by the thorough analysis of the included research and the review. First, because of their constrained phase transition temperature ranges, some BPCMs may only apply to a narrow temperature range in thermal energy storage devices. Furthermore, it is possible that organic BPCMs have lower thermal conductivity than their inorganic counterparts. Because the PCM can no longer transmit heat, this may lead to lower charging and discharging rates. Furthermore, the extremely high thermal conduction of inorganic PCMs may be altered by adding biomaterials. The same characteristic may cause volume variations in some inorganic BPCMs during phase transitions, resulting in material instability and structural issues with building materials or encapsulation matrices. Third, the long-term stability of BPCMs is dependent on how well they work as containment systems, heat transfer fluids, and product encapsulations. Encapsulating BPCMs properly is essential to their long-term effectiveness and prevention of leakage. Developing encapsulation techniques that are both inexpensive and durable without compromising the PCM's thermal properties might be challenging. Stated differently, inadequate compatibility might lead to decreased performance, deterioration, or leakage. Finally, from an economic perspective, producing BPCMs—especially those derived from specialized feedstock—may be more costly than producing them using standard substitutes. Maintaining cost-effectiveness while achieving scalability can also be challenging [\[113\]](#page-18-0). The most often employed organic BPCMs in TES systems nowadays are saturated fatty acids derived from vegetable and animal fats due to their biodegradability, sustainability, and environmental friendliness. They have a reasonable latent heat, a minor volume change during phase transition, an appropriate melting-freezing transition temperature range, and the potential to crystallize with less supercooling [\[113\]](#page-18-0). They still don't transfer heat as well as conventional paraffin PCMs [[114](#page-18-0)]. This would account for the scientist's intention to support the melting point of saturated fatty acids by making eutectic mixtures, or composites, of BPCMs. Regarding the thermal stability trend, the material employed affects the thermal stability of single BPCMs. For instance, organic BPCMs' thermal stability from carbohydrates, fatty acids, or plant oils varies. Certain organic materials' phase shift properties may be impacted by chemical processes that occur over several heat cycles or by degradation at high temperatures. When composite BPCMs are made of organic and inorganic components, their thermal stability depends on how well the BPCMs and the supporting elements in the composite work together. Inorganic material composites, for example, can contribute to the PCM's overall stability and durability by averting any possible degrading issues. In other words, standalone BPCMs are variable in their degree of reusability. While many BPCMs could undergo gradual degradation after repeated heating and cooling cycles, others might have a phase change temperature or latent heat capacity that varies over time. Their reusability and durability can be improved by using the proper encapsulation and confinement techniques. It's noteworthy that composite BPCMs can demonstrate superior reusability than standalone BPCMs since the supporting materials give stability. The inorganic components can strengthen their structural integrity, reducing the PCM's susceptibility to phase shifts, temperature fluctuations, and environmental degradation. In conclusion, compared to conventional organic and inorganic BPCMs, composite BPCMs can provide enhanced heat conductivity, a significant advantage. Combining inorganic and organic PCM might create strong composites with improved thermal conductivity and latent heat storage capacity. The performance of TES systems is increased overall when connected organic and inorganic components are present because they improve heat transfer during phase transitions. Composite BPCMs offer the flexibility to be aligned within the necessary temperature ranges for various applications, in addition to their ability to be produced steadily.
