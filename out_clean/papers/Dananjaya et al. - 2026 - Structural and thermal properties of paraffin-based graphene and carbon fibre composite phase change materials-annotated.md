# Structural and thermal properties of paraffin-based graphene and carbon fibre composite phase change materials


# ARTICLE INFO

*Keywords:* Phase change materials Graphene Carbon fibre Thermal properties Battery thermal management systems Sustainable energy solutions

#### ABSTRACT

Given the energy crisis, decreasing resources of non-renewable energy, and environmental degradation by hydrocarbon-based energy, novel approaches must be designed. Phase Change Materials (PCM), which absorb and release heat during phase transition, are a suitable choice for battery thermal management systems. However, most pure PCMs possess poor thermal conductivity, limiting practical applications. Enhancing their thermal properties is crucial for effective utilization of them across a wide range of applications. This study focuses on improving PCM's performance by incorporating carbon-based fillers, such as milled carbon fibres and graphene, into paraffin wax. Composite phase change materials were prepared with carbon filler loadings of 1–5 wt.% and 8 wt.%, 11 wt.%, and 14 wt.%, using methods such as sonication and magnetic stirring. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were used to assess their thermal properties. Results showed significant improvements in thermal conductivity and stability with carbon fillers, though bulk heat capacity decreased slightly. Among the tested compositions, the milled carbon fibre-paraffin composite of 5 wt.% exhibited the highest thermal conductivity of 1.448 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. The degradation temperature of paraffin increased by 10–30 ◦C with carbon-based fillers, depending on composition. DSC analysis showed phase change peaks between 30–40 ◦C (solidification) and 50–60 ◦C (melting), confirming the preservation of paraffin's thermal properties. Overall, the findings of this study highlight the potential of composite PCMs in enhancing the performance of battery thermal management systems (BTMS), offering a promising avenue for future energy solutions through improved materials that can be promising for thermal management applications.

# **1. Introduction**

The energy crisis, which is worsening due to the depletion of nonrenewable resources and increasing greenhouse gas emissions, urgently requires novel and sustainable energy solutions [\[1](#page-16-0)–7]. In this context, polymer-based materials and nano materials have gained increasing attention due to their lightweight nature [\[8,9](#page-16-0)], design versatility [10–[12](#page-16-0)], and compatibility with energy storage systems [\[13](#page-16-0), [14\]](#page-16-0). Among many strategies under investigation, Phase Change Materials (PCMs) emerge as promising candidates for efficient thermal energy storage and management [[15\]](#page-16-0). PCMs function by absorbing and releasing latent heat during phase transitions, hence maintaining precise thermal regulation in a wide array of applications that include energy systems, building materials, and BTMS [\[16](#page-16-0)]. Their wider applications

are naturally restricted by their inherent shortcomings, such as low thermal conductivity and reduced efficiency in heat transfer [\[17](#page-16-0)].

Organic, inorganic, and eutectic are three types of PCMs, among which paraffin-based PCMs are widely used owing to their high latent heat capacity of approximately 200–250 J/g, chemical stability, and low cost [\[18](#page-16-0)]. Despite these advantages, low thermal conductivity in the range of 0.2 to 0.3 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹ is one of the main factors that usually restricts the use of paraffin very common organic PCM-in highly efficient thermal management applications [\[19](#page-16-0)]. Concerning these limitations, researchers have focused on improving the thermal properties of PCMs by incorporating high-performance additives to enhance the heat transfer characteristics of PCMs [[20\]](#page-16-0).

Carbon-based materials, such as graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and carbon fibres, are emerging as promising thermal

CNTs offer thermal conductivity of around 3000 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹ (for single-walled CNTs), while carbon fibres exhibit thermal conductivities ranging from 5 to 1000 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹, depending on the type [\[24](#page-16-0)]. Additionally, these carbon-based materials offer significant thermal stability, with graphene and CNTs maintaining stability at temperatures exceeding 600 ◦C in an inert atmosphere, and carbon fibres typically stable up to 400–700 ◦C, depending on their grade [\[25](#page-16-0)]. Their lightweight properties, with densities of approximately 2.2 g/cm³ for graphene and CNTs and 1.75–2.0 g/cm³ for carbon fibres, along with mechanical reinforcement capabilities providing tensile strengths of up to 2 GPa for CNTs and 4–7 GPa for carbon fibres, make them highly effective as thermal conductivity enhancers and structural reinforcements in PCMs [[26,27](#page-16-0)]. These materials create conductive networks within the PCM matrix, which greatly enhance the pathways for heat transfer. Furthermore, their compatibility with organic PCMs makes them ideal for composite material design.

Advanced material processing techniques, such as sonication, magnetic stirring, and surface modification, are used to incorporate carbonbased fillers into paraffin wax and other PCMs, ensuring uniform dispersion and strong interfacial interactions [[37\]](#page-16-0). The resultant composite PCMs possess enhanced thermal conductivity and stability, hence making them ideal for applications requiring efficient thermal management [[38,39](#page-16-0)]. Such improvements will go a long way in significantly expanding the utility of PCMs in systems where effective heat dissipation is an important factor in maintaining operational efficiency and safety, such as BTMS [\[40](#page-16-0)].

This paper intends to develop carbon-based filler-enhanced paraffin composite PCMs and investigate the effect of filler loading on thermal conductivity, stability, and phase transition behaviour through systematic variation of filler concentration and advanced characterization techniques such as DSC and TGA. The approach will provide insight into the mechanisms that underlie thermal enhancement and will guide the optimization of the formulations of PCMs. As mentioned earlier, enhancing the thermal performance of PCMs is crucial to make them a more viable choice for use in battery thermal management systems. The core objective of this research was to develop PCMs with both high thermal conductivity and high latent heat capacity to efficiently absorb the heat generated by a battery. The decision to employ a paraffin-based phase change material was driven by its ease of compounding and its significant latent heat storage capacity within a relatively narrow melting temperature range. To enhance its thermal properties, varying proportions of carbon-based fibres and graphene were introduced into the mixture.

The results obtained in this study are expected to contribute to the development of composite PCMs with superior thermal management properties. Such materials have very good prospects for solving various problems in energy storage, renewable energy systems, and thermal regulation technologies, and correspond to current global requirements for sustainable and efficient energy technologies.

# *2.2. Sample preparation*

Different percentages of carbon fibre and graphene were mixed in a series of samples to study the influence of filler type and loading on thermal and structural properties of paraffin wax-based phase change materials (PCMs). As presented in [Table 3](#page-3-0), formulations were designed to increase the content of the fillers from 2 wt % to 14 wt % by decreasing the paraffin wax proportionally. These specific loading concentrations were used to strike the point where increases in thermal conductivity and structural strength are expected, in line with literaturereported ranges for filler-reinforced PCMs [\[41](#page-16-0)–43]. Carbon fibre and graphene were selected due to their high thermal conductivity and to enhance PCM performance, to allow for direct comparison of the reinforcing effect of both over a consistent range of concentrations [\[44](#page-17-0)–46].

## *2.2.1. Preparation of carbon fibre/paraffin wax composite phase change material*

Solid paraffin wax was preheated and melted before use. A predetermined proportion of carbon fibre (see [Table 4](#page-3-0)) was then added to the molten paraffin wax. The mixture was magnetically stirred at a constant temperature of approximately 80 ◦C for two hours at 600 rpm to ensure uniform dispersion. The mixture was magnetically stirred at a constant temperature above the melting point of the paraffin wax for 2 h to ensure uniform dispersion. Upon completion of stirring, the mixture was allowed to cool naturally, resulting in the final composite sample.

*2.2.2. Preparation of graphene/paraffin composite phase change materials* Graphene nanosheets were dispersed in anhydrous ethanol at a specified concentration and sonicated for 30 min to achieve complete dispersion. This was followed by ultrasonic treatment using a probe sonicator (frequency: 20 kHz, power: 300 W) for 30 min to further enhance the dispersion of carbon fibres and break up any agglomerates. Paraffin wax powder was gradually added to the suspension under continuous stirring at 600 rpm, ensuring uniform dispersion of the fillers. Stirring was continued until complete evaporation of ethanol was achieved, which typically took 2 h. The resulting mixture was allowed to cool, after which it was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 70 ◦C for t2 h to ensure the complete removal of residual ethanol. The dried material was then compacted into cakes, yielding the final composite samples.

## *2.3. Characterization techniques*

# *2.3.1. Scanning electron microscope (SEM)*

A Field Emission Gun Scanning Electron Microscope (FEG-SEM, Oxford Instruments) was used to observe the microstructure of the samples, with sample thicknesses ranging from 8 mm to 10 mm. The filament current, emission current, accelerating voltage, and pressure were set to default values to ensure sufficient electron penetration without damaging the sample surface. Field of view settings (10 µm, 20 µm, 50 µm, and 100 µm) were selected based on the sample and filler ratio to capture both the overall shape of the surface and the distribution of the fillers. To ensure the samples were electrically conductive and to prevent the creation of static electric fields during electron irradiation, non-conductive samples were coated with a thin gold/palladium layer (80:20 ratio, 6.0 nm thick) via sputtering, with a sputtering current of 40 mA and a maximum sputtering time of 5 min.

## *2.3.2. Volumetric specific heat and thermal measurements*

To ensure consistency and accuracy in thermal conductivity measurements and microscopic analyses, all composite samples were prepared in a fixed cylindrical shape with dimensions of 50 mm diameter and 10 mm thickness using a standardized mold. Volumetric specific heat is a critical parameter in thermal conductivity measurements and (*continued on next page*) related thermal calculations [[47\]](#page-17-0). It represents the energy required to raise the temperature of a specific volume of a material by 1 ◦C. As shown in Eq. (1), the volumetric specific heat (Cv ) can be calculated by multiplying the gravimetric specific heat (Cp) by the material's density (ρ):

Cv = Cp⋅*ρ* (1)

The measured values of volumetric specific heat are generally robust against uncertainties in the input parameters used for calculations. Consequently, an approximate value for specific heat is often sufficient to compute the thermal conductivity accurately. The primary source of variability in volumetric specific heat is the density of the insulation material. ## *2.3.3. SH-3 sensor for thermal property measurement*

The SH-3 sensor is a dual-needle sensor designed for measuring volumetric heat capacity, thermal diffusivity, thermal conductivity, and thermal resistivity. It has dimensions of 30 mm in length and 1.3 mm in diameter. This design makes it compatible with most solid and granular materials, though it is unsuitable for low-viscosity liquids due to the significant heat pulse it generates, which can induce free convection in liquid samples. The sensor's compact size also makes it particularly advantageous for applications in the food industry.

Measurement with the SH-3 sensor typically requires two minutes, comprising a 30-second heating phase followed by a 90-second temperature monitoring phase. Temperature readings are recorded at 1-second intervals (i.e. I Hz) throughout the measurement period.

# *2.3.4. Dual-needle algorithm*

The SH-3 sensor employs a dual-needle algorithm to derive thermal properties. During its operation:

- 1. Heat is applied to the heated needle for a predetermined duration (t).
- 2. Temperature measurements are recorded in the monitoring needle, positioned 6 mm from the heated needle, during both the heating and subsequent cooling phases.

Processing of the recorded temperature data involves several steps:

- Subtraction of ambient temperature.
- Correction for any drift rates observed during the measurement period.

The corrected data are then fitted to mathematical models (Eqs. (2) and (3)) using a least-squares procedure. This algorithm provides precise analysis and interpretation of temperature data, enabling the accurate determination of thermal properties for the tested materials.

Δ*T* = ( *<sup>q</sup>* 4*πk* ) *Ei* (<sup>−</sup> *<sup>r</sup>*<sup>2</sup> 4*Dt*) *t* ≤ *th* (2)

Δ*T* = ( *q πk* ){ *Ei* [ <sup>−</sup> *<sup>r</sup>*<sup>2</sup> 4*D*(*t* − *th*) ] − *Ei* [ − *r*<sup>2</sup> <sup>4</sup>*Dt*]}*<sup>t</sup> <sup>&</sup>gt; th* (3)

The parameters mentioned above are defined as follows: Δ*T* represents the temperature rise at the measuring needle ( ◦C); *q* denotes the heat input at the heated needle (W m⁻¹); *K* is the thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹); *r* is the distance between the heated needle and the measuring needle (m); *D* refers to the thermal diffusivity (m² s⁻¹); *t*  represents the time measured (s); *t*h is the heating time (s); and *E*ᵢ denotes the exponential integral, which is approximated using polynomial expressions as described by Abramowitz and Stegun (1972). To ensure measurement accuracy, each sample was evaluated six times using a TEMPOS thermometer (United States).

#### *2.3.5. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)*

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Q500 Thermogravimetric Analyzer (TA Instruments, USA) to evaluate sample thermal and oxidative stability by measuring mass loss with temperature. The analysis covered 30 ◦C to 500 ◦C at a heating rate of 10 ◦C/min to ensure accuracy. Approximately 10 mg of each sample was placed on a platinum holder, with careful alignment for stability. The decomposition behaviour was monitored under nitrogen atmosphere, and data were analyzed using TA Universal Analysis software to produce thermogravimetric curves.

# *2.3.6. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)*

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was conducted using a Q100 Differential Scanning Calorimeter (TA Instruments, USA) to evaluate the thermal properties of the samples, including Tm, Tg, Tc, and latent heat. Samples weighing 2–10 mg were encapsulated in aluminium pots using a crucible sealer to prevent leakage. The analysis was performed over a temperature range of − 30 ◦C to 120 ◦C, with a heating rate of 10 ◦C/min under nitrogen atmosphere for accurate and high-resolution data. Sample preparation involved precise weighing with a 0.1 mg resolution analytical balance (Ohaus Adventurer, Sweden), and care was taken to avoid contamination. Thermal data were processed using TA Universal Analysis 2000 software.

## **3. Results and discussion**

## *3.1. Appearance of samples*

The appearance of the graphene/paraffin composites and carbon fibre/paraffin composites is illustrated in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b). From the analysis of the external appearance of carbon fibre/paraffin and graphene/paraffin composites, it can be observed that at low filler concentrations, the surface of the paraffin composite PCMs remains relatively smooth, indicating a homogeneous structure. As the filler content increases from 1 wt. % to 5 wt. %, the composite surfaces remain generally smooth, although a noticeable deepening of the material's colour occurs, highlighting the visible effect of the filler. However, when the filler content reaches 8 wt. % or higher, the surface roughness of the composites significantly increases, with slight agglomeration observed. This phenomenon aligns with the findings in some of the previous studies on PCMs, though the agglomeration decreases with a reduction in paraffin content. As filler loading increases, the composites darken, indicating higher filler dispersion, though higher loadings (8 %− 14 %) show agglomeration that may affect thermal conductivity. Graphene/ Paraffin composites display more uniform darkening due to better dispersion of graphene, while Carbon Fibre/Paraffin composites show visible fibre clusters, potentially disrupting heat transfer. Uneven surfaces at higher loadings in both composites suggest reduced structural integrity, possibly impacting thermal and latent heat performance. For example, Ortaç et al. [\[48](#page-17-0)] reported the poly(Styrene-block-Ethylene Glycol Dimethyl Methacrylate) (PS-PEG DM) copolymer using boron nitride (BN) and lead oxide (PbO) nanoparticles and experienced a similar trend in surface. Qu et al. [[49](#page-17-0)]. further reported the same behaviour while adding fumed silica to the paraffin matrix. Additionally, the introduction of fillers causes deformation in the polymer chains.

Carbon fibre-reinforced paraffin composites exhibited higher surface roughness, ranging from 5.1 nm –3.0 µm, compared to graphenereinforced composites, which showed lower surface roughness values of 2 nm–1.5 µm [\[50](#page-17-0)]. This difference is likely attributed to the larger size and irregular morphology of carbon fibres, which result in less uniform dispersion within the paraffin matrix. In terms of uniformity, carbon fibre composites displayed a uniformity index of 60–80 %, whereas graphene composites exhibited higher uniformity, ranging between 85 and 95 %, due to the smaller particle size and planar structure of graphene, which allows for better integration into the matrix [\[51](#page-17-0)]. The use of ultrasonication in the production of graphene-reinforced PCMs ensures more homogeneous mixing and reduces graphene filler aggregation compared to carbon fibres. When the filler content exceeds 5 wt. %, the composites display noticeable porous voids, attributed to poor interfacial adhesion between paraffin and carbon-based nanofillers. As presented by Ouikhalfan et al. [[52\]](#page-17-0) while introducing 2wt. % hexagonal boron nitride to the PCM, higher filler contents are attributed to the agglomeration of filler. Higher filler mass fractions also increase the likelihood of crack formation within the composites. The Fig. 2 illustrates the thermal energy transfer and phase change behaviour of graphene/paraffin and carbon fibre/paraffin composite. In the graphene/paraffin composite (top panel), the graphene particles are distributed homogeneously in the paraffin matrix, leveraging the high thermal conductivity of graphene to provide enhanced heat transfer throughout the phase change process. As the material undergoes a phase change from solid to liquid, maximum energy absorption and release are made possible, illustrated by the energy flow arrows, highlighting graphene's improved heat conductivity. For comparison, the carbon fibre/ paraffin composite (bottom panel) contains networked carbon fibres, which offer more conductive routes due to their rod-like structure. While the carbon fibres enhance heat transfer during phase change as well, agglomeration of the fibres is evident with less uniform heat transfer than that of the graphene composite. The comparison unveils the influence of the filler morphology and type on thermal performance and efficiency of energy exchange of composite PCMs and their potential in future-generation energy storage and thermal management systems.

Furthermore, the experimental use of magnetic stirring caused a small portion of paraffin and filler to remain incompletely mixed, leading to material accumulation on the beaker walls. This effect, more pronounced at higher filler concentrations, introduces observational challenges and suggests the need for improved mixing methods.

## *3.2. Microstructure analysis*

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images of paraffin/graphene composites at 20 µm and 100 µm scales, along with SEM images of paraffin/carbon fibre composites at 10 µm, 20 µm, 50 µm, and 100 µm scales, were analyzed to gain insights into the microstructure of paraffinbased composite PCMs.

As depicted by SEM images in [Fig. 3,](#page-6-0) the surface of the pure paraffin material appears relatively smooth and compact, consisting of interconnected paraffin plates with occasional structural gaps. However, due to the extremely low electrical conductivity, (10<sup>−</sup> 16 to 10<sup>−</sup> 14 S/m) of pure paraffin, even after the application of a conductive coating, the images lack clarity and detail, limiting the resolution and visibility of finer structural features.

In [Fig. 4,](#page-7-0) the red circles highlight graphene particles, while the blue ellipses indicate the presence of cracks. Within the paraffin-based PCM matrix, graphene appears as thin and short white strips dispersed across the surface. This observation, particularly for a Paraffin/Graphene composite containing 4 wt. % graphene at a scale of 100 µm, can be attributed to graphene being embedded within the paraffin matrix. The dispersion of graphene is critical, as it dictates the formation of thermal pathways that enhance the composite's performance. These strips also signify the incomplete exfoliation of graphene sheets during the composite synthesis, which could affect the material's uniformity and heat transfer capabilities. In PCM nanocomposites, the white coloration arises from electron charging on the graphene particles, a phenomenon that occurs due to the high conductivity and localized field effect of graphene interacting with the electron beam during SEM imaging [\[53](#page-17-0)].

Graphene-infused paraffin PCM nanocomposites exhibit a highly textured surface. As the graphene filler content increases, the surface texture of the composite becomes progressively more wrinkled, with more curled edges [\[54](#page-17-0)]. These features arise from the intrinsic flexibility and high surface energy of graphene sheets, which cause them to bend and wrinkle upon interaction with the paraffin matrix. The wrinkling increases the available interfacial area, which promotes phonon scattering and enhances matrix-filler bonding [[55\]](#page-17-0). This phenomenon occurs because the increased proportion of filler alters the structural configuration of the paraffin, enhancing its specific surface area. The wrinkled surface texture also facilitates better interaction between the paraffin matrix and graphene filler, promoting the formation of conductive networks essential for thermal energy transfer. Furthermore, the curling of graphene sheets can trap paraffin molecules, creating microenvironments that stabilize the composite structure.

At higher graphene loadings, the material experiences significant aggregation due to the high specific surface area of graphene and its strong π-π stacking interactions [[56\]](#page-17-0). This aggregation leads to the formation of stacked graphene layers within the PCM matrix, creating a reticulated inter-filler structure that gives the surface a rougher appearance. The increased filler loading results in a percolation threshold being reached, where continuous conductive networks form, significantly improving thermal conductivity. However, the stacked layers may also limit phonon mobility in certain directions, creating anisotropic thermal properties. These features contribute to the enhancement of thermal conductivity and other thermal properties by accelerating heat transfer, but excessive aggregation could hinder uniform heat distribution across the matrix.

The irregular shape and non-uniform thickness of the graphene particles are notable. While some particles promote the flow of heat carriers, others may impede it. These irregularities are due to variations in the exfoliation and dispersion processes during composite synthesis, which influence the material's thermal anisotropy [\[57](#page-17-0)]. Nevertheless, the graphene's distribution, even if uneven, improves the thermal pathway for phonons, thereby enhancing the thermal conductivity of the paraffin matrix. The enhancement is more prominent in regions where graphene aligns to form continuous networks, facilitating rapid heat transfer [[13\]](#page-16-0). Conversely, regions with isolated particles may act as barriers, reducing the efficiency of heat propagation within the composite.

However, cracks, as indicated by the blue ellipses, emerge regardless of the graphene loading. As the filler content increases, the cracks become more numerous, larger, and deeper. These cracks result from suboptimal compatibility between the paraffin matrix and the graphene filler, leading to voids within the composite. Cracks act as thermal resistance zones, where the continuity of the heat transfer network is disrupted. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced at high filler loadings, where stress concentration due to graphene aggregation exacerbates crack formation. These voids inhibit the efficient flow of heat carriers, diminishing the overall thermal performance of the material. Understanding and mitigating these compatibility issues are essential for optimizing the composite structure and functionality.

When the SEM image scale is reduced to 20 µm, finer details of the graphene strips and cracks become more visible. The addition of graphene filler introduces folds and wrinkles to the composite surface, which act as thermal channels for phonon diffusion over wider regions. The folds increase the path complexity, allowing phonons to diffuse through multiple scattering events, which enhances overall thermal conductivity. This structural modification not only improves heat transfer but also suggests potential self-reinforcing mechanisms within the composite due to graphene's mechanical resilience. The ability of the wrinkles to align with thermal gradients further enhances directional heat transport, demonstrating the intricate interplay between structure and function in the composite PCM.

As depicted in [Fig. 5,](#page-9-0) the dispersion of carbon fibres within the paraffin matrix is significantly more homogeneous than that of graphene nanoparticles. The carbon fibres are visibly more tightly bound to the paraffin matrix, with distinct interfaces observable in the SEM images. This improved binding may be attributed to the weaker van der Waals interactions between carbon fibres compared to graphene nanoparticles, which allow for better distribution and adhesion [[58\]](#page-17-0). This homogeneity is crucial, as it facilitates the formation of continuous pathways for phonon transport, thereby enhancing thermal conduction. Unlike graphene's tendency to aggregate at higher loadings due to strong π-π interactions, the dispersion of carbon fibres remains relatively stable across different loading levels, ensuring consistent thermal performance improvements [[59\]](#page-17-0).

When comparing paraffin/carbon fibre composite phase change materials at different loadings (e.g., 1 wt. % and 14 wt. %), the number of fibres within the composites increases markedly [(Fig. 5)](#page-9-0). At a loading of 11 wt. %, SEM images at a scale of 20 µm reveal a noticeable rise in the concentration of fibres, which can be attributed to the low density of carbon fibres. This low density allows for an increased volume fraction of the filler at higher loadings without significantly altering the composite's mass. This structural evolution with increasing fibre content contributes to enhanced thermal conductivity by creating a denser network for phonon transport. However, the distribution of fibres also leads to increased complexity in the composite microstructure, potentially introducing localized variations in thermal properties [\[60](#page-17-0)].

At a finer scale of 10 µm, SEM images of paraffin/carbon fibre composites with loadings of 1 wt. % and 8 wt. % highlight the clear morphology of the fibres. These fibres effectively form interconnected thermal pathways, significantly improving the thermal conduction rate of the composites and, consequently, their overall thermal properties. However, some short fibres appear to be embedded in the paraffin matrix and are not visible under SEM imaging. This embedding might occur during the fabrication process, where mechanical forces break and bury fibres into the matrix. Additionally, some fibres within the PCM matrix are visibly torn or broken. While these fragmented fibres may reduce the effectiveness of thermal pathways, they also create new interfaces that could scatter phonons, further complicating heat transfer dynamics.

Most carbon fibres are observed to be randomly oriented within the paraffin matrix, which introduces both advantages and challenges. Random orientation enhances isotropic thermal properties but also leads to significant phonon scattering, which may reduce the overall heat transfer efficiency. Despite this, the addition of carbon fibres dramatically improves the thermal performance of the paraffin matrix, as their high aspect ratio and intrinsic thermal conductivity establish efficient pathways for phonon and heat transport. The balance between orientation, dispersion, and interaction with the matrix is critical to optimizing the composite's thermal performance.

In certain SEM images, long black bands are visible, indicating areas where the coating failed to adhere uniformly. These regions exhibit weaker thermal conductivity due to insufficient matrix-filler bonding, which disrupts the formation of continuous thermal pathways. Such inconsistencies in adhesion also obscure the SEM observation, complicating the assessment of filler distribution and microstructural uniformity. Addressing these adhesion issues would be essential for achieving the desired thermal efficiency and consistency in PCM composites. To improve filler dispersion and minimize interfacial defects, future work could incorporate surface modification techniques such as silanization or acid treatment of carbon fibres and graphene, which enhance interfacial bonding with the paraffin matrix. Additionally, the use of surfactants or coupling agents (silanes or compatibilizers) can significantly reduce filler aggregation and improve matrix–filler compatibility.

#### *3.3. Thermal conductivity/volumetric heat capacity/diffusivity*

Typically, lattice vibrations or direct contact are used to transfer thermal energy. As a result, parameters including inherent thermal characteristics, filler dispersity, aspect ratio, and heat route orientation all contribute when it comes to thermal conductivity [[61,62](#page-17-0)]. The obtained thermal properties are illustrated in Table 5.

As the concentration of carbon fibres in the composite increases, the thermal conductivity correspondingly rises, peaking at a filler loading of 5 wt. %. At this optimum level, the thermal conductivity reaches approximately 1.448 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹. This improvement results from the formation of an interconnected network of carbon fibres that supports efficient phonon transport. However, when the filler loading exceeds 5 wt. %, the trend in thermal conductivity changes. This behaviour arises due to several factors, including structural disruptions in the composite. Specifically, areas where paraffin and filler are well-mixed tend to develop significant cracks, which impede the formation of continuous thermal pathways and diminish the overall conductivity improvement.

The thermal conductivity of paraffin composites was measured using the TEMPOS system, which operates on the transient line heat source principle. Volumetric specific heat (Cv) was calculated using the formula Cv=Cp.ρ, where Cp is specific heat and ρ is density. While Cp can be approximated with minimal impact, density has a greater influence on Cv. This calculation was essential for evaluating the composites' thermal performance.

At lower filler loadings near 5 wt. %, graphene exhibits a tendency to aggregate, forming concentrated clusters within the matrix. While such aggregation can sometimes be a drawback, in this case, it proves advantageous for thermal conductivity enhancement. These clusters establish large, effective thermal conductivity channels that dominate the heat transfer process [\[63](#page-17-0)]. As a result, even though small cracks and voids form during the synthesis of the composite, their negative impact on thermal conductivity is relatively minor compared to the benefits

provided by the aggregated filler. This indicates a complex interplay between filler distribution and matrix interaction, where controlled aggregation can improve composite properties. Wang et al. [[64\]](#page-17-0) studied graphene-based PCM for solar thermal storage and conversions and found graphene distribution and its interaction with the matrix improve its thermal properties via heat exchange networks. Also, Fan et al. [[7](#page-16-0)] reported the ability of graphene nano platelet to improve the networks with the fluids could enhance their capabilities to transfer heat.

Moreover, the inclusion of carbon fibres not only enhances thermal conductivity but also significantly increases the specific volumetric heat capacity of paraffin. This parameter, which measures the material's ability to store thermal energy per unit volume, reaches values as high as 6 MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹ or higher with the addition of carbon fibres. The high aspect ratio of carbon fibres and their superior thermal storage properties enable the composite to retain and manage heat more effectively. Parallelly, Dong et al. [\[65](#page-17-0)] studied carbon fibre filled paraffin composites as phase change materials and found the high aspect ratio of carbon fibre could improve the heat exchange.

The observed reversal in thermal properties beyond 5 wt. % filler loading, especially the drop in thermal conductivity and volumetric heat capacity that cannot be attributed to inconsistencies in sample shape or size, as all samples were prepared using fixed cylindrical molds (10 mm thickness and 50 mm diameter) and trimmed precisely to maintain uniform volume. Instead, the decline is primarily due to filler agglomeration and morphological instability at higher loadings, particularly with carbon fibres, which tend to cluster and disrupt the conductive network established at lower concentrations. This transition behavior likely corresponds to the onset of a percolation threshold, where filler connectivity is no longer linearly proportional to loading due to aggregation and network breakdown. However, a quantitative analysis of the percolation threshold is not explicitly conducted in this study. Future work will aim to incorporate percolation theory and effective medium approximations to better model this critical transition. Moreover, the role of filler-matrix interfacial thermal resistance, which becomes increasingly prominent at higher loadings, will be explored using theoretical models and image-based quantification. This leads to increased interfacial resistance, void formation, and reduced effective thermal pathways. At 14 wt. %, oversaturation causes further network breakdown, decreasing both thermal conductivity and material density, which in turn reduces volumetric heat capacity. While microscopic imaging supported the identification of agglomerates, it lacked the resolution to quantify void fractions or interfacial defects, which are likely contributors to the observed property degradation.

A clear trend is observed where the bulk heat capacity of all carbon- based fillers decreases as their loading in the paraffin PCM increases, as shown in Fig. 6. Unlike thermal conductivity, which depends on the structural organization of materials, bulk heat capacity is an intrinsic property determined by the material's ability to store thermal energy per unit mass. Paraffin PCM inherently possesses a much higher volumetric heat capacity compared to carbon-based fillers. This discrepancy arises because the addition of nanofillers displaces paraffin within the matrix and inhibits its natural thermal expansion, thereby reducing its overall heat absorption capacity [\[66\]](#page-17-0).

The volumetric heat capacity of pure paraffin is notably greater because it can store more heat within a given volume[\[66](#page-17-0)]. However, introducing fillers compromises this capacity, as the filler particles occupy significant space in the matrix. This trade-off highlights a fundamental limitation in composite design: any enhancement in thermal conductivity through filler addition typically results in a proportional decrease in volumetric heat capacity. For instance, the inclusion of graphite not only restricts paraffin's free expansion but also disrupts its molecular arrangement. The bulky structure of graphite particles displaces paraffin molecules, requiring less energy to overcome their bonds during heating. This phenomenon further reduces the heat capacity of the composite and impacts its overall energy storage efficiency [[67\]](#page-17-0).

For composites with graphene as the filler, the thermal conductivity of paraffin improves modestly, reaching values of approximately 0.9 W

The unique properties of graphene allow it to form efficient thermal pathways, yet its effect diminishes at higher loadings due to limitations in filler dispersion and interaction with the matrix [\[23](#page-16-0)]. Moreover, graphene's contribution to the volumetric heat capacity of paraffin is insignificant. Unlike carbon fibres or other fillers, graphene does not considerably enhance the heat absorption potential of the paraffin matrix, suggesting that its primary role is confined to improving heat transfer rather than energy storage as reported by Zhu et al. [\[68](#page-17-0)]

The interrelation between thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, and thermal diffusivity is fundamental in understanding the thermal behavior of composite PCMs. Thermal diffusivity, which represents the rate at which heat spreads through a material, is determined by the balance of these two properties [\[69](#page-17-0)]. This relationship is mathematically expressed in the governing Eq. (4):

*α* = *λ/ρ*⋅Cp (4)

where α is the thermal diffusivity, λ is the thermal conductivity, ρ is the density, and Cp is the specific heat capacity. While the addition of fillers enhances λ, the concurrent decrease in Cp due to filler-induced displacement and matrix alteration highlights the inherent trade-offs in optimizing PCM composites. This equation underscores the need for strategic filler design and loading control to achieve a balance between efficient heat transfer and adequate energy storage in thermal applications [\[70,71\]](#page-17-0).

To better quantify the real-world usefulness of these composites and guide optimization, an energy storage performance metric was introduced by calculating a figure of merit (FOM), defined as shown in Eq. (5).

FOM = *λ* × ΔH (5)

This metric, which incorporates both thermal conductivity (λ) and latent heat of fusion (ΔH), allows for direct comparison of thermal transport and storage capabilities.

The Figure of Merit (FOM) analysis reveals a nuanced balance between thermal conductivity enhancement and volumetric heat capacity reduction as filler content increases. For carbon fibre/paraffin composites, FOM rises sharply from 0.0907 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 1 wt. % to a maximum of 0.2977 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 5 wt. %, representing an increase of over 228 %, indicating enhanced thermal transport relative to energy storage. Beyond 5 wt. %, FOM declines significantly to 0.1152 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 14 wt. %, a decrease of 61 % from the peak, reflecting filler agglomeration and network disruption. Graphene/paraffin composites exhibit consistently higher FOM values, starting at 0.2988 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 1 wt. % and peaking at 0.4868 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 5 wt. %, a 63 % improvement, before gradually decreasing to 0.2800 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 14 wt. %, a 43 % decline from peak. The superior FOM of graphene composites highlights graphene's ability to form effective thermal networks despite its negligible contribution to volumetric heat capacity. The decline in FOM at higher loadings for both composites correlates with observed reductions in volumetric heat capacity, up to approximately 70 % decrease for carbon fibre/paraffin from 6.483 to 1.944 MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹ between 1 and 14 wt. %, underscoring the critical trade-off between filler-induced conductivity gains and the loss of paraffin's intrinsic heat storage. These trends emphasize that an optimal filler concentration around 5 wt. % exists to maximize phase change material thermal performance by balancing conductive pathways and energy storage capacity while excessive loading induces filler agglomeration and void formation, degrading overall function.

For instance, the thermal conductivity of a 5 wt. % carbon fibre/paraffin composite reaches an impressive 1.448 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹, which is approximately 6.23 times higher than that of pure paraffin (0.2323 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹). These findings highlight the superior ability of carbon fibres to form interconnected thermal pathways within the paraffin matrix, thereby facilitating more efficient heat transfer [\[72](#page-17-0)].

While these fillers enhance thermal conductivity, the volumetric heat capacity of paraffin-based PCM decreases as the filler proportion increases. This decline occurs because fillers displace paraffin within the matrix, reducing its inherent ability to store thermal energy. However, despite this decrease, the overall volumetric heat capacity of the composite remains higher than that of pure paraffin. This balance suggests that the addition of fillers not only improves heat transfer properties but also retains some of the energy storage characteristics of the matrix, making it suitable for thermal management applications.

The experimental process, however, encountered several challenges at higher filler loadings. When the filler ratio exceeds 8 wt. %, the composite becomes increasingly inhomogeneous. The excessive number of fillers overwhelms the paraffin matrix, preventing a uniform distribution and leading to localized aggregation. This agglomeration disrupts the structural integrity of the composite, creating regions where the fillers block potential thermal channels [[73\]](#page-17-0). Furthermore, the random orientation of nanofillers in the matrix exacerbates phonon scattering at the boundaries, impeding efficient heat transfer [\[74](#page-17-0)]. As a result, the enhancement of thermal properties becomes marginal beyond 8 wt. % filler loading.

These observations underline the need for optimized filler loading to maximize the thermal performance of paraffin PCM composites. While moderate filler concentrations (e.g., 5 wt. %) effectively enhance thermal conductivity and diffusivity, excessive loadings introduce structural inefficiencies that counteract these benefits. This highlights the importance of achieving a balance between filler distribution and matrix compatibility to fully leverage the thermal advantages of carbon-based fillers in paraffin-based PCMs.

The thermal stability of the samples was evaluated from the onset temperature and the degradation temperature. This thermal stability range defines the operational limit of paraffin-based PCMs and reflects their suitability for moderate-temperature applications.

The weight loss profiles and degradation behavior of paraffin/carbon fibre and paraffin/graphene composite PCMs closely mirror those of pure paraffin. This similarity indicates that the integration of carbonbased fillers does not fundamentally alter the thermal decomposition mechanism of paraffin. Such consistent behavior across all composites underscores the compatibility of the fillers with the paraffin matrix, as well as their ability to preserve the intrinsic phase change characteristics of paraffin according to Wu et al. This integration is crucial for ensuring that the composites retain their thermal energy storage capabilities while benefiting from enhanced thermal conductivity and mechanical stability provided by the fillers.

To further elucidate the effects of fillers on thermal degradation, the nodal temperatures at key weight loss stages and the residual masses at the end of the degradation process are tabulated in [Table 6](#page-14-0). These metrics offer a deeper understanding of the thermal stability and residue formation in paraffin-based PCMs. The residual mass of the composite materials is indicative of the filler content, as carbon-based fillers typically do not fully decompose within the temperature range of the experiment. This residual content reflects the structural contribution of the fillers and their potential role in enhancing the thermal endurance of the composites.

Additionally, the weight loss trend suggests that the fillers form a protective barrier around paraffin chains, partially mitigating the rate of thermal decomposition. This could lead to prolonged thermal stability and delayed degradation onset for composite PCMs compared to pure paraffin [\[77](#page-17-0)]. Such observations highlight the dual benefits of carbon-based fillers in improving both the thermal and structural resilience of paraffin-based PCMs under high-temperature conditions.

As demonstrated in [Table 6](#page-14-0) and Fig. 9, the incorporation of carbonbased fillers, such as carbon fibre and graphene, leads to a noticeable increase in the degradation temperature of pure paraffin by approximately 10–30 ◦C. However, this enhancement in thermal stability is not entirely consistent, as the observed increment varies depending on the composition and distribution of the fillers. This variability highlights the complex interactions between the filler and the paraffin matrix, which may be influenced by factors such as filler dispersion and bonding strength.

Additionally, the final residual mass of the composite PCMs shows a clear dependence on the loading of carbon-based fillers. At a filler loading of 1 wt. %, the residual mass is minimal, at less than 1 %, but it increases significantly to 14.04 % at a loading of 14 wt. %. This trend is attributed to the inherently high thermal stability of carbon-based fillers. Carbon fibres exhibit degradation temperatures of around 700 ◦C, while graphene remains stable up to approximately 1000 ◦C. Consequently, even at the experimental maximum temperature of 500 ◦C, these fillers contribute to a greater residual mass, underscoring their role in enhancing the thermal endurance of the PCM composites. The relatively high rate of weight loss in the specified temperature range is indicative of the thermal decomposition of paraffin chains. However, the retention of carbonaceous residue at higher filler loadings suggests that the fillers act as stabilizing agents, delaying the onset of complete degradation [[79\]](#page-17-0). This stabilization effect can be beneficial for applications requiring thermal resilience, as it extends the effective temperature range of the PCM composites.

Moreover, the lack of pronounced mutual interference between the paraffin matrix and the fillers, as evidenced by the similar overall degradation trends, indicates a good level of compatibility between the two phases. This compatibility ensures that the phase change behavior of paraffin, which is crucial for its thermal energy storage functionality, remains largely intact despite the incorporation of fillers. Such characteristics make carbon fibre and graphene fillers effective additives for improving the thermal properties and stability of paraffin-based PCMs.

Both carbon-based fillers contribute to an improvement in the thermal stability of paraffin wax, albeit to varying degrees. Carbon fibre and graphene act as stabilizing agents that delay the onset of thermal degradation. However, the presence of structural defects in the carbonbased fillers, such as discontinuities in fibres or imperfections in graphene sheets, can present significant challenges. These defects may limit the efficiency of the fillers in uniformly distributing thermal stress and maintaining matrix integrity under elevated temperatures. Graphene exhibits a slightly greater ability to enhance the degradation temperature of paraffin-based composite PCMs compared to carbon fibres. This can be attributed to graphene's highly ordered crystalline structure and its stronger interfacial interactions with the paraffin matrix [[80\]](#page-17-0). Such interactions promote the formation of a robust network that mitigates thermal stress and slows the decomposition of paraffin chains at higher temperatures.

The collective findings emphasize that paraffin-based composite PCMs demonstrate exceptional thermal stability below 180 ◦C, a crucial threshold for many practical applications [\[81](#page-17-0)]. This temperature range is particularly significant for battery thermal management systems, where maintaining stable thermal conditions is essential for ensuring operational safety and efficiency. The ability of carbon-based fillers to sustain the integrity of paraffin wax at elevated temperatures makes them highly suitable for use in thermal energy storage and dissipation systems in battery technologies.

Moreover, the synergistic relationship between the paraffin matrix and the carbon-based fillers provides a dual benefit: while the fillers enhance thermal stability, they also create microstructures that potentially facilitate heat dissipation. However, addressing structural defects in the fillers could further optimize their performance, thereby expanding the applicability of these composites in more demanding thermal management scenarios.

# *3.5. Other thermal properties*

The DSC curves of Paraffin/Carbon Fibre composite PCM, Paraffin/ Graphene composite PCM, and pure paraffin, presented in Figs. 10 and 11, reveal significant insights into the thermal behaviors of these samples. It can be seen that all samples have two obvious exothermic peaks during the solidification process and two endothermic peaks during the melting process. These peaks are mainly distributed within the temperature ranges of 30–40 ◦C and 50–60 ◦C, respectively, in agreement with experimental results of a study by Falahatian et al. [\[82](#page-17-0)]. He synthesized mesoporous silica–graphene composites with different graphene concentrations as support structures, with paraffin serving as the phase change material to develop shape-stabilized phase change composites with high PCM content and enhanced thermal conductivity and experienced endothermic peaks during 50–60 ◦C range.

The peaks at lower temperatures are associated with homogeneous nucleation mechanisms, especially the solid-solid phase transition of paraffin. This is the reorganization of paraffin molecules into more thermodynamically stable phases in the absence of impurities or other external nucleating agents [[83\]](#page-17-0). On the other hand, the peaks at higher temperatures correspond to non-homogeneous nucleation, which is linked to the solid-liquid phase transition of paraffin. The participation of the fillers in these processes is very limited because the characteristic transition temperatures do not practically change.

The melting and freezing temperatures of the composite materials are close to pure paraffin. This indicates that the addition of carbon fibres and graphene does not destroy the intrinsic phase transition characteristics of paraffin. Such consistency in thermal behavior means that the fillers have been incorporated into the paraffin matrix without altering its fundamental thermal behavior. This observation again reflects the good compatibility of the fillers with the paraffin matrix. The fillers seem to be embedded inside the matrix without affecting characteristics such as thermal conductivity while still allowing the paraffin to change phases. Again, this result reflects the structural and thermal stability that carbon fibres and graphene give without disrupting the intrinsic characteristics of paraffin.

These results evidence the opportunities of Paraffin/Carbon Fibre and Paraffin/Graphene composites for thermal energy storage applications, especially in the scenario where the original thermal and phasechange properties of paraffin are to be preserved. Such composites can, to a large extent, accomplish a balanced task between conserving these properties while attaining improved thermal conductivity along with stability, as expected from efficient thermal management systems.

From the area size of the peaks in the DSC images, the phase change temperature and latent heat are determined for both the composite and pure paraffin. In the case of composite phase change materials, when the filler content is below 5 wt. %, the thermal properties are very promising, with increased thermal conductivity and insignificant loss in latent heat and specific heat capacity. While a more dramatic impact of the filler contents, especially beyond 11 wt. %, can be found, changes in the latent heat and specific heat capacity are connected with the variation of filler content. Therefore, higher performance in composite phase change materials could be realized at low concentrations.

Irrespective of filler proportion, the composite phase change materials can still do an excellent job of absorbing and releasing the heat with a view to their feasibility for thermal management applications. Increasing filler proportion therefore resulted in the progressive

reduction of latent heat. The obtained trend could be explained from the fact that latent heat in composite phase change material arises from paraffin content only; hence, higher paraffin fraction would translate into greater latent heat capacity [\[84](#page-17-0)]. When the filler content is beyond the threshold value, the contribution of paraffin to latent heat becomes reduced, hence a reduction in the overall latent heat of the composite.

If multiple cycling tests were conducted on these composite materials, it would likely show that the addition of carbon-based fillers helps maintain the high latent heat of the composite phase change material over time [[85\]](#page-17-0). The presence of these fillers also aids in limiting the degrading of physical properties within the composite, largely due to the higher thermal conductivity and heat capacity stemming from the carbon-based fillers [\[86](#page-17-0)]. The fillers contribute not just to thermal transfer efficiency but also have a role to play in maintaining the effectiveness of a material in storing and releasing energy throughout its life cycle.

The thermal behaviors observed in these composites align with findings from previous studies, including those by Falahatian et al. [\[82](#page-17-0)]. The incorporation of carbon-based fillers, such as carbon fibres and graphene, enhances the thermal stability of the PCMs, making these composites a promising candidate for thermal energy storage applications.

## **4. Conclusions**

Milled carbon fibres and graphene were incorporated for the first time into a paraffin matrix to form morphologically stable composite PCMs. These composites retained phase change behavior comparable to pure paraffin, while exhibiting significantly enhanced thermal properties. Notably, the addition of 5 wt. % carbon fibre increased the thermal conductivity to 1.448 W *m*<sup>−</sup> 1 K<sup>−</sup> <sup>1</sup> , showing approximately 6.23 times that of pure paraffin. Graphene reinforcement led to an increase in melting point by 10–30 ◦C, improving thermal stability and heat management potential.

However, when the total filler content exceeds 8 wt. %, inhomogeneity and agglomeration occur, reducing performance. Thus, careful control of filler loading and filler size should be essential and studied further. As per the present results, a maximum loading of 5 wt. % is recommended for better performance. Additionally, improving the mixing method (high-shear or ultrasonication instead of electromagnetic stirring) may yield better homogeneity at higher loadings. Therefore, carbon fibre and graphene enhanced thermal conductivity, melting temperature, and thermal stability in paraffin-based PCMs, making them promising candidates for thermal management applications, especially in battery systems, while maintaining structural stability and latent heat performance under cyclic operation.
