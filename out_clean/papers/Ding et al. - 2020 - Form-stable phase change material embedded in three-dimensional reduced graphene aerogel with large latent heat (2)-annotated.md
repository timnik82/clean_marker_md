# Form-stable phase change material embedded in three-dimensional reduced graphene aerogel with large latent heat for thermal energy management

#### ABSTRACT

Form-stable phase change material (PCM) composites is fabricated by vacuum-assisted melting infiltration (PEG/rGO-MI) technique, while the sample prepared by hydrothermal reduction method (PEG/rGO-HT) is also provided as control. PEG-6000 and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) are used as PCM and the porous supporting material, respectively. PEG molecules are fully filled in the connecting pore matrix of rGO aerogel with disordered arrangements for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, while distributed in the lamellar gaps of rGO nanoflakes with a regular orientation due to hydrogen bonding interactions for the PEG/rGO-MI sample. The C/O atomic ratios increase from 2.03% to 3.12% and 2.3%, respectively, for the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples, indicating an increased sp2 -hydridized C and simultaneously a decreased fraction of oxygen-containing functional groups. The Liquid leakages indicate that they both possess shape-stabilized property. The PCM mass percentage is 85.6% for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, while up to 96.6% for the PEG/rGO-MI sample. The melting latent heat of the PEG/rGO-HT sample is 139.4 J/g, whereas it is 205.2 J/g for the PEG/rGO-MI sample, much larger than that of most published works. The PEG/rGO-MI sample also exhibits excellent short-term thermal insulation property, which has promising application in complex environment for thermal energy management.

# **1. Introduction**

Thermal protection issue is of crucial importance in numerous fields, such as high-speed space-vehicles, protective garments in hightemperature and harsh environment, as well as in industrial filed, such as high-temperature furnaces, steam pipelines and other equipment. As for the firemen and spacemen, they may suffer heat strain when encountering the high temperature environments with a time span of several minutes or few hours, therefore, except for the ceramic-based heat protection material, the high efficient short-term thermal insulation materials inside are urgently demanded. In addition, in the field of new energy automobile, the explosion of battery pack could cause serious risk, therefore, the high-efficient short-term thermal insulation for thermal management are also needed for covering on the surface of battery pack to increase the escape time of people inside [\[1\].](#page-9-0) There are many kinds of thermal insulation materials that have been studied during the past years, such as fibrous porous materials, porous particles-based materials, polymer-based porous materials and nanoporous materials [\[2–6\],](#page-9-1) among which aerogel based nanoporous materials with excellent thermal insulation property have been regarded as one of the most promising thermal insulation materials [\[6–8\].](#page-9-2) Aerogels are porous materials composed of nanoparticles or polymers which have extremely low thermal conductivity, low bulk density, high porosity and specific surface area, therefore, they have been widely used in applications such as thermal insulation materials, catalyst barriers, high-efficient adsorbents and chemical sensors [\[9–13\]](#page-9-3). Within many kinds of aerogel materials, silica aerogels have caused extensive interests and have been mostly studied due to their comprehensive properties, however, their intrinsic fragility and weak skeleton has limited their application in many fields. Therefore, many researchers have devoted to the study of aerogel based thermal insulations with more robust of flexible characteristics [\[14,15\]](#page-10-0). In recent years, a novel kind of aerogel, graphenebased aerogels have caused much attention due to their excellent mechanical property and thermal insulation property, and they have been considered as a novel kind of promising thermal insulation material. Zu et al. [\[16\]](#page-10-1) prepared a novel superelastic multifunctional aminosilanecrosslinked graphene aerogel (AC-rGO) via a facile and scalable strategy. The results showed that the AC-rGO aerogel possessed a low

bulk density (1–5 mg/cm3 ), high specific surface area (up to 472 m<sup>2</sup> /g) and an extremely low thermal conductivity of 19 mW/(m·K), which is even lower than the static air. Yue et al. [\[17,18\]](#page-10-2) also developed a novel nitrogen-doped graphene aerogel, which exhibited extremely low gaseous thermal conductivity of 12.58 mW/(m·K) with a low bulk density of 32 mg/cm<sup>3</sup> . Lei et al. [\[19\]](#page-10-3) also proposed that the thermal insulation property of silica aerogel can be further improved via introduce of graphene oxide (GO) due to the interfacial interaction between GO nanosheets and silica matrix. However, the individual use of graphenebased aerogel is not sufficient for the short-term thermal insulations applications. Therefore, it is urgently needed for researchers to find a better way to further improve the thermal insulation property of graphene-based aerogels. In recent years, some researchers have showed that the combination of phase change materials (PCMs) and porous aerogel matrix might be an efficient way for further increasing the thermal insulation property of aerogel based materials [\[20–22\]](#page-10-4).

PCMs are special substances with high capacity and high energy storage density, and their performance provides steady temperature via adsorbing/releasing latent heat during phase change process, therefore, they have wide applications in solar energy storage and conversion, industrial waste heat recovery and utilization, photo-thermal energy conversion, solar domestic water system, and natural ventilation [\[23–26\]](#page-10-5). According to the types of phase change processes, PCMs can be divided into three types: solid-solid PCMs, solid-liquid PCMs, and liquid-gas PCMs [\[27\].](#page-10-6) Among them, the solid-liquid PCMs are mainly studied due to their unique advantages such as high latent heat, high heat capacity and easy manipulation [\[28–30\]](#page-10-7). The solid-liquid PCMs can be further classified as organic PCMs and inorganic PCMs, and organic PCMs exhibit more advantages such as low corrosion, high volumetric latent heat storage capacity, chemical stability and moderate phase change temperatures and enthalpies, in comparison with inorganic PCMs [\[27\].](#page-10-6) Among the organic PCMs, PEG is a kind of excellent polymeric PCMs due to its high latent heat, stable chemical and thermal stability, and non-toxicity. In addition, PEG has suitable phase change temperature ranging from 3.2 °C to 68.7 °C [\[31\],](#page-10-8) which is dependent on its molecular weight in the range of 100–20,000, therefore, different kinds of PEG can be applied in specific situations. However, one issue for PEG usage is its liquid leakage during melting process, therefore, many researchers have devoted their efforts to solve this problem. Many porous matrix such as metal foams, porous ceramics, carbon nanotubes, expanded graphite, mesoporous silica, and aerogels have been used for shape-stabilization of PEG based PCMs [\[28\]](#page-10-7). In addition to the usage of pore matrix, Liu et al. [\[32\]](#page-10-9) also developed the microencapsulated PCM using melamine as the shell and n-dodecanol as the core for preventing the liquid leakage.

Since rGO aerogel is a typical light-weight material with large porosity and flexible skeleton, therefore, it may be an ideal porous matrix for PEG molecules. Moreover, when PEG is incorporated in rGO aerogel, the thermal insulation property of rGO aerogel may be further enhanced caused via the thermal energy management due to the latent heat of PEG during phase change process. In the past few years, many researches have devoted to the study of PEG/GA based composites for thermal energy management. Tang et al. [\[33\]](#page-10-10) proposed a PEG/graphene oxide aerogel shape-stabilized composite for photo-to-thermal energy conversion and storage by adjusting the oxidation degree of graphene oxide. The results showed that with higher oxidation levels, the PCM composite showed excellent shape-stability, thermal repeatability and the latent heat was up to 199.6 J/g. Scaffaro et al. [\[34\]](#page-10-11) developed a PEGylated-graphene aerogel via the self-assembly technique. The synthetic route involved two steps including coupling of GO with an amino-terminated polyethylene glycol sample by carbodiimide and the conversion of hydrogel to aerogel using the freezing drying technique. The 3D composite displayed an ultra-light and highly porous network, and the composite also possessed high mechanical properties as well as a good biocompatibility. Liao et al. [\[35\]](#page-10-12) prepared a lightweight, mechanically strong, high-quality graphene aerogel within the interconnected pores of melamine foam, followed by further carbonization. The carbonized graphene/melamine foam was used to encapsulate PEG via the impregnation technique, and the resulting PCM composite obtained a much higher thermal conductivity and outstanding thermal stability. In addition, many other researchers [\[36–38\]](#page-10-13) have also reported the fabrications of PEG/GA shape-stabilized PCM composites, however, they often paid attention to two issues including increasing the thermal conductivity and preventing the liquid leakage of PCM, and they always concentrate on photo/electro-to-thermal energy conversions. However, the preparation routes are somewhat complicated and to the best of our knowledge, the PCM composites using as the short-term thermal insulation via thermal management of PCM composite is seldom reported. Herein, in this study, we have involved 3D reduced graphene aerogel as the interconnected pore matrix for embedding the PEG PCM molecules, the thermal conductivity is not improved, while the latent heat and thermal stability are greatly enhanced, which therefore improves the short-term thermal insulation property.

Herein, we have prepared the novel PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite via the vacuum-assisted melt infiltration technique, and the PEG/rGO-HT sample using the hydrothermal reduction method is also provided as control. During the fabrication of hydrothermal reduction method, PEG molecules are mixed with GO solution with the assistance of surfactant, followed by the hydrothermal reduction and freezing drying processes. In the melt infiltration method, ascorbic acid is adopted as the reduction agent for the formation of rGO hydrogel. The rGO aerogel is thus obtained after further freezing drying process, and then the PEG/rGO-MI sample is obtained via the vacuum-assisted melt infiltration. The effects of PEG amount addition, reaction time and dwelling time on the physicochemical properties of the PEG/rGO-HT during the hydrothermal reduction process are well investigated. The morphologies, microstructures, chemical structures, and thermal-physical property are compared for these two samples. The PEG/rGO-MI sample also exhibits excellent shape-stabilization property, large latent heat and superior thermal energy management efficiency, which has great potential in complex environment for thermal energy management.

# **2. Experimental procedures**

# *2.1. Synthesis of the GO solution*

GO was prepared by the improved Hummer's method [\[39\].](#page-10-14) In a typical synthesis, 100 g of powdered flake graphite and 50 g of sodium nitrate were added into 2300 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid in a 0 °C ice-bath. While maintaining vigorous stirring, 300 g of potassium permanganate was added into the solution with temperature below 20 °C. The ice-bath was then removed and the temperature of suspension was increased to 35 °C and maintained at that temperature for 30 min, and the solution became brownish grey. At the end of 30 min, 4600 mL of DI water was slowly added into the solution and the temperature was increased to 98 °C, and it was maintained for another 15 min. After that, the solution was further diluted by 14,000 mL warm DI water and treated by 30% H2O2 solution to reduce the residual permanganate and manganite dioxide to colorless soluble manganese sulfate. The GO powders were therefore obtained after filtration, centrifugal washing and dehydration at 40 °C overnight in vacuum. The as-prepared GO powders were dispersed in water to form the GO solution with GO concentration of 2 mg/mL, and the solution was used for further experiment.

## *2.2. Synthesis of the PEG/rGO-HT sample*

20 mL GO solution (2 mg/mL) was added into 20 mL DI water in a beaker, and it was stirred for 30 min and ultrasonicated for another 30 min for better disperse of GO powders in the solution. Different amounts of PEG6000 (0 mg, 500 mg, 1000 mg, 2000 mg, and 3000 mg) were then added into the solution with the assistance of 10 mg cetyltrimethylammoniumchloride (CTAC), which can be used as surfactant for better combination of PEG and GO. The solution was further stirred for 30 min, followed by pouring into the Teflon-lined reactor and was put into oven for hydrothermal reduction process ([Fig. 1(](#page-2-0)c)). The temperatures were varied with 120 °C, 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C, and 200 °C, with the dwelling time of the peak temperatures set at 6 h, 12 h and 18 h, respectively. After the hydrothermal reduction process, the PEG/rGO-HT hydrogels were then soaked in DI water for 48 h, during which the water was exchanged for 8 times. After that, the hydrogels were frozen at −50 °C for 5 h, and then they were put into the freezing drying machine, the PEG/rGO-HT PCM composite can be therefore obtained after drying for 48 h. The typical synthesis procedures can be seen in [Fig. 1(](#page-2-0)a).

#### *2.3. Synthesis of the PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite*

20 mL GO solution (2 mg/mL) was added into 20 mL DI water in a glass bottle with lid, and then 160 mg ascorbic acid as the reducing agent was introduced into the solution, followed by stirring for 10 min. The bottle was then put into the oven with temperature at 95 °C, followed by maintaining at that temperature for 6 h, therefore, the 3D rGO hydrogel was formed via chemical reduction process and self-assembly technique ([Fig. 1(](#page-2-0)c)). The pristine rGO aerogel can be therefore prepared after water washing and freezing drying processes. The rGO aerogel was then put into a beaker with excess amount of PEG6000, and it was put into the oven for further vacuum-assisted melt infiltration process. The infiltration temperature was 90 °C, with the dwelling time of 60 min. Liquid PEG6000 was then spontaneously adsorbed into the rGO aerogel pore matrix due to the capillary force. The PEG/rGO-MI sample was taken out and the excessive PEG was removed until the weight loss keeping constant. The preparation route can be seen in [Fig. 1(](#page-2-0)b).

# *2.4. Characterizations*

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were tested by the X'Pert PRO MPD, a diffractometer with CuKα1 radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The Xray tube was operated at 35 kV and 30 mA. Thermal gravimetric (TG) and differential scanning calorimetry analysis (DSC) were carried out by NETZSCH STA449C Thermogravimetric analyzer under N2 atmosphere with a flowing rate of 30 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 °C/ min. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and EDS images ware conducted using a Nova NanoSEM 450 field emission scanning electron microscope. The densities of the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples are estimated and calculated with a cylindrical shape by measuring mass, diameter and height. Each measurement was carried out for three times. The room temperature thermal conductivities were studied by the measuring apparatus (TPS2500, Hot Disk, Sweden) using a transient plane heat source method. The short-term thermal insulation property was performed by an infrared imaging device, which detected the transient temperature change on the cold face under a constant temperature of the hot plate. The XPS spectra of the GO, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples were carried out on the 250XI X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy instrument. The FTIR spectra of the samples were tested by the Nicolet IS 10 FT-IR using a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans. The Raman spectra of were demonstrated by a Raman spectrometer (LabRam HR Evolution, USA) with the excitation wavelength of 532 nm.

## **3. Results and discussion**

[Fig. 2(](#page-3-0)a–c) presents the photographs of the PEG/rGO-HT samples with different PEG addition amounts, reactions temperatures and dwelling time. The as-prepared PEG/rGO-HT samples all exhibit black color with PEG embedded in the rGO aerogel matrix. As seen in [Fig. 2(](#page-3-0)a), the PEG amounts correspond to 0 mg, 500 mg, 1000 mg, 2000 mg, and 3000 mg, respectively, from the left-most to the right- most. It is observed that when no PEG is involved in the hydrothermal reduction process, the as-prepared rGO aerogel shows large shrinkage during the hydrothermal reduction process. With the increase of PEG amount from 0 mg to 3000 mg, the shapes of the as-prepared PEG/rGO-HT samples become larger, indicating that more PEG molecules are imbedded into the rGO aerogel matrix. The latent heat of the PEG/rGO-HT sample will increase with more PEG embedded into the rGO matrix. When the PEG amounts involved are 500 mg, 1000 mg, and 2000 mg, the PEG/rGO-HT samples exhibit smooth morphologies. It is found that when the PEG amount increases to 3000 mg, some amounts of white PEG are separated out from the rGO matrix, causing the sample cracked. Therefore, the optimal PEG amount is determined at 2000 mg. As shown in [Fig. 2](#page-3-0)(b), the reaction temperatures are 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C and 200 °C from left to right. It is observed that when the hydrothermal reaction temperatures are at 140 °C and 160 °C, the hydrothermal reduction processes are not totally completed, therefore, the shrinkages of the PEG/rGO-HT samples are large with wrinkle surface. In fact, we have also synthesized the sample with reaction temperature of 120 °C, and the result shows that even no 3D shape-stabilized PEG/ rGO-HT sample is formed. The densities of the PEG/rGO-HT samples with hydrothermal reduction temperatures at 140 °C, 160 °C, 180 °C and 200 °C are 0.092 g/cm3 , 0.10 g/cm<sup>3</sup> , 0.338 g/cm<sup>3</sup> and 0.009 g/ cm3 , respectively. The bulk densities increase with the increase of hydrothermal temperatures, however, with a further increase of the reaction temperature of 200 °C, it is found that the bulk density sharply decreases to 0.009 g/cm<sup>3</sup> , which is mainly due to the decomposition of PEG molecules under such a high hydrothermal environment, leaving the rGO pore matrix alone. Therefore, the optimal reduction temperature can be 180 °C. It is rational to further investigate the effects of dwelling time on the morphologies of the as-prepared samples ([Fig. 2](#page-3-0)(c)). The reaction dwelling time is varied with 6 h, 12 h and 18 h from left to right. The bulk densities of the three samples are 0.250 g/ cm3 , 0.338 g/cm<sup>3</sup> and 0.255 g/cm<sup>3</sup> , respectively. It indicates that 6 h of the reaction time is not sufficient for this hydrothermal reduction process, while some parts of PEG molecules have been decomposed with dwelling time of 18 h. The optimal reaction dwelling time is therefore determined at 12 h based on the morphology and bulk density of the samples. In conclusion, the optimal parameters for the hydrothermal reduction method are PEG addition amount of 2000 mg, reaction temperature of 180 °C and dwelling time at 12 h. [Fig. 2](#page-3-0)(d) shows the typical rGO aerogel prepared by the chemical reduction method, as well as the derived PEG/rGO-MI sample. It is well acknowledged that the melting PEG molecules can be adsorbed into the rGO aerogel matrix via capillary force [\[40,41\]](#page-10-15). There is a small volume shrinkage after PEG melting infiltration, however, the mass weight gain can be up to 952%, compared with the original rGO aerogel. The PEG/ rGO-MI sample possesses a much larger mechanical strength after combination with PEG PCM, with bulk density at 0.45 g/cm3 , which is much higher than that of the PEG/rGO-HT sample.

[Fig. 3](#page-3-1) exhibits the XRD patterns of the pristine PEG, rGO, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples. As seen in the figure, for the pristine rGO, no evident diffraction peaks are observed, whereas there is a weak and wide peak at around 24°, which is a typical characteristic of amorphous reduced graphene oxide. In addition, two sharp peaks at 19.1° and 23.2°, which corresponds to the PEG lattice plane of (1 2 0) and (1 3 2), respectively, appear in three samples including the pristine PEG, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples [\[42\]](#page-10-16), implying the well crystallization of PEG. It is noted that the peak corresponding to rGO for the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples cannot be clearly figured out due to the weak intensity of rGO diffraction peak, which has been reported in other published work [\[42\].](#page-10-16) No other new peaks except for the peaks of PEG are observed after combination with rGO for both the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples. Therefore, it can be suggested that after combination with rGO via the chemical reduction and hydrothermal reduction processes, the rGO and PEG molecules are well physically combined. Moreover, it can be mentioned that the intensity of the diffraction peaks at around 19.1° and 26.4° of PEG/rGO-MI sample is even higher that of the pristine PEG, which means that with the addition of rGO via the chemical reduction/vacuum-assisted melt infiltration technique, the degree of PEG segments crystallization have been further enhanced after interaction with rGO nanoflakes, which can be responsible for the high latent heat afterwards.

[Fig. 4](#page-4-0) shows the SEM images of the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI sample under different magnification times. It is mentioned that rGO aerogels both possess nanosheets structures with rippled rough morphologies due to their ultrathin features, and the overlapping of the flexible graphene nanosheets results in the pore structures within both these two samples. The rGO aerogel matrix acts as the supporting, which can encapsulate PEG effectively within its porous structures. It is worth mentioned that for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, we can clearly see the PEG molecules are fully filled in the open and connecting pore matrix of rGO with disordered arrangements, which is caused by the fact that during the hydrothermal reduction process, the PEG molecules are interpenetrating with rGO nanoflakes under the assistance of CTAC surfactant, therefore, the contact of GO and PEG are more compact and the crystallization of PEG can be partly destroyed due to the insertion of rGO segment between PEG molecules. However, for the PEG/rGO-MI sample, the PEG molecules are adsorbed into the lamellar gaps of rGO nanosheets, forming a much more regular structure of the PCM composite. It is also found that the surface of PEG/rGO-MI sample turns smooth, which is attributed to the interface of PEG and the supporting material due to surface tension and hydrogen bonding interactions [\[43\]](#page-10-17). The corresponding SEM mapping images of these two samples are shown in [Fig. 5](#page-4-1), and the EDS spectra is also provided in Fig. S1. It is noted from Fig. S1, only C and O elements originating from PEG and rGO are detected by the EDS measurement, and the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples have comparative C atomic percentages of 69.8% and 63.2%, respectively, with the O atomic percentages of 30.2% and 36.8%. It is observed from [Fig. 5](#page-4-1) that the C and O elements are homogeneously distributed in the rGO aerogel pore matrix, further verifies the successful combination of PEG and rGO for these two techniques.

To further understand the reduction process and interactions between rGO nanoflakes and PEG molecules, the XPS spectroscopy test is carried out [(Fig. 6](#page-5-0), Fig. S2 and [Table 1)](#page-5-1). [Fig. 6](#page-5-0)(a) shows that the survey spectra of three kinds of materials exhibit only C and O elements. The C/O atomic ratio represents the degree of reduction, which shows a slight increase from 2.03% to 3.12% and 2.3%, respectively, for the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples, indicating an increased sp2 hydridized C and simultaneously a decreased fraction of oxygen-containing functional groups [\[44\].](#page-10-18) The increase is not evident, which can be attributed to the involvement of PEG molecules, which have high oxygen amount within their molecular segments. The C1s spectra can be divided into four peaks: 284.5 eV, 285.4 eV, 286.9 eV and 288.2 eV, which correspond to the CeC, CeOH, C(epoxy/alkoxy), and C]O bonds, respectively [\[45\]](#page-10-19). It is noted that for the PEG/rGO-MI sample in the [Fig. 6(](#page-5-0)d), in comparison with pristine GO, the amount of C]O groups is significantly decreased, and the amount of oxygen-containing CeOH groups has also decreased from 29.5% to 18.3%, while the amount of C(epoxy/alkoxy) groups increase from 38.7% to 73.6%, indicating the success of reduction of oxygen-containing species. However, it can be observed for the PEG/rGO-HT sample in [Fig. 6(](#page-5-0)c), the amount of CeOH and C]O groups has increased, which is attributed to the intermolecular between oxygen-containing PEG segment and rGO nanoflakes under high temperature reaction process. As for the O1s spectra, it is composed of four species: 530.7 eV, 531.5 eV, 532.6 eV and 533.5 eV, which correspond to C]O(conj), C]O, CeOeC, CeO groups, respectively [\[46\].](#page-10-20) The results show the similar trend with that of the C1s spectra, and for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, the oxygen-containing groups increase while for the PEG/rGO-MI sample, the reduction process has been well completed. It is worth noting that that C] Oconj specie resulting from the C]O bond and C]C conjugate would appear after reduction process, which is consistent with that of the previously published work [\[46,47\].](#page-10-20)

[Fig. 7](#page-6-0) presents the FTIR spectra of pristine PEG, rGO, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples. As for the pristine PEG, the stretching vibrations of eCH2e bonds could be found at 2890 cm−1 and 958 cm−1. The bands at 3447 cm−1, 1635 cm−1 and 1115 cm−1 can be ascribed to the stretching vibration of OeH, C]O and CeO bonds, respectively, while the band at 1468 cm−1 and 1348 cm−1 are from the deformation vibration of CeH bonds [\[27\].](#page-10-6) The OeH bending vibrations appear at 1276 cm−1 and 1242 cm−1. In the spectra of rGO, the band at around 3459 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of the OeH bond. The band at 1646 cm−1 can be ascribed to the stretching vibration of C]C bond, and the band at 1383 cm−1 originates from the bending vibration of OeH bond [\[48\]](#page-10-21). The band at around 2914 cm−1 comes from the CeH bond, which is located at the edge of the rGO. In the spectrum of the PEG/rGO-MI sample, we see that this spectra concludes almost all the characteristic bands of rGO and PEG, demonstrating that PEG molecules have been adsorbed into the rGO porous architecture and there is no chemical reaction between rGO and PEG during the vacuum-assisted melt infiltration process, similar to that of published work [\[49\]](#page-10-22). Based on the FTIR results, it can be deduced that there is good physicochemical compatibility among the components of the PEG/rGO-MI sample. In addition, it is worth noting that the hydroxyl band of the PEG/rGO-MI sample exhibits a shift to a lower wavelength, compared with the pristine PEG, which further verifies the existence of hydrogen bonding between rGO and PEG molecules[\[30\]](#page-10-23). It is found that some bands, such as those locate at around 1965 cm−1 and 2247 cm−1, disappear after hydrothermal reduction process for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, as compared with the pristine PEG. This may be attributed to the partly destruction of PEG molecule during the hydrothermal process due to the interactions between eOH and carboxylic groups of rGO and PEG, therefore, the degree of crystallization of PEG molecules can be decreased, which keeps consistent with the XRD results.

[Fig. 8](#page-6-1) displays the Raman spectra of the rGO-HT, rGO-MI, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples, where rGO/HT and rGO/MI refer to the pristine rGO aerogel using the hydrothermal reduction and chemical reduction techniques, respectively. These two samples are fabricated for investigating the influence of PEG on the rGO aerogel during these two reaction processes. All of these four spectra exhibit D (1340 cm−1) and G (1595 cm−1) bands, which are associated with the sp2 carbon atoms in the hexagonal carbon framework and sp3 -hybrized carbon atoms at the edge or defects on the grapheme plane, respectively [\[40,43,50\].](#page-10-15)The intensity ratio of D and G bands (ID/IG) can be considered as the amount of defects and disorder in carbon materials. The ID/IG values of the rGO-HT, rGO-MI, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples are calculated as 1.07, 1.08, 0.92, and 1.28, respectively. The evident increase of ID/IG value for PEG/rGO-MI is caused by the fact that more PEG molecules are grafted on the pore matrix of rGO aerogel. In addition, it can be mentioned that after the hydrothermal reduction process, the ID/IG value for PEG/rGO-HT decreases from 1.07 to 0.92, which shows that the addition of PEG molecules may promote the formation of graphitization of rGO, similar with that the published work by Chen et al. [\[51\]](#page-10-24).

[Fig. 9](#page-7-0) presents the shape-stabilized properties of the PEG/rGO-MI sample using an oven as heat resource. As shown in the figure, pristine PEG and PEG/rGO-MI sample are both placed in a glass-surface vessel and heated in an oven with temperature of 80 °C for 40 min. With heat adsorbed, pristine PEG gradually melts with time collapses, and PEG molecules are completely melted after 40 min, whereas the PEG/rGO-MI sample remains solid. The surface of the PEG/rGO-MI sample appears moist, but no liquid leakage is observed during the whole process. The lipophilic property and capillary forces and hydrogen bonding between rGO and PEG render the PEG/rGO-MI sample excellent shapestabilization property, which is extremely crucial for its usage during applications. We have also carried out the experiment on the PEG/rGO-HT sample, which can be seen in Fig. S3. This sample also exhibits good form-stable property. The shape-stabilization test indicates that both these two samples exhibit excellent property for preventing the liquid leakage under elevated temperatures.

In order to investigate the thermal stability and PCM mass fractions within the composite, the TGA and DSC curves of PEG, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples are present in [Fig. 10.](#page-7-1) It is found in [Fig. 10(](#page-7-1)a) that there is almost no weight loss before 360 °C for the pristine PEG. It is mentioned that a dramatic weight loss is observed in the temperature range of 360 °C to 432 °C, along with an evident endothermic peak in the DSC curve. This process corresponds to the decomposition of PEG [\[42\]](#page-10-16), and residual carbon char of about 0.9% after 600 °C is observed. The DTG curve in Fig. S4 evidently shows this weight loss rate during the heating process for all the three samples. As for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, there is a slight weight loss at around 296 °C, which may be caused by the break of the C]O bonds, as revealed by the XPS results. The ultimate weight maintains at around 15.3% of its original weight, which means that the PCM mass percentages can be calculated as 85.6%. As for the PEG/rGO-MI sample, the onset temperature for

weight loss is at around 344 °C, 16 °C earlier than pristine PEG, which can be caused by the interior CeOeC and hydrogen bonds. It is worth noting that the ultimate weight for the PEG/rGO-MI sample is at around 4.3%, which indicates that the PCM mass fraction in the sample can be up to 96.6%, beneficial for the large latent heat of the PCM composite.

The latent heat and phase-transition temperature of the PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples under flowing nitrogen are also characterized by DSC technique, which can be seen in [Fig. 11](#page-8-0) and [Table. 2](#page-8-1). In comparison with the pristine PEG sample in Fig. S5, the two PCM composites possess similar characteristic peaks, suggesting that rGO has slight effect on the phase change behavior of PEG molecules. As for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, the melting temperature and the melting latent heat are 33.73 °C and 139.4 J/g, and the crystallization temperature and crystallization latent heat are 61.12 °C and 175.7 J/g, respectively. It is found that the melting latent heat is 89.3% of that of the pristine PEG, which keeps consistent with TG result (85.6%) in [Fig. 10](#page-7-1)(a). However, after 50 thermal cycling test, the melting temperature and melting latent heat keep almost changed, while the crystallization temperature and crystallization latent heat decreases to 59.64 °C and 149 J/g, which shows that the thermal stability is not excellent for PEG/rGO-HT sample. Intriguingly, as for the PEG/rGO-MI sample, the melting and freezing latent heat are 205.2 J/g and 223.4 J/g, which are even higher than those of the pristine PEG (196.8 J/g and 186.5 J/g, respectively). The probable interpretation is that rGO nanoflakes restrict the movement of PEG molecules, which demands more energy to overcome the intermolecular forces of PEG molecules. In addition, the XRD patterns in [Fig. 3](#page-3-1) show that the PEG molecules are well confined by amorphous rGO nanoflakes with better degree of crystallization, which also promotes the improvement of latent heat of the PEG/rGO-MI sample. Moreover, the hydrogen bond revealed by the FTIR spectra in the PEG/rGO-MI sample may also promote the increase of its large latent heat. This phenomenon has also been reported by Niu et al. [\[52\]](#page-10-25). It is worth mentioned that the latent heat almost keeps unchanged after 50 thermal cycles, which suggests that the PEG/rGO-MI sample has extremely excellent thermal stability during thermal cycling test. We have also listed a table [(Table 3)](#page-9-4) rendering some recently published literatures reporting typical organic PCMs supported with porous carbon-based materials, the as-prepared PEG/rGO-MI sample is more superior with higher PCM loading percentage and higher latent heat, which is beneficial for thermal energy management.

We have focused on further improving the short-term thermal insulation property of the rGO aerogel, and PEG can be a promising material due to its large latent heat and excellent thermal stability. The short-term thermal insulation property can be therefore improved due to the large latent heat during the solid-liquid process. As compared with the pristine rGO aerogel, the thermal conductivity is greatly improved due to the incorporation of PEG molecules. Therefore, thermal insulation property here means a comprehensive result of the composite material. We do not hope that the PCM composite has an extremely large thermal conductivity because it would decrease the thermal insulation property via solid phase heat conduction, and we also don't want it to be rather low since it is not beneficial for the occurrence of phase change process of PEG. It is found in [Fig. 12](#page-9-5) that the thermal conductivities for the pristine PEG, PEG/rGO-HT and PEG/rGO-MI samples are 0.16, 0.10, and 0.15 W/(m·K), respectively. In addition, we have also tested the thermal conductivity of the rGO aerogel, which is at around 0.032 W/(m·K). Therefore, it can be concluded that the thermal conductivity of the PCM composites are mainly determined by PEG. The thermal conductivity of 0.15 W/(m·K) corresponding to the PEG/rGO-MI sample is suitable to balance between these two factors. We have also recorded the back temperature changes of the pristine rGO aerogel and PEG/rGO-MI sample during the heating process using the infrared thermal imaging test. The temperature rise curves are exhibited in [Fig. 13](#page-9-6) and Fig. S6. The temperature of the hot surface is fixed at 80 °C, the temperature of rGO aerogel surface gradually increases, and after 6 min, the surface temperature has come to 63.5 °C, while the PEG/rGO-MI sample undergoes a solid-liquid melting process, therefore the temperature maintains at around 40 °C for around 2 min and the then increases to 55.2 °C in 6 min. The heat transfer is blocked by the PEG/rGO-MI sample via the PEG phase change process, therefore, the PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite has great potential in complex environment for thermal energy management.

# **4. Conclusions**

The PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite is fabricated via the chemical reduction/vacuum impregnation technique, and is firstly designed for thermal energy management in the field of short-term thermal insulation. The microstructures, morphologies and the thermophysical properties of the PEG/rGO-MI sample are compared with those of the PEG/ rGO-HT sample. The rGO aerogel matrix and PEG are well physically combined for both two PCM composites, and the addition of rGO nanoflakes promote the degree of PEG segments crystallization of the PEG/rGO-MI sample. PEG molecules are fully filled in the open and connecting pore matrix of rGO with disordered arrangements for the PEG/rGO-HT sample, while the PEG molecules are distributed in the lamellar gaps of rGO nanoflakes with a regular orientation due to surface tension and hydrogen bonding interactions for the PEG/rGO-MI sample. The PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite shows excellent shape-stabilized property with no liquid leakage under elevated temperatures. The PCM mass percentage is determined at 96.6%, much higher than that of the PEG/rGO-HT control sample. The melting latent heat of the PEG/rGO-HT sample is 139.4 J/g, while it is 205.2 J/g for the PEG/ rGO-MI sample, which is even larger that of the pristine PEG due to the restriction of rGO nanoflakes, higher degree of crystallization of PEG molecules and hydrogen bond inside the composite. The PEG/rGO-MI PCM composite can be potentially applied in thermal storage complex environment for thermal energy management and conversion systems.
